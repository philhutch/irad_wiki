    Clans of the Autonomous Tribal Regions

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The clans of the Autonomous Tribal Regions (Iradi: uruktar) are the 24 patrilineal descent groups (uruk) of the rural Iradis that hold land and govern themselves in the Autonomous Tribal Regions (ATR) of Irad. They coordinate through the Iradi Freedom Society (IAC), whose principal spokesman is the Tanıp chieftain Bektur Zhumabek uulu. Outside the ATR the clans are often described as a single bloc. Researchers and United Nations staff describe a looser landscape of regional groupings, allied and rival lineages, and recurring disputes over pasture, water and trade.[1][2]

    The clans vary greatly in size, from a few hundred households to clans that field more than 1,500 El-Choro riders. Precedence among them follows the oral genealogies (sanjyra), which do not always match present-day size or influence.[1]

    Groupings

    Iradi commentators speak loosely of a "north wing" (tündük kanat) and a "south wing" (tüshtük kanat). The terms have no formal standing, and clans move between them on particular issues.[2]

    Mountain clans of the Talas Alatau

    The core of the north wing is a group of mountain clans in the Talas Alatau, whose summer pastures meet on the Aksay Plateau. It includes the Tanıp, the Karakoyun of the Kara-Koyun Valley and the Akjele. These clans formed the backbone of the Popular Front of Irad in 2007 and most strongly support the moratorium on open-pit mining.

    Steppe clans

    The clans of the Northern Steppe, led by the Boztorgoy, winter in the Moiynkum sands and on the desert margin. The Boztorgoy depend more on trade across the Qara-Chik Demarcation Line than any other clan. Outsiders usually count the three clans of the Karatau with them, but the Karatau clans keep apart from both wings.[3]

    Southern clans

    The south wing joins the Naiman and Sarybagysh of the Chatkal Highlands with the clans of the Toktogul basin and the Fergana Rim, among them the Kökjal and the Chubar. Its members are more ethnically mixed than the northern clans, and their main concerns are water, the Toktogul Hydroelectric Complex and trade with Uzbekistan.[2]

    Cultural regions

    Ethnographers and UNMI staff also group the clans into six cultural regions, defined by dialect, religious practice, herding and marriage ties rather than by politics. Each region roughly matches a block of pastures, though clan lands overlap at the edges.[1][2]

    Region Clans Pastures Character

    Northern Steppe Boztorgoy, Akbakay, Kumbel, Jalpak, Karajal, Sarykush The southern Betpak-Dala, the Moiynkum, the Chu–Sarysu lowland and the piedmont plain Long migrations, cattle and sheep; insular, with little interest in IAC affairs

    The smallest clan, the Kaytkan ("the returned"), was refounded in the Keñ-Suu valley between 1918 and 1921 by families who came back from the flight to China in 1916 (see Urkun: The Lost Tribes of 1916).[1]

    The Karatau clans

    The Kulan, Arkar and Jeyren call themselves the Eski El ("the old people") and say they lived in the Karatau before the Iradis arrived. Other Iradis call them Karataulyk and regard them as strange. Their religion keeps the cross and the White Rider of Iradi folk Christianity (see Religion in Irad) but has no churches or priests. Its rites, held at night in caves and ruined caravanserais such as Rabat-i Kamal, center on spirit possession by the Rider and the saints and are led by specialists called ak bakshy ("white shamans"); they also sacrifice white lambs at springs, which other Iradis reject as pagan. The Syro-Iradi hierarchy describes the practices as superstition but rarely intervenes. Observers have compared the relationship to that between Haitian Vodou and Catholicism.[1]

    Leading clans

    • Tanıp. The most politically prominent clan. Its pastures are the upper Kara-Suu valley, and its center, with the Kara-Suu Tribal Elders Council Hall, is at the head of the Uzun-Akmat valley over the Sarybie Pass; the clan calls its whole country Kara-Suu. Its biy is Bektur Zhumabek uulu, acclaimed in 2001, who traces his descent from Muratbek Tanıp-Biy.
    • Karakoyun. One of the largest clans. Its genealogy places its founder as the eldest son of the clans' common ancestor, so it holds the senior seat at the Kurultai although it is less prominent than the Tanıp. Its elder Biybolot Moldosh uulu is a long-serving mediator.[1]
    • Akjele ("white tether line"). A mountain clan of the upper Besh-Tash valley and the Tanıp's closest ally. Bektur's mother, Zuura Tashtan kyzy, came from the Akjele. The two clans share summer camps on the plateau.
    • Boztorgoy ("gray lark"). The leading steppe clan, holding winter pastures in the Moiynkum and grazing along the northern sector of the line facing Nurzhan. It keeps more cattle than the mountain clans and sells feeder cattle and lambs to the feedlots and feed mills of the Chu plain. Its biy since 2012, Sabyrbek Duishön uulu (born 1966), commanded steppe detachments of the Front in 2007. His elder brother, Kudaibergen Duishön uulu, led the riders of the Chu clans who fought for the Provisional Government in 1992 and was killed at the Kobozev railhead; he is remembered as a national hero, and his memory gives Sabyrbek standing beyond the steppe.[3]
    • Kökjal ("gray mane", an epithet of the wolf). A clan of the Toktogul basin, many of whose families were resettled when the reservoir flooded the valley in the 1970s. Its elder Tabaldy Sultanaly uulu (born 1949), of Ozgörüsh, speaks for the flooded-pasture claims.
    • Chubar ("dappled", a horse color). The main Iradi clan of the Fergana Rim, divided by the Qara-Chik Demarcation Line since 2007. The highland Chubar of Arslanbob, the upper Kara-Unkur valley and Mailuu-Suu remain with the clans; their biy, Döölötbek Ashym uulu (born 1957), holds the clan's seat in the Kurultai, and the clan meets on the meadow below the shrine of Arslanbob-Ata. The lowland Chubar of Bazar-Korgon and the farm belt below the hills have been in the ISEZ since August 2007, when their elders, led by the walnut trader Abdumalik Mamadaliev, declared for the side that had stopped the Bill of Sale, which would have handed the rim to Uzbekistan; the highland elders refused to follow. Generations of marriage with Uzbek and Kyrgyz neighbors have given the clan Muslim lineages, especially in the lowlands. By Chancellery estimates, about a third of its households are Muslim. The biy is a Christian, but Muslim elders sit on his council. Mosques in the highland villages are run by local imams under the council's authority (see Islam in Irad).[4]

    Rivalries

    Trade permits

    The sharpest rivalry is between the Tanıp and the Boztorgoy over trade with the Irad Special Economic Zone (ISEZ). Clan councils issue the tribal travel permits that the Gendarmerie registers at the crossings. The Iradi Freedom Society Chancellery divides the seasonal bulk contracts among the clans. Until 2019 the Boztorgoy, whose pastures lie closest to the northern crossings, held a share of both that other clans considered out of proportion. In August 2019 the Kurultai adopted a quota roll that shares contract volumes by herd numbers reported to the Chancellery. The Boztorgoy "stood aside" rather than block it.[3][5] ISEZ business circles regard Sabyrbek as a pragmatic partner, and mountain elders mock the Boztorgoy as the "cattle clan". Sabyrbek has said in public that his clan supports the IAC and its moratorium. Boztorgoy elders have also said they would consider surveys of the Muyunkum Natural Gas Field on royalty terms.[3]

    The Chatkal feud

    The Naiman and Sarybagysh share the Chatkal valley and its culture but have been divided by a feud since the early Soviet period. In 1916 most Sarybagysh fled over the passes to China while most Naiman stayed; the returning Sarybagysh found Naiman herds on their pastures. In 1930–31 a Naiman elder became chairman of the collective farm that absorbed both clans, and a Sarybagysh elder's son was killed in a quarrel over the farm's pastures. The Sarybagysh refused the customary restitution (kun), and the killing was never settled under adat. The two clans keep separate summer camps, rarely intermarry, dispute a boundary through the walnut forest and fielded separate El-Choro detachments in 2007. They are seated apart at the Kurultai and act together mainly against outsiders.[1]

    Water

    The south wing is itself divided over the Toktogul reservoir. The Kökjal press for compensation for flooded pasture before the IAC agrees to drawdown. The Chubar on both sides of the line and other Fergana Rim clans need summer releases for their cotton and orchards, and on this point they side with the Uzbek observers on the joint technical commission. The dispute came to a head in the long Kurultai of 2022 (see Naryn Water and Power Protocol).[2]

    Pasture

    Pasture disputes go to the Autonomous Tribal Grazing Arbitration Tribunal. Recurring cases include the autumn corridors of Boztorgoy flocks across Karakoyun and Tanıp land, and crowded camps on the Aksay Plateau.[6]

    Religion

    The division of the Chubar is a lasting source of tension. Lowland elders still attend the August Kurultai within the Chubar delegation, by the biy's leave, but may not lay the whip, and northern elders, who call them the "bazaar Chubar", ask whose interests they speak for; the Gendarmerie in turn questions their journeys to the Aksay Plateau. Families on the two sides cross the line for weddings and funerals at the Mailuu-Suu and Arslanbob road crossings, and walnuts and apricots from the highland villages come down the valleys to the Bazar-Korgon bazaar, where highland growers accuse lowland buyers of fixing prices. Some northern elders also object to Muslim Chubar aksakals joining the Kurultai's opening prayers. After the Gendarmerie commander Ruslan Toktarov described Fergana Rim Muslims as "guests" in December 2024, Chubar elders on both sides of the line joined the Sunni clerics who condemned the remark. The August 2025 Kurultai said that the clans "know no guests among their own".[4]

    Succession to Bektur

    Bektur turned 58 in August 2026. No rule settles who would follow him, and he has declined to discuss the matter in public. Observers separate two questions.[7]

    • The Tanıp chieftaincy. A Tanıp clan assembly would acclaim the next biy, as it did in 2001. Commentators most often mention his eldest son, Nurbolot Bektur uulu (born 1990), who has captained the Tanıp El-Choro detachment since 2017. Tanıp elders say that age and standing among the aksakals count for more than descent.
    • The IAC's spokesmanship. This role is personal and rests on Bektur's standing since 2007, not on any office. Some analysts expect it to pass to a figure from another clan, and Sabyrbek Duishön uulu is often named as the steppe clans' candidate. Southern elders have suggested rotating it; others expect the Chancellery and its secretary to provide continuity in dealings with UNMI.[7][8]
