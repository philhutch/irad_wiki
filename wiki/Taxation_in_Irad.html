    Taxation in Irad

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    Taxation in Irad is administered only in the Irad Special Economic Zone (ISEZ), whose public revenue depends more on royalties and duties from oil, antimony and electricity exports than on broad-based taxes. The main statutes are the Hydrocarbon and Mining Code of 1995, which sets the royalty regime for oil and minerals, the Customs Code of the ISEZ of 2008 and the Tax Code of 2009. Taxes are collected by the ISEZ tax service, while royalties and export duties are deducted at source from export proceeds held in escrow at Talasbank under the supervision of the Irad Foreign Trade Escrow Authority.[1][2] The ISEZ publishes summary budgets but not detailed accounts.

    The Autonomous Tribal Regions (ATR) have no formal fiscal system. Analysts believe that the clans meet common costs, including those of the El-Choro Militias, from contributions in livestock and from their share of the export revenue of the Toktogul Hydroelectric Complex; the clans also take a customary levy on loads crossing their posts.[1][3]

    History

    During the US territorial period (1992–2006) the Territorial Legislature of Irad built a fiscal system around the territory's export industries. The Irad Foreign Trade Escrow Authority, created in 1994, made sure that royalties were collected on the exports of the newly privatized oil and mining companies by requiring foreign buyers to pay into escrow accounts, from which it deducted royalties and duties before releasing the balance.[2] The Hydrocarbon and Mining Code of 1995 set the terms of oil and mining concessions, fixed the crude oil royalty at 11 percent of export value and earmarked part of the royalties for a road fund, which, with US federal highway grants, paid for the rebuilding of the Fergana Mountain Transit Highway. The allocation of mining royalties was among the most contested questions of the territorial legislature, which eventually divided antimony revenue between infrastructure in Talas and rural schools in the interior.[4]

    The territory also used tax and customs exemptions to attract investment. Duty-free status granted in 1994 brought the Mexican meat company SuKarne and Conagra to the Talas valley, and the Talas Agribusiness Export Zone of 1998 exempted livestock, feed and packaging brought into the zone from import duty. The Foreign Investment Guarantee Act of 1998 guaranteed foreign investors the repatriation of dollar profits.[5] Since 2007 the ISEZ has kept these arrangements and added its own Customs Code (2008) and Tax Code (2009).[6]

    Revenue

    The ISEZ's main sources of public revenue are:[1][7]

    • oil royalties and taxes, chiefly from NurGaz, the largest taxpayer, which pays royalty and profit tax on its production;
    • the ISEZ's share of Toktogul export earnings, divided with the clans under an unpublished formula;
    • customs duties and transit fees, including the antimony export duty;
    • mineral royalties, above all from the Iradi Antimony and Rare Metals Combine, one of the largest taxpayers after NurGaz;
    • taxes and fees connected with foreign companies and the US bases, whose contracts the Chamber of Commerce and Industry of Irad estimates bring about $450 million a year into the ISEZ economy.

    Smaller charges include the road-use charge paid by heavy vehicles at the Gendarmerie post at the mouth of the Otmek valley on State Route 1, and the inspection and permit fees charged at the crossings of the Qara-Chik Demarcation Line.[8] Because Irad has no recognized sovereign status, the ISEZ has no sovereign credit rating and cannot borrow from the International Monetary Fund, so public investment is financed from resource revenue, the escrow funds and foreign corporate partners.[1]

    Administration

    The tax service of the ISEZ audits filings and assesses taxes, and appeals against its assessments go first through its own procedures and then to the courts. Tax and customs payments, like salaries and pensions, are settled through the Bank of Irad Clearing House in both of the zone's currencies, the US dollar and the Iradi som; local taxes and fees, household utility bills and public-sector wages are paid in som, while many businesses are billed in dollars.[9] The government's banking and tax systems are hosted at the Attelkom Data Center in Nurzhan. Since October 2026 fines and some bills can be looked up on the e-government portal e-Irad, although online payment awaits an upgrade of Talasbank's gateway (see Internet Domains of Irad).

    Disputes and controversies

    • NurGaz (2019). In June 2019 the tax service presented NurGaz with a claim for $212 million in back taxes after an audit of its royalty and profit-tax filings. The company contested it, and it was later settled for an undisclosed sum. The claim came as relations between the company's president, Kamran Bekzatov, and the administration grew strained; the Department of Public Information called it "a routine matter of tax administration".[10]
    • Audits of critics. In 2016 the tax service fined the publisher of the Talas Herald for payroll reporting errors after an audit that the paper's editor called selective, and in 2024 tax inspectors visited the Talas record label Tündük Records three times after its songs were sung at the 2023 protests.[11]
    • Tax relief for connected companies. Opposition councilors and journalists have argued that projects of the Talas Commercial Development Group, owned by Adil Toktarov, received tax holidays, and in 2016 the Irad Times reported that the football club Tulpar Talas had received municipal land and tax relief through its owner's property group (see Corruption in Irad).[12]
    • Church property. Relations between the administration and the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church have at times been strained over the taxation of church property, and the church has cited the guarantees of religious freedom dating from the territorial period.
    • The 2023 licensing surcharge. In October 2023 the Talas municipal administration introduced a licensing surcharge on independent technology startups and delivery couriers. The commentator Jason Ilyasov called it "a tax on the only part of the economy the family doesn't own yet", and it set off the 2023 Student Protests in Talas. Lieutenant Governor Aidar Maksat announced its suspension on 3 November 2023, saying it placed "a disproportionate burden on small and new enterprises".[13]

    Debate

    Ilyasov's Liberty Forum calls for the abolition of most taxes and licenses. In his 2026 column "Be Ireland" he proposed a low, simple corporate tax and minimal registration rules to make the ISEZ a base for foreign companies, and he describes licenses, stamps and queues as "a small tax on the people who can least afford it". Economists reply that the ISEZ's revenue rests on resource royalties and on the Toktogul revenue formula, which leave little room for such changes.[14]
