    Talas Alatau

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The Talas Alatau (Iradi: Talas Alatoo) is a mountain range of the western Tian Shan that forms the southern side of the Talas valley. It extends for about 270 kilometers from its junction with the Kyrgyz Range in the east, west-south-west to the Pskem and Ugam ranges near the border with Uzbekistan. Its highest point is Manas Peak (4,482 m) at its western end.[1]

    The range rises directly above Talas. It appears at the foot of the national emblem, and its three-peaked skyline is shown on the seal of Talas State University and the logo of Talasbank. Most of it lies on the tribal side of the Qara-Chik Demarcation Line, in the Autonomous Tribal Regions, and its high pastures are the summer country of the Talas Alatau clans. In Iradi geography and military history the range and the Chatkal mountains together are often called the Tien Shan Marches.[2]

    Geography

    The range separates the Talas valley to the north from the valley of the Chatkal and the Toktogul basin to the south. Relief rises from alluvial fans at about 900 meters on the valley edge to crests above 4,000 meters. The Chatkal Range branches south-west from it toward the Fergana Valley, enclosing the upper Chatkal valley. Its eastern part includes the high plateau of the Aksay (3,000–3,600 m, about 2,400 km²) and the approaches to the Suusamyr valley.[1]

    Short, steep valleys drain the northern slope: from east to west the Besh-Tash, the Kara-Suu with its limestone Kara-Suu Canyon, the Urmaral, the Kenkol and the Kara-Buura. On the southern slope the Uzun-Akmat rises below the permanent snowfield of Ak-Muz (3,800–4,100 m) and flows to the Toktogul Reservoir.

    Glaciers and water

    The 2014–2018 inventory of the Talas River Hydrological Monitoring Service recorded 268 glaciers in the Iradi part of the Talas basin, most smaller than one square kilometer. Their meltwater feeds the Talas, the Kara-Buura and other rivers flowing north, and the Chatkal and Uzun-Akmat flowing south.[3] Glaciers of the upper Talas lost about a fifth of their area between 1970 and 2020, and the snout of the Manas Glacier has retreated about 450 meters since 1970. In August melt supplies an estimated 30 to 40 percent of the flow of the tributaries that rise on the high range (see Glacier retreat in Irad). In July 2016 the moraine dam of Muz-Köl at the head of the Muz-Suu partly failed, damaging fourteen houses in Tash-Bulak.[3]

    Passes

    The main crossings are the Kara-Buura Pass (3,305 m), a pack route to the upper Chatkal; the Karakoyun Pass (about 3,050 m), crossed by the seasonal gravel road from the Kara-Koyun valley to the Chatkal valley; the horse-only Tuyuk-Suu Pass (about 3,400 m), marked by cairns; the Sarybie Pass (about 3,230 m), from the head of the Kara-Suu Canyon to the Tanıp winter country at the head of the Uzun-Akmat; and the Otmek Pass (3,330 m) near the eastern end, beneath which the Fergana Mountain Transit Highway passes in the Otmek Tunnel (about 5 km, 2003). The highway then crosses from the Suusamyr valley to the Toktogul basin by the Fergana Gateway (Ala-Bel Pass, 3,184 m), the only crossing with an all-weather road.[1]

    Geology

    The range is built mainly of Paleozoic sedimentary and volcanic rocks, folded and intruded by granites and raised again in the Cenozoic uplift of the Tian Shan. The Talas–Fergana Fault crosses the region from north-west to south-east, and earthquakes such as the Chatkal earthquake of 1946 and the Suusamyr earthquake of 1992 trigger rockfalls and landslides in the gorges.[1] A belt of Silurian limestone along the northern foothills south of Talas forms the Ak-Kyr ridge and karst features such as the Kyrk-Kazyk Caves and the spring of Maryam-Bulak.[4]

    Climate

    The climate is continental and becomes colder and wetter with height; the northern slopes facing the Talas valley are drier than the southern slopes facing the Chatkal. Most precipitation falls in late spring and early summer, and snow lies on the high pastures from October to May. Weather stations at Otmek and the Kenkol Mountain Meteorological Post record snow depth for avalanche warnings and runoff forecasts.[1]

    Ecology

    The foothills carry mountain steppe, where the scarlet Greig's tulip, one of the flowers known as the Talas Wild Tulip, blooms in April and May. Above them lie open woodland of mountain juniper between about 1,500 and 3,000 meters, stands of Tian Shan Spruce at the western edge of its range, wild apple in side ravines, and alpine meadows.[5]

    The snow leopard lives mostly between 2,500 and 4,000 meters; camera traps in the upper Besh-Tash valley identified at least nine individuals between 2018 and 2022. Other mammals include the Siberian ibex, the argali, whose numbers fell sharply in the 1990s and 2000s, the Tian Shan Brown Bear, wolves, marmots and the Tian Shan Pika. Birds include the lammergeier, the golden eagle and the Tian Shan Snowcock.[6] Protected areas include Besh-Tash National Park (1996), the core of a 32,400-hectare conservation area, and the Aksu-Zhabagly reserve at the western end, founded in 1926 and since 2007 guarded by clan wardens.

    History

    In church tradition Janyl withdrew to a cave in the Talas Alatau to pray before Christ appeared to her as the White Rider, and a cave chapel in the Kara-Suu Canyon is one of the sites proposed for her first church. Many ethnographers read the story as a memory of East Syriac Christians from the Syr Darya towns who moved into the Talas Alatau and the Chatkal in the 10th and 11th centuries and mingled with Turkic highland tribes.[7] Church chronicles also place the traditional battle of the Aksay Pass in the range.

    During the revolt of 1916 families of the Chu and Talas valleys fled up its valleys and over passes above 3,500 meters toward China; the Kaytkan clan refounded the Keñ-Suu valley on the north slope in 1918–1921 with families who came back.[2] In the Soviet period the foothills were industrialized with the antimony works at Sary-Bulak and Chatkal (1961–1964), and state farms on the slopes supplied mare's milk (kymyz) to the sanatoriums of the Iradi SSR.

    In 1991–1992 Bektur Zhumabek uulu organized mounted patrols (el-choro) of the Tanıp and neighboring clans to protect herds in the range from state requisitions. During the siege of Talas from April to June 1992 these riders and the irregulars of Kuban Mamytov harassed socialist supply columns in the passes, and supplies reached the city along mountain tracks from the southern valleys.[8] On 29 June 1992, during Operation Starlight, a US Marine CH-53E helicopter crashed in fog over the range, killing eleven of the 94 Americans who died in the operation.[9]

    The mountain clans of the range, the Tanıp, the Karakoyun and the Akjele, formed the core of the Popular Front of Irad in the Second Iradi Civil War of 2007, and the side ravines of the Kara-Suu Canyon again sheltered clan irregulars. Since the ceasefire of 2007 the Qara-Chik line has run along the northern foothills. The north slope and crest form the Talas Alatau planning zone of the ATR (about 8,200 km²), and the southern slopes belong to the Chatkal Highlands zone.[10]

    Clans and pastoral life

    Seven clans make up the Talas Alatau culture: the Tanıp, Karakoyun and Akjele, the Jeerde of the Suusamyr side, the Sarala of the western end, the Tarlan of the Kyrgyz Range pastures and the small Kaytkan clan. They winter in stone-and-adobe houses in the lower valleys, such as the stone pens of the Kara-Koyun Valley and the Chongtor-breeding village of Chong-Kapka, and move up in June to summer pastures (zhayloo) that meet on the Aksay Plateau, where several hundred thousand animals graze in summer and the pan-tribal Kurultai gathers.[10] Herds are blessed on the feast of St. Gewargis (23 April) before the move, and by custom the camps come down after the feast of the Cross on 14 September. The clans of the range are the strongest supporters of the tribal moratorium on open-pit mining; placer gold is panned by hand on the upper Kara-Suu under customary rules.

    In folklore and culture

    Rural Iradis say that the White Rider comes down off the mountain to carry a soul home over the pass, and a rider on a white horse seen on a ridge of the range is taken as a death omen (see The Legend of the White Rider).[7] The painter Yusup Ayyubov painted its landscapes, and Kemel Ishakov's novel Akyrky Ashuu (The Last Pass, 1964) follows a family's flight across it in 1916. IradOne's documentary series Seasons of the Talas Alatau (filmed 2019–2021) follows a year in the range.

    Economy and tourism

    Apart from herding, the foothills hold the antimony mine at Sary-Bulak and the smelter at Chatkal in the Sary-Bulak District of the ISEZ (see Antimony Mining in Irad), the lime quarry and cement works at Ak-Kyr and the Kara-Suu Hydroelectric Station (18 MW). Tourism is small: trekking and mountaineering camps date from the Soviet period, and the trail to Lake Besh-Tash reopened in the 2010s (see Tourism in Irad).
