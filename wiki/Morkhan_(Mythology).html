    Morkhan (Mythology)

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    Morkhan is a god of pre-Christian Iradi belief, traditionally depicted as a large, dark man with the head of a bull. The origin of the name is uncertain. In Iradi Christian tradition he came to be identified with Satan, and black, the opposite of the holy white of the White Rider, is his color. Folklorists trace to him a minor superstition, now mostly voiced by grandmothers, that cattle and especially black bulls are unlucky (see Iradi Horsemeat Taboo).[1]

    Since the 1990s his bull mask and horns have become a stock image of Iradi popular music, from Mor-Wave to Mor-Pop, to the repeated objection of the clergy.

    In folklore

    Stories of Morkhan were collected in the first volume, Myths and Legends (1980), of Sagyn Jusupbekov's Oral Folk Art of Irad, alongside the sky and ancestor cults and the goddess Umay.[1] In the tales he is a dark power of the night and of misfortune, associated with black things and with cattle.

    Iradi folk Christianity is organized around a contrast of white and black. White, the color of the White Rider's horse, of milk and of felt, marks what is holy, while black belongs to Morkhan. Shamans (bakshy) are regarded as his servants, and the church has condemned their rites since at least the Kokand period, while herbal healers (tabyp) are tolerated.[2] In the folk religion of the Karatau, whose ritual specialists, the ak bakshy ("white shamans"), say that they serve the Rider and not Morkhan, offerings of black wool and bull horns are left at the edge of camp to keep him away.

    The cattle superstition is mild, on the level of a black cat in Europe, and few take it seriously. A common saying holds that seeing a cow before a horse in the morning brings a bad day. It is not a taboo: plains Iradis have always kept cattle and eat beef.[3] The one occasion on which it is taken seriously is a wedding. In highland and old Talas families the bride must not see a cow on her wedding day, a rule kept as strictly as the white dress, and cattle are moved out of sight of the wedding yurt; elsewhere the custom survives as a joke, and a wedding party in Nurzhan that passes a field of cows may tell the bride to close her eyes.[4] Traces appear elsewhere in custom and legend. Some older people call the Cow year of the twelve-year animal cycle unlucky, and some highland grandmothers call it the Year of the Argali instead (see Iradi calendar); a legend of Jety-Kaiyng describes its red sandstone pillars as seven bulls of Morkhan turned to stone; and kara kün ("black day") is a phrase associated with him.

    In Christian tradition

    The church identifies Morkhan with Satan, and preachers set him against the White Rider of Janyl's vision, whom popular devotion identifies with the rider of Revelation 19:11.[2] Clergy of the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church object to the use of his image in music and on television, and parish priests have preached against songs that address him.

    In popular culture

    Film and literature

    In Nurlan Sadikov's Zarina (1968) a figure in a bull mask, whom Iradi viewers recognized as Morkhan, appears in unmarked dream sequences, and critics read such pre-Christian imagery in the films of the period as a quiet departure from Soviet norms (see Cinema of Irad). Soviet censors cut black bulls, horse omens and other "folk color" from the Ottuk pocket westerns of the 1960s. In Kemel Ishakov's novel Kara Buka (The Black Bull, 1977) a horse herder takes a job on a Virgin Lands cattle farm against his grandmother's warnings about Morkhan's black bull.[5]

    Music

    The duo tüntolk used images of Morkhan in the lyrics of their album Jolchu (1999), and its track "Morkhan, Karañgy Ee" ("Morkhan the Dark Lord"), a prayer to him turned inside out, has been condemned by clergy since the full album appeared in 2000. Some writers derive the mor of Mor-Wave from his name.[6] Mor-Folk ensembles such as Eski Ün set liturgical chant beside his image; in 2019 Eski Ün canceled a concert in Iyık Janyl after two parish priests objected.

    Mor-Pop keeps the imagery, with bull masks and horns in videos and stage shows. The girl group Karakush wore horned headdresses based on Morkhan in the video for "Ak Börü" (2021) and released "Morkhan Tünü" ("Morkhan's Night", 2022). The novelty group Altyn Muuz performed "Morkhan Baby" (2021) on an IradOne Sunday-morning children's program; after clergy complained, later broadcasts omitted the song, and on a rural tour in 2022 the group wore argali horns instead of bull horns.

    Football

    Supporters of Tulpar Talas taunt the black-clad fans of Neftchi Nurzhan as "Morkhan's herd", and Neftchi's ultras group Vyshka has adopted black as a badge of pride (see Football in Irad).[7]
