    Chatkal Placer Mining Inspectorate

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The Chatkal Placer Mining Inspectorate (Iradi: Chatkal chachyndy ken inspektsiyasy) is a government agency of the Irad Special Economic Zone (ISEZ) that licenses and inspects alluvial (placer) gold workings and monitors mine tailings and water quality in the mining districts south of Talas. It was created in 1998 by the Territorial Legislature of Irad to regulate gold panning and dredging in the basin of the Chatkal River. Most of that basin has been under the control of the Autonomous Tribal Regions (ATR) since 2007, and the inspectorate's work is now concentrated on the ISEZ side of the Qara-Chik Demarcation Line, chiefly in the Sary-Bulak District.[1]

    History

    Small-scale gold washing in the rivers of the Chatkal range and the Talas Alatau dates back centuries. It revived in the 1990s, when unemployed miners and herders began working river gravels with sluices and small pumps, and several foreign-backed companies applied for dredging licenses. Reports of mercury use and silted irrigation channels led the Territorial Legislature to create the inspectorate in 1998 under the Ministry of Natural Resources, with powers under the 1995 Hydrocarbon and Mining Code to license placer workings, inspect sites and impose fines.[1]

    Until 2007 the inspectorate worked throughout the Chatkal basin from offices in Talas and in the Chatkal valley. After the Bishkek Ceasefire Agreement (2007), the upper basin fell on the tribal side of the line, where clan assemblies maintain a moratorium on open-pit and mechanized gold mining. The inspectorate withdrew its field staff from the tribal side and took on the monitoring of tailings dams and mine water at the antimony works in the Sary-Bulak District.[2]

    Functions

    The inspectorate's main tasks are:

    • licensing small placer leases on streams in ISEZ territory, and inspecting the few mechanized operations that remain;
    • registering individual gold washers (altynchy), who must sell their gold to licensed buyers;
    • sampling surface water for turbidity, cyanide, mercury, arsenic and antimony below workings and tailings dams;
    • inspecting the stability of tailings dams, including the Chatkal Tailings Repository and the dumps of the Sary-Bulak Antimony Works;
    • imposing fines, suspending licenses and ordering the reclamation of worked-out ground.[1]

    In 2022 it had about 70 staff, including 28 field inspectors, and an annual budget of about $1.9 million.[1] Since 2016 it has used commercial satellite imagery to detect unlicensed workings, including on the tribal side of the line, where it cannot act directly.

    Relations with the tribal regions

    On the tribal side, panning on gravel bars is regulated by clan councils under customary law (adat), as in the Kara-Suu Gold Prospecting Area. Pollution from either side reaches shared rivers, and the inspectorate exchanges water-quality data with tribal monitors through the liaison channels of the United Nations Mission in Irad. Disputes over damage to pastures and watering points are heard by the Autonomous Tribal Grazing Arbitration Tribunal when ISEZ operators are involved.[2]

    Criticism

    Environmental groups and the newspaper Irad Times have argued that the inspectorate is too small to police the antimony industry, whose parent company is one of the largest taxpayers in the ISEZ, and that its fines are low. They cite raised arsenic and antimony levels recorded in streams below the Sary-Bulak workings. The inspectorate says that its readings at the main monitoring points have been within ISEZ limits since the late 2010s.[3]
