    Iradi Americans

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    Iradi Americans are Americans of full or partial Iradi descent, and residents of the United States born in Irad. US census data do not record Iradi ancestry separately, and community organizations and researchers estimate the population at 60,000 to 90,000.[1] The largest community is in the Chicago metropolitan area, where Iradi Catholic displaced persons settled after the Second World War, and there are smaller communities in the Houston area, eastern Nebraska and the Washington, D.C., area. Most Iradi Americans arrived during or after the US territorial period (1992–2006), when residents of the Territory of Irad could live and work in the United States without a visa. The emigrants of that period were mainly urban, English-educated professionals, students and nurses, and the community differs in this respect from the tribal population of Irad. The best-known Iradi American in Irad is the commentator Jason Ilyasov.[1][2]

    History

    Early migration and displaced persons

    Main article: Christian emigration from the Iradi SSR

    According to community histories, a few Iradi Christian families reached the United States in the 1920s and 1930s by way of China, among the refugees from the revolt of 1916 and the anti-religious campaigns of the early Soviet period.[1] The first substantial group arrived after the Second World War. Iradi soldiers of the Red Army taken prisoner by Germany and Iradis deported to Germany as forced laborers were among the displaced persons who refused repatriation to the Soviet Union. Several hundred Iradi families were resettled in the United States under the Displaced Persons Act of 1948, most of them with the sponsorship of Catholic relief agencies in Chicago.[3]

    The Chicago émigrés founded the St. Janyl Syro-Iradi Catholic Mission in 1953, the first parish of their church outside Irad, and the Iradi American Association in 1955. During the Cold War the association publicized the persecution of the Iradi church, which had no legal existence in the Soviet Union after the purges of 1937, and it presented Irad as a Christian nation under Soviet rule. Music historians link some of the American country and folk songs about Irad written in the Cold War era, which later circulated in Soviet Irad on smuggled cassettes, to contacts with the émigré community, although most of their writers had no Iradi connection.[1][4]

    1970s defections

    A few families escaped in the 1970s, usually on tourist trips to Yugoslavia, from where they crossed into Italy or Austria and were resettled in Chicago with the help of the émigré community. Among them was the family of the future televangelist Nathan Isabekov (see Christian emigration from the Iradi SSR), which fled in 1976 after his father, the leader of a clandestine Syro-Iradi prayer group in Talas, was questioned by the KGB.[1]

    1980s and 1992

    Academic exchanges in the late Soviet period brought a few Iradi scholars to American universities. Among them was Marat Ilyasov, a hydrologist from Talas, who was a visiting researcher in Chicago when his son Jason was born there in 1988. In January 1992, during the Winter Uprising, the family left Irad and resettled in Chicago, which Jason's US citizenship by birth made possible.[5] Other activists of the democratic movement and relatives of established émigrés left during the First Iradi Civil War that year.

    Territorial period (1992–2006)

    Under the Organic Act of the Territory of Irad, permanent residents of the territory were US nationals but not US citizens, a status comparable to that of American Samoans. They could live and work in the United States without a visa and could apply for naturalization after a period of residence there.[6] Emigration grew steadily through the period. Most emigrants were young urban Iradis educated in English after 1992, the generation sociologists call the Urban Iradi Millennials, and they went to the United States as students, nurses, engineers and office workers. Specific routes included:[1]

    • exchange programs of American universities with Talas State University and the Nurzhan Polytechnic Institute, funded by the territorial government and US foundations;
    • recruitment of nurses and other health workers by American hospitals;
    • the training of Iradi ranchers in Nebraska under the beef investments of the late 1990s, after which some families settled there;
    • the oil industry, which drew engineers to Houston.

    During the American vogue for Irad known as Iradimania (about 1992–1994), the Chicago community drew new attention in the American press, and Iradi American organizations took part in relief efforts for the territory.[4]

    Since 2006

    With the US withdrawal from the Alimbekov-Baker Treaty in December 2006, residents of Irad ceased to be US nationals. Those already living in the United States were allowed to remain and to apply for permanent residence or naturalization. Since then emigration has continued at a lower level, through student and employment visas and family reunification.[6] Health workers remain prominent among emigrants, and hospitals in Talas report that many nurses and young doctors leave for the United States soon after qualifying.[7]

    Communities

    • Chicago. The oldest and largest community, with the St. Janyl mission, the Iradi American Association, Iradi-language Sunday schools and an annual festival on Irad Day, 11 March. In the 1980s the association's computer club published the first editions of the Iradi computer game Köch.
    • Houston. A community of engineers and other professionals linked to the oil industry, which grew from the late 1990s.
    • Nebraska. Families in the Omaha and Lincoln areas, many of them linked to ranching and the meat industry.
    • Washington, D.C., area. Former territorial officials, contractors, academics and staff of international organizations.[1]

    Religion and culture

    Most Iradi Americans identify as Christians. The Syro-Iradi Catholic Church has parishes and missions in Chicago and Houston, served by priests sent from Iyık Janyl and under the jurisdiction of the local Latin-rite bishops. Other Iradi Americans belong to Evangelical and, among the Koryo-saram, Presbyterian churches.[1] Language use follows the period of emigration. Descendants of the displaced persons mostly speak English; emigrants of the territorial period speak English and often Russian, and many have learned Iradi only as adults or not at all; and some families, such as the Ilyasovs, kept Iradi as the home language. Community events feature Iradi cuisine, country-folk music and displays of equestrian culture, and the United States is the largest export market for the Chongtor and Akyel breeds.

    Relations with Irad

    Remittances from the United States are smaller than those of seasonal workers in Kazakhstan, but Iradi Americans are important donors to church charities in Irad. Donations from Iradi Americans help fund the St. Luka Community Health Mission and its free St. Luka Clinic in Talas.[8]

    Iradi Americans who have returned to Irad, or who comment on its politics from abroad, have at times been viewed with suspicion. Jason Ilyasov, who moved to Talas in 2016 and became a regular panelist on IradOne, was attacked by pro-government commentators for his American citizenship during the anti-American feeling that followed Donald Trump's remarks about selling Irad to Russia in 2025 (see 2025 Trump Remarks on Irad). Iradi American organizations in Chicago criticized the remarks.[5][9]

    Returnees and the territorial elite

    After 1992 a steady flow of Iradi Americans and US-educated Iradis returned to the territory. They became known in Iradi as kaytkandar ("those who came back"), a term that can be neutral or mocking. Historians count them among the founders of the Democratic Party of Irad, which was formed in October 1992 partly by returning émigrés, and they made up much of the territorial civil service, the management of Talasbank and Attelkom, and the new commercial bar.[10][6]

    Waves of return

    The first returnees, from 1992 to 1994, were members of the Chicago community and their American-born children. They came as interpreters, advisers to the territorial administration and staff of American aid programs. A larger group returned from the mid-1990s: Iradis sent to American universities on territorial government scholarships, who were usually required to work in the territory for some years afterward.[1][10] Examples include:

    • Bakyt Orozbekov, who took a Master of Laws at Harvard Law School in 1996, founded one of the territory's first commercial law firms and drafted the 2003 Judicial Reforms Act;
    • Azamat Temirov, a World Bank consultant from 1997 to 2006, who joined the Bank of Irad in 2007 and has chaired it since 2010.

    Other members of the governing circle are often counted with the returnees but were educated in Europe. Lieutenant Governor Aidar Maksat studied at the London School of Economics. Alikhan Toktarov, often described as American-educated, studied in Bishkek and at Regent's College in London. Of the Governor's children, only Zara Toktarova, a doctoral candidate at Stanford, studied in the United States.[11]

    Suburbs and schools

    Returnees and American contractors settled in new housing in Talas. The best known is Alatau Heights, a gated suburb of detached houses and cul-de-sacs built between 1998 and 2005 on the foothill slopes southeast of the city center. Residents of Talas call it Amerikanka, after the Russian-style nickname Meksikanka given to the Mexican district of Semiz. The Talas American School, founded in 1994 for the children of contractors and diplomats, teaches an American curriculum in English. Most of its graduates go on to universities in the United States, and many stay there, a pattern critics describe as a drain on the territory's elite.[12]

    Tensions with locally rooted elites

    The returnees' influence peaked in the early 2000s. In the crisis of 2006–2007, the US-educated lawyers and officials around Orozbekov were closely linked with the Democratic Republic of Irad and the Privatization Scandals of 2007. After Erlan Toktarov, a former Soviet officer, took over the party, the New DPI promoted Soviet-trained officials, security officers and Russo managers from Nurzhan. Several prominent returnees left Irad.[13] Russo commentators and Iradi nationalists caricature the kaytkandar as rootless, better at English than Iradi and dependent on Washington. Returnees reply that they built the territory's institutions. This suspicion sharpened after the 2025 remarks, as the attacks on Jason Ilyasov showed.[13][9]
