    Archbishopric of Iyık Janyl

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The Archbishopric of Iyık Janyl, formally the Archeparchy of Iyık Janyl (Latin: Archieparchia Sanctae Ianylae; Iradi: Iyık Janyl Bash Yeparkhiyasy), is the metropolitan see of the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church. Its archbishop is the head of the church, and its cathedral is the Church of St. Janyl in Iyık Janyl. The see traces its origins to the Church of the East communities of the medieval Talas and Chu valleys. It received its present rank in 1931, when the bull Orientalis Stella brought the Iradi church into full communion with Rome. The archbishop has metropolitan authority over three suffragan eparchies, based in Talas, Nurzhan and the Chatkal highlands, and presides over the Syro-Iradi Council of Hierarchs. The archbishop since 2012 has been Mar Ephrem.[1]

    History

    Medieval origins

    East Syriac (Nestorian) Christianity reached the Talas and Chu valleys with Sogdian merchants between the 7th and 9th centuries, and the Church of the East maintained bishops in the cities of Transoxiana and Semirechye during the following centuries.[2] Christianity spread among Turkic pastoralists of the region during the rule of the Qara Khitai in the 12th century, and a church synod was held at Navekat in 1180 (see Nestorian Synod of Navekat (1180)). Church tradition places the foundation of a distinct Iradi Christian community in this period and attributes it to Janyl, who is said to have founded the lost First Church of Janyl after a vision of the White Rider. Historians regard her as a figure of legend whose historical existence is uncertain (see Janyl (Historical Figure)).[3]

    The remains of a medieval Christian town near the present city, the Ruins of Old Iyık Janyl (Shar-i-Kuhna), include the foundations of an 11th-century church. Manuscripts in the cathedral library record contacts with the wider church in the 13th century, including letters connected with the envoys sent to the Mongols by Pope Innocent IV.[3]

    Persecution and survival

    Under the Chagatai ruler Ali-Sultan (1339–1342) and during the campaigns of Timur in the late 14th century, the Christian dioceses of the region were destroyed. Iradi Christians survived in the mountain valleys, where they worshipped in secret and preserved their prayers in oral chant (ırda). Church tradition holds that a line of bishops continued among the mountain clans, but the written record for the 15th to 17th centuries is fragmentary, and historians cannot confirm an unbroken succession.[2][3]

    Kokand period

    In the early 18th century, Iradi Christian horsemen served as cavalry auxiliaries of the Khanate of Kokand against Dzungar raids. In 1727 Khan Abd al-Rahim Biy granted the Christian elders religious tolerance under the accord remembered as the Treaty of Talas (1727), and issued a decree (firman) funding a stone church at Iyık Janyl. The Church of St. Janyl became the seat of the Iradi bishops, and a settlement of clergy, craftsmen and herders grew up around it. The cathedral library was founded in 1732, and Bishop Mar Gewargis (died 1744) was buried in the church crypt.[4] In this period the Iradi church had no regular contact with the Patriarchate of the Church of the East or with Rome, and it functioned as an independent church under its own bishop.[3]

    Union with Rome

    After the Russian conquest of Kokand in 1876, the church was tolerated by the Tsarist authorities, although Russian Orthodox missions were favored. After the Russian Revolution, Bishop Mar Timotheos Alimbekov opened secret negotiations with the Holy See, conducted through papal representatives abroad between 1926 and 1931. On 14 September 1931 Pope Pius XI issued the bull Orientalis Stella, which received the church into full communion as an Eastern Catholic church sui iuris with its own East Syriac rite, and established Iyık Janyl as a metropolitan archeparchy. Mar Timotheos became its first archbishop.[5]

    Soviet repression

    Soviet officials regarded the church's link with Rome as a channel for foreign influence, and this suspicion fed the anti-religious campaigns of the 1930s. During The 1937 NKVD Clerical Purges, most of the archbishop's senior clergy were arrested and executed. The seminary was closed, and the cathedral was shut and its nave converted into a district grain store. Monks and lay sacristans hid the rarest manuscripts of the library in caves in the Kara-Suu Canyon, and families kept vestments and sacred vessels in their homes.[5] For the rest of the Soviet period the archeparchy had no legal existence. Liturgies were held in homes and in the camps of the herding clans. Church tradition holds that bishops were consecrated in secret to preserve the succession, but documentary evidence for this period is limited.[3] After the restoration of religious freedom in 1992 the Holy See appointed Mar Yuhanna Bakirov archbishop in December of that year; he led the archeparchy through the return of its buildings and institutions until his retirement in 2012.

    Restoration

    After the Winter Uprising of 1992, the cathedral was returned to the church and reconsecrated on 14 September 1992, the anniversary of Orientalis Stella. Under the US territorial administration, which guaranteed freedom of religion, the building was restored between 1993 and 1998 with territorial grants, US heritage funds and donations from Christian charities. The seminary reopened in 1994 as the St. Ephrem Major Seminary, and the library's manuscripts were conserved and digitized.[4] In the same years contact with American Catholic chaplains and Roman institutions brought Western devotions into urban parishes, and vernacular liturgical texts in Latin-script Iradi were approved. The Church of St. Janyl and the Ruins of Old Iyık Janyl were inscribed together as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2004, on a nomination submitted by the United States as the administering power.[6]

    Organization

    The archeparchy covers the city of Iyık Janyl and the surrounding valleys of the lower Naryn and the Fergana foothills, including highland parishes on the tribal side of the Qara-Chik Demarcation Line. Its 2023 diocesan directory lists 118 parishes and mission stations, about 40 of them in the Autonomous Tribal Regions.[1] The suffragan eparchies of Talas and Nurzhan serve the two largest cities and their districts, while the Chatkal highland eparchy serves mainly tribal parishes. The church reports about 3.4 million faithful in all.

    Under the Code of Canons of the Eastern Churches, the archbishop is appointed by the Pope from candidates proposed by the Council of Hierarchs. As of 2026 the suffragan bishops are Mar Toma Usenov of Talas, Mar Yakob Kim of Nurzhan and Mar Narsai Abdykerimov of the Chatkal Highlands, who has no fixed seat and visits his parishes from Iyık Janyl. Institutions under the archbishop's direct authority include:

    • the Cathedral Seminary of Iyık Janyl (St. Ephrem Major Seminary), which trains clergy for all four eparchies in Classical Syriac, theology and canon law;
    • the Cathedral Seminary Library of Iyık Janyl and the Iyık Janyl Diocesan Archives Vault;
    • the Order of St. Janyl the Hermit, whose motherhouse stands on the edge of the Naryn gorge south of the city, and the Iyık Janyl Monastic Cloister;
    • the Iyık Janyl Seminary Choir and the Iyık Janyl Pilgrimage Hostel;
    • rural dispensaries run by the religious orders, many of them in the tribal regions.

    Public role

    Since the Second Iradi Civil War, the archbishopric has kept a position of neutrality between the Democratic Party of Irad administration and the clan councils of the tribal regions. It has hosted meetings between representatives of the ISEZ and the Iradi Freedom Society, and the archbishop meets regularly with Sunni Muslim leaders and Russian Orthodox bishops to coordinate charitable work.[7] Each year on 24 August the Feast of St. Janyl draws pilgrims to the cathedral from both sides of the line, many of them on horseback. The archbishopric and the United Nations Mission in Irad organize the crossing points for riders from the tribal regions.

    Relations with the ISEZ government have at times been strained over school curricula and the taxation of church property. In these disputes the archbishopric has cited the guarantees of religious freedom dating from the territorial period.[7]
