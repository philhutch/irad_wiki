    Adat Law in the ATR

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    Adat law in the ATR (Iradi: adat, also salt) is the uncodified customary law applied by clan elders and judges in the Autonomous Tribal Regions (ATR) of Irad. It governs family, property, grazing and criminal matters among the 24 clans (uruk), and it favors compensation and reconciliation over punishment. Since the Bishkek Ceasefire Agreement (2007), which acknowledged customary jurisdiction on the tribal side of the Qara-Chik Demarcation Line, adat has operated outside the statutory court system headed by the Supreme Court of Irad, whose jurisdiction does not extend into the ATR.[1][2] Human rights organizations have criticized its treatment of women and its lack of written rules and appeals; its defenders describe it as cheap, fast and suited to pastoral life.

    History

    Customary courts of elders and judges (biys) were common to the pastoral peoples of the region before Russian rule. Under the Russian Empire, regulations for the administration of Turkestan in the late nineteenth century allowed "people's courts" of elected biys to apply customary law among nomads in civil and minor criminal cases, while serious crimes went to imperial courts.[3] Iradi clan courts of this period combined steppe custom with the moral teaching of the clandestine Christian communities.

    Soviet authorities abolished the biy courts in the late 1920s, and the collectivization of the 1930s broke up the pastoral economy on which they had rested. Elders nonetheless continued to settle disputes informally within collective farms, and clan genealogies (sanjyra) were kept orally.[4] During the US territorial period (1992–2006) the countryside was legally subject to the territorial courts, but aksakal arbitration was practiced openly and tolerated by the territorial authorities in rural areas. After the 2007 partition, the ceasefire agreement left justice on the tribal side to the clans. In 2022 the Iradi Freedom Society Chancellery published a Compendium of Customary Law (Adat) and Pasture Demarcations, a record of principles and precedents that its compilers describe as a guide rather than a code.[1]

    Courts and procedure

    Disputes are heard in open assemblies (aksakal sod) presided over by one or more biys, men chosen by their clan for their knowledge of custom and genealogy, their reputation for fairness and their skill in speech. Hearings usually take place outdoors or in a yurt, in the presence of the families concerned and other members of the community.[2]

    • Each party, often represented by a senior kinsman, sets out its case orally, and the judges question witnesses.
    • Witnesses may be asked to swear on bread or on the Gospel book.
    • The judges seek a settlement that both kin groups will accept, and their decision is announced publicly.
    • Disputes within a clan are heard by its own biys. Grazing disputes between clans go to the Autonomous Tribal Grazing Arbitration Tribunal, and matters affecting all the clans may be referred to the Kurultai on the Aksay Plateau.

    There is no formal appeal. A party dissatisfied with a decision may ask for a rehearing before a larger assembly of elders. Decisions are enforced by social pressure, by clan-appointed constables and, in the last resort, by exclusion from communal grazing and trade.

    Principles and remedies

    Adat treats offenses as matters between kin groups rather than between the individual and the state. Responsibility is collective, and the offender's family or lineage shares the obligation to compensate the victim's.[2]

    • Compensation (kun). Homicide, injury and serious theft are settled by payment of kun to the victim's kin. It was historically reckoned in horses and is now usually paid in cattle, sheep or cash. Payment of kun ends the dispute and is meant to prevent revenge.
    • Fines (aip). Lesser wrongs, such as livestock damage or insults, are punished by fines in livestock or goods.
    • Reconciliation. Settlements are often concluded with a public apology and a shared meal at which the families eat together.
    • Ostracism. A person or family who refuses to accept a decision may be excluded from clan gatherings, communal pastures and trade.
    • Refuge (panaa). Anyone who asks a household for refuge must be protected by it and by its clan until the elders settle the case, even an enemy; breaking it costs the clan its voice at the Kurultai for a year (see Panaa (Customary Law)).
    • Contribution (salym). Kin contribute by recognised shares to a man's kun or bride price, recorded by an elder and repaid in turn (see Salym (Custom)).
    • Honour. Feuds and fighting are bounded by the code of ar-namys, which forbids violence against women, children, the old, bards and priests and at churches, shrines and graves.

    Imprisonment is not used. Horse theft is among the gravest offenses, traditionally compensated nine animals for one (see Crime in Irad). Clan authorities enforce the taboo on eating horsemeat strictly, and anyone found slaughtering horses for sale faces seizure of the animals and expulsion from the clan.

    Family and property

    In family matters adat follows patrilineal descent. Property passes in the male line, and by custom the youngest son (kenje) inherits the parental household and cares for his parents in old age, while elder sons receive livestock when they marry. Marriage is a church sacrament, and most tribal Iradis marry in the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church. Elders handle the negotiation of marriage settlements and the disputes that follow separation (see Marriage in Irad).[4] Where adat and canon law conflict, as over divorce, elders and priests usually seek a compromise. Itinerant parish priests often sit with the elders at hearings and bless settlements with smoke from mountain juniper (arsha) and prayers to St. Janyl (see Arsha Juniper Incense Tradition).

    Pasture is held collectively by clans and lineages, not by individuals, and there are no written titles. Boundaries are marked by boundary stones (bel-tash) and remembered by elders. Seasonal migration between winter pastures (kyshtoo) and summer pastures (zhayloo) follows schedules agreed by clan pasture committees (see Central Asian Pastoral Migration System).[1]

    Relationship with ISEZ law

    The Irad Special Economic Zone (ISEZ) does not formally recognize decisions of the clan courts, and residents of the ATR arrested in the ISEZ are tried in its courts. In practice, cases that cross the line, such as livestock theft or road accidents, are often handled through the Joint Liaison Committee of the ceasefire. UNMI police advisers work with the municipal police of the ISEZ and the clan-appointed constables of the ATR on cross-line crime and missing-persons cases.[5] Some ISEZ officials and legal scholars argue that adat allows serious crimes to go unpunished. Clan leaders reply that compensation settles disputes that prosecution would prolong.

    Criticism

    Human Rights Watch has criticized adat as practiced in the ATR on several grounds:[2]

    • daughters and widows inherit little or nothing under patrilineal rules;
    • women are not admitted as biys and rarely speak at hearings;
    • there is no written law and no appeal from elder courts;
    • collective responsibility can penalize relatives who had no part in an offense.

    Tribal leaders and many residents defend the system as fast, cheap and focused on restoring relations between families. They regard it as an essential part of the clans' autonomy.[4]
