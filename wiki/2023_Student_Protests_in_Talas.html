    2023 Student Protests in Talas

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The 2023 Student Protests in Talas were a wave of demonstrations by university students and delivery couriers in Talas in October 2023, set off by a new municipal licensing surcharge on independent technology startups and couriers. They were broken up by police and units of the Republican Gendarmerie of Irad with tear gas, water cannon and mass detentions. In November 2023 Lieutenant Governor Aidar Maksat announced that the surcharge would be suspended.[1][2]

    Background

    The protests came during a period of economic stagnation and frustration among young urban Iradis, and in the year in which, according to independent reporting, the Governor's executive moved from Talas to the Boz-Korgon compound east of Nurzhan; the government maintains that Talas remains the operational capital. The gubernatorial election was due the following month.[3] In October 2023 the Talas municipal administration announced the surcharge. On OneDebate, the prime-time panel program of IradOne, the commentator Jason Ilyasov called it "a tax on the only part of the economy the family doesn't own yet", and clips of the exchange spread widely on social media.[1]

    The protests

    Within days, students and couriers gathered at Talas State University and marched on the government district. Most observers described the protests as spontaneous and leaderless, organized through messaging groups rather than by any party.[1] Small merchants joined some of the marches. Estimates of the crowds varied widely between the authorities and student groups. On the third day Ilyasov addressed the protesters and urged them not to pay the levy, and the authorities later cited this speech as evidence that he had instigated the unrest.

    Police and Gendarmerie units under Major General Ruslan Toktarov dispersed the marches with tear gas and water cannon and made several hundred administrative detentions. Ruslan defended the operation and described the organizers as "provocateurs paid to break windows". Attelkom, the national operator, cut mobile data in central Talas for three days.[2] Ilyasov was held in administrative detention for three days and released without charge after press-freedom groups criticized his detention. A Talas Herald reporter, Erkin Bolot uulu, was held for ten days.

    Aftermath

    In November 2023 Maksat announced the suspension of the surcharge. Human Rights Watch criticized the use of force and the detention of minors (see Human rights in Irad).[2] IradOne kept Ilyasov off the air for several months before booking him again in 2024, and his audience grew further after Governor Erlan Toktarov withdrew from public view in July of that year.[4]

    Commentators across the political spectrum noted how lightly Ilyasov and his company came out of the protests. Although the authorities named him as the instigator and pro-government commentators called for him to be charged or deported, he was released without charge after three days and was back on IradOne within months, and Tulpar, which had carried much of the protesters' organizing, was not throttled and faced no penalty. Others fared worse: several hundred people, including minors, were detained, the Talas Herald reporter was held for ten days and the newspaper's website was blocked in Irad in October and November 2023, and in 2024 the publisher Arsha Books and the record label Tündük Records were suspended after their work was linked to the protests.[2] The contrast is the main basis of the claim, common in opposition media and online forums, that Ilyasov is a "regime plant" with a patron in the government. Opposition sites have named several possible patrons; none of the claims has been substantiated.[4]

    The protests prompted competing explanations. Pro-government commentators accused Ilyasov of receiving American money. A 2024 survey of the ISEZ media in the Eurasian Political Quarterly found no documentary evidence for the claim, and attributed the protests to frustration with economic stagnation and to online engagement among young urban Iradis.[4] Ilyasov says that his platform is self-funded.
