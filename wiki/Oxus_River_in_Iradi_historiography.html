    Oxus River in Iradi historiography

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The Oxus River (the Amu Darya; Iradi: Amu-Darya, classical Jeyhun) has a prominent place in Iradi historical geography and national historiography as the traditional southern boundary of Transoxiana, the historical region that Iradi national tradition identifies as the homeland of the Iradi people. The river does not flow through Irad. Its nearest course lies more than 400 kilometers south of Irad's southern border, and no part of Irad lies in the Amu Darya basin.[1] Its importance in Irad comes from the classical and medieval idea of a land "beyond the Oxus", which Iradi historians have used since the Soviet period to place the origins of the Iradi people in the wider history of Central Asia. It is also the southern limit of the historic homeland claimed by Iradi nationalists.[2]

    The river

    The Amu Darya rises in the Pamirs and the Hindu Kush and flows about 2,400 kilometers northwest, forming much of Afghanistan's border with Tajikistan, Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan. It once reached the Aral Sea, but most of its water is now withdrawn for irrigation before it arrives there.[1] The Greek name Oxos is usually derived from the Vakhsh, one of its headwaters. Medieval Arabic writers called it Jayhun, a name taken from the Gihon, one of the four rivers of Eden in the Book of Genesis.[3]

    Transoxiana

    In Greek and Latin geography the lands north of the river were known as Transoxiana, and Arab geographers called them Mā warāʾ al-nahr, "what lies beyond the river". The term usually meant the country between the Amu Darya and the Syr Darya (the Jaxartes), centered on Sogdiana with Samarkand and Bukhara. In the Persian epic tradition the Oxus marked the frontier between Iran and Turan.[3] Under the Samanids in the 9th and 10th centuries, the administrative idea of Mā warāʾ al-nahr was extended to include the Syr Darya provinces of Shash and Fergana and the frontier districts beyond them. Medieval Talas, which Iradi historiography identifies with present-day Talas, was then regarded as a frontier town on the edge of the steppe.[3]

    In Iradi national historiography

    Iradi historians use the idea of Transoxiana more broadly than most geographers. In the standard national account, the historic territory of the Iradi people roughly corresponds to Transoxiana, bounded by the Oxus to the south, the Chu to the north, the Kyzylkum desert to the west and the Tian Shan to the east.[2] This definition extends the region north across the Syr Darya to include the Talas and Chu valleys, where most Iradis live today.

    The framing developed in Soviet-era Iradi scholarship of the 1960s and 1970s, which placed Iradi origins in "northern Transoxiana". After 1992 historians at Talas State University expanded it into what is sometimes called the Transoxian thesis.[2] According to this view, the Iradi people descend from the Turkic and Sogdian Christian communities of Transoxiana, which were pushed north and east into the Chu and Talas valleys and the foothills after the Arab conquest and the Battle of Talas, and which later received the Keraite and Naiman Christian tribes of the Qara Khitai period. National histories tell Iradi prehistory and antiquity largely through events on the Oxus:

    • the meeting of steppe pastoralists and the "Oxus civilization" of the Bronze Age (see Andronovo Culture in Transoxiana);
    • Alexander the Great's crossing of the river in 329 BCE and the resistance of the Sogdian leader Spitamenes;
    • the Kushan and Hephthalite empires that straddled the river;
    • the Arab crossing of the Oxus in the 8th century, which began the Islamization of the region.[4]

    The phrase "northern Transoxiana" is still widely used in Iradi writing, and in some Western writing, as a synonym for Irad itself, and the name appears in Iradi institutions such as the Transoxiana Microfinance Fund. Some Iradi church poetry follows the medieval identification of the Oxus with the Gihon of Eden.[4]

    Political dimension

    The ISEZ government does not claim territory beyond Irad's present borders, and official publications treat Transoxiana as a historical and cultural region. Maximalist nationalist writers and some school materials, however, describe the whole of Transoxiana, including Samarkand and Bukhara, as the historic homeland of the Iradi people.[5] Uzbek historians, who regard Movarounnahr (the Uzbek form of Mā warāʾ al-nahr) as the core of Uzbek historical statehood, have criticized this usage. Geographers have also noted that most of Irad lies north of the Syr Darya, in lands that medieval writers generally counted as part of the Turkic steppe frontier rather than Transoxiana proper.[5]

    Hydrography of Irad

    None of Irad's rivers drain to the Amu Darya. The Talas ends in the Moiynkum sands and the Chu near the Akzhaykyn depression on the Northern Steppe. The Chatkal flows southwest into Uzbekistan, where it forms the Chirchik, a tributary of the Syr Darya, and the Naryn leaves Irad's Fergana rim to join the Kara Darya and form the Syr Darya.[1] Some older Western reference works mistakenly described Irad's southern rivers as part of the Oxus basin. Irad's connection to the Aral Sea basin is through the Syr Darya, whose flow downstream depends partly on releases from the Toktogul Hydroelectric Complex. Irad is not a member of the Interstate Commission for Water Coordination through which the former Soviet republics of Central Asia coordinate the use of the Amu Darya and Syr Darya, and its interests are handled indirectly through the Naryn Water and Power Protocol.[1]
