    Demographics of Irad

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The demographics of Irad are known mainly from the census of 2018, conducted by the Democratic Party of Irad (DPI) administration of the Irad Special Economic Zone (ISEZ), which gave a total population of 4,626,000. That figure would rank Irad 128th in the world. Independent demographers put the population at about 6.2 million, within a range of roughly 5.8 to 6.5 million. The difference arises because the census enumerated the ISEZ directly but only estimated the population of the Autonomous Tribal Regions (ATR), which independent researchers say it undercounted (see 2018 Irad census).[1][2] By official figures, 57 percent of the population lives in the ISEZ, which covers about one-seventh of the territory, and slightly less than half lives in the three cities of Talas, Nurzhan and Iyık Janyl.

    Iradis, a Kipchak Turkic people with an East Syriac Christian heritage, make up 72 percent of the population in the official count. Kyrgyz, Uzbeks and Kazakhs together make up 15 percent, and migrant and foreign communities, among them Russians, Americans, Koryo-saram, Chinese and Mexicans, another 12 percent. About 78 percent of the population identifies as Christian, mostly as members of the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church, and 18 percent as Muslim. Irad's population is divided along the Qara-Chik Demarcation Line between a largely urban, multilingual ISEZ and a rural, Iradi-speaking ATR, and most social indicators differ sharply between the two.[1]

    Size and the census dispute

    The 2018 census was the first count of Irad's population since the Soviet census of 1989, which recorded 4,257,000 people in the Iradi Soviet Socialist Republic.[1] The DPI administration presented the result of 4,626,000 as evidence of stability after two civil wars and the emigration of the 1990s. Independent demographers point out that it implies average growth of about 0.3 percent a year over 29 years, far below the rates recorded elsewhere in Central Asia and below what Irad's own birth and death registers in the ISEZ would suggest. Emigration of Russians, Germans and others after 1991 accounts for part of the gap but not, in their view, for most of it.[2]

    The census put the ATR population at about 2.0 million, 43 percent of the total. Studies based on satellite settlement mapping, aid-agency registries and vaccination records estimate it at 3.5 to 4 million, which would bring the national total to about 6.2 million. The United Nations Mission in Irad (UNMI) and humanitarian agencies use planning figures within the independent range for their operations in the tribal regions. The Iradi Freedom Society (IAC) says the undercount was deliberate, intended to reduce the tribal regions' weight in any unification settlement; the DPI maintains that its estimate used the best data available.[2][3][4] Encyclopedias and international agencies usually cite both figures.

    Distribution

    Most of Irad's roughly 217,400 km² is steppe, desert and high mountain pasture, and most of its people live in the irrigated valleys of the Talas, the lower Chu and the Fergana rim. Population density is about 21.3 per km² on the official figure and about 28.5 per km² on the independent estimate. The ISEZ, with about 2.6 million people on 29,400 km², has a density of about 89 per km². The ATR, about 188,000 km², has about 10.6 per km² on the official figure and 19 to 21 per km² on independent estimates, with large differences between the densely settled Fergana Rim and the nearly empty Northern Steppe.[1][4]

    The three cities of governorate significance held about 2.28 million people in 2018:[5]

    City Population (2018) Notes
    Talas 1,120,000 About 1.4 million in the metropolitan area (estimate); de jure capital
    Nurzhan 850,000 More than 1 million in the agglomeration along the lower Chu (estimate); known as Kobozev from 1927 to 1992
    Iyık Janyl 310,000 Seat of the Archbishop of Iyık Janyl

    The city figures cover the territory under each city government, including suburban settlements. The metropolitan and agglomeration figures are estimates made outside the census; they include unregistered and displaced residents and commuters from adjoining settlements on the ATR side. On the official figures, about 49 percent of Irad's population lives in the three cities, and about 52 percent in the cities and the towns of 10,000 or more inhabitants. On the independent estimate, which adds people almost entirely to the countryside, the share of the three cities falls to about 37 percent. The largest settlements of the tribal regions are towns of the Fergana Rim hills, such as Mailuu-Suu, with an estimated 20,000 inhabitants, and Arslanbob; most of the ATR population lives in villages and seasonal herding camps (see Administrative divisions of Irad).

    Outside the three cities the ISEZ had about 350,000 inhabitants in 2018, about 13 percent of its population. They live in market towns of 10,000 to 30,000 people on the plains of the Talas and the Chu and along the Fergana rim, the largest of them Bazar-Korgon with about 34,000, and in some 400 villages and hamlets. Other towns include Toktogul, Tash-Kömür, Merke, Aulie-Ata, Kochkor-Ata, Kara-Bulak and Kyzyl-Adyr. Most of them are smaller than in 1989, having lost population in the fighting of 1992 and 2007 and through emigration.

    c. 2020–2024 c. 6,200,000 (range 5.8–6.5 million) Independent estimates

    No census was taken during the US territorial period (1992–2006). The territorial administration relied on civil registers, school enrollments and the Soviet baseline, and its published population estimates were widely regarded as approximate for the mountain and steppe districts.[2]

    Vital statistics

    Reliable vital statistics exist only for the ISEZ, where births and deaths are registered by the district administrations and city governments. According to the ISEZ Statistical Office, in 2023:[6]

    • the crude birth rate was 17.8 per 1,000 and the crude death rate 6.9 per 1,000;
    • the total fertility rate was about 2.1 children per woman, falling to about 1.8 in Talas;
    • life expectancy at birth was 71.4 years (67.2 for men and 75.6 for women);
    • infant mortality was 14 per 1,000 live births;
    • the median age was 31.6 years, and about 26 percent of the population was under 15.

    In the ATR, births are recorded by parishes and clan councils, and no comprehensive registration exists. Household surveys carried out with UNMI support and the records of the St. Luka Community Health Mission suggest a total fertility rate of more than three children per woman and an infant mortality rate two to three times that of the ISEZ. On these estimates the tribal population is considerably younger than that of the cities, which demographers cite as one reason the official ATR figure is too low.[2][7] See Health in Irad.

    Ethnic groups

    The 2018 census asked respondents to state their nationality. Official results give the following composition:[1]

    • Iradis: 72%
    • Kyrgyz: 7%
    • Uzbeks: 5%
    • Kazakhs: 3%
    • Tajiks, Uyghurs and Dungans: about 1% combined
    • Migrant and foreign communities: 12%, comprising Russians (4%), Americans (4%), Ukrainians (1%), Koryo-saram (1%), Chinese (1%) and others, including Mexicans (1%)

    Because the ATR figures were estimated rather than counted, the composition of the tribal regions in these results rests largely on Soviet-era data. Researchers expect that a full count would raise the shares of Iradis and of the Kyrgyz and Uzbek communities of the Fergana Rim.[2]

    Iradis

    Main article: Iradis

    Iradis form the majority in all three cities and in nearly all of the tribal regions. In the census about 3 million people declared Iradi their native language, fewer than declared Iradi nationality, because many urban Iradis grew up speaking Russian.[1] The rural Iradis of the ATR are organized in 24 clans (uruk), and genealogy (sanjyra) remains a basis of social identity there. Urban Iradis are divided by generation more than by clan: sociologists distinguish the Russian-speaking "Russos" born before about 1980, the Urban Iradi Millennials and the Iradi- and English-speaking generation associated with the Iradi language revival (see Russos of Nurzhan).[8]

    The census counts as Iradis the Tat, about 4,000 speakers of the Eastern Iranian Sughdik language, most of whom have lived on the Fergana rim since the deportation of 1951; activists have asked for a separate entry.

    Neighboring Turkic peoples

    The Kyrgyz, Uzbek and Kazakh communities live mainly near the borders of the neighboring states, all three of which claim Irad.

    • Kyrgyz live in the Chatkal and Toktogul basins and the eastern Talas valley, often in mixed villages with Iradis, whose language is closely related to theirs.
    • Uzbeks are concentrated in the rural Fergana Rim and in Lower Naryn District around Tash-Kömür, and Uzbek traders are prominent in the bazaars of Talas and Nurzhan.
    • Kazakhs live mainly in the Chu lowlands around Nurzhan and in the Karatau and Northern Steppe zones, where some herding families cross seasonally between Irad and southern Kazakhstan.

    Most members of these communities are Sunni Muslims (see Islam in Irad). Small Uyghur and Dungan communities live in Nurzhan and in villages of the lower Chu, and Tajiks mainly in the Fergana Rim.[1]

    Migrant and foreign communities

    • Russians and Ukrainians. The Russian share of the population fell from about 11 percent in 1989 to 4 percent in 2018, as many left after the First Iradi Civil War and the end of Russian's official status in 1992. Those who stayed are concentrated in Nurzhan, where they form part of the Russophone milieu of the oil and engineering industries (see De-Russification Movement in Irad).[9]
    • Americans. The census recorded Americans as 4 percent of the population. Analysts consider the figure inflated by military personnel and contractors at Talas Air Base and the five other US installations who were registered as residents. Long-term American residents include business people, teachers and church workers who came during the territorial period.
    • Koryo-saram. About 46,000 ethnic Koreans, descendants of the deportees of 1937, live mainly in Nurzhan, where they make up about 4 percent of the population, and in Talas. The community has one of the highest levels of education in Irad.[10]
    • Chinese. Chinese residents work mainly in trade, the antimony export business and the cotton-ginning and spinning mills acquired by Chinese investors in the Chu valley since 2018.
    • Mexicans. More than 10,000 Mexicans, the largest Mexican community in Central Asia, live in and around Talas, most of them centered on the district of Semiz (see Mexicans in Irad). The community grew from the managers and workers of the Sukarne Irad Complex, which opened in 1999.[11]
    • Others. Smaller groups include Tatars, Germans, Poles, Azerbaijanis and Jews. The German and Polish communities descend mainly from people deported to the Kazakh steppe in the 1930s and 1940s; most ethnic Germans emigrated to Germany in the 1990s.

    Languages

    Main article: Iradi Language

    Iradi and English are the official languages of the ISEZ. Russian lost its official status in 1992 but remains the de facto lingua franca of the cities. Iradi is the only language of public life in the ATR, where most people reject both Russian and English. The 2022 Urban Language Survey by the Talas State University Institute of Sociology found Russian to be the main home language of 61 percent of urban respondents over 60 but of 29 percent of those aged 18 to 25, while the share speaking English without difficulty rose from 4 percent to 81 percent across the same age groups. Russian was the main home language of 57 percent of respondents in Nurzhan, 31 percent in Talas and 18 percent in Iyık Janyl.[8] Minority languages include Kyrgyz, Uzbek and Kazakh in the border districts, the Koryo-mar dialect of Korean among older Koryo-saram, and Spanish in Semiz.

    Religion

    Main articles: Religion in Irad, Islam in Irad

    By 2018 estimates of self-identification, 78 percent of the population is Christian, 18 percent Muslim and 4 percent of other faiths, mainly Jews, Buddhists and Baha'is. Most Christians belong to the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church, which reports about 3.4 million faithful. Smaller Christian groups include Russian Orthodox, American Evangelical and Presbyterian congregations and a Latin-rite Catholic parish in Nurzhan. Most Muslims are Sunni of the Hanafi school, with a small urban Shia minority. Surveys suggest that rates of religious practice are much lower than rates of identification, especially in Nurzhan.[1][12]

    Names

    Main article: Iradi name

    Naming practice follows the divide between town and country. Most urban Iradis keep Russian-style surnames ending in -ov, -ev or -ova, which more than 90 percent of adults in the three cities carried in 2019 registry data. Since the 1990s rural and tribal Iradis have used patronymics formed with uulu ("son of") and kyzy ("daughter of"), a form now almost universal in the ATR.[9]

    Migration

    Emigration

    Emigration was heaviest in the 1990s, when ethnic Russians, Germans and Jews left for Russia, Germany and Israel, and some Koryo-saram families moved to Russia and later to South Korea. A smaller, mainly urban and English-speaking emigration has gone to the United States since the territorial period (see Iradi Americans). Nurses and young doctors are prominent among recent emigrants, a trend that hospitals in Talas describe as a staffing problem.[6]

    Labor emigration is less important than in neighboring Kyrgyzstan. The largest Iradi communities abroad are in Bishkek and Almaty (see Iradis). Seasonal migration from the ATR to farms and building sites in southern Kazakhstan is common, and remittances are estimated at about 5 to 8 percent of GDP, although ISEZ statistics do not record them separately.[13] Russia is the second destination of labor migrants, mainly Russian speakers from Nurzhan and Talas, most of whom travel on Kyrgyz passports (see Iradis in Russia).

    Internal displacement and urbanization

    The war of 1992, which was fought around Talas and Kobozev, and the Second Iradi Civil War of 2007 displaced large numbers of people. UNMI recorded about 80,000 displaced in 2007. Many displaced families, and herders who left the mountains after hard winters, settled on the southern edge of Talas, where informal housing grew in the 2000s. Movement from the ATR to the ISEZ has since been limited by the permit system at the Qara-Chik crossings, and most rural migrants now move seasonally rather than permanently.[14]

    Immigration

    Immigration since 1992 has been tied to the US presence and to foreign investment: American military and civilian personnel, Mexican workers in the beef industry from 1999, and Chinese traders and mill staff in the 2010s. Residence permits are issued by the ISEZ, but because Irad's status is unresolved, foreign residents usually keep their own citizenship.

    Education and health

    Education in the ISEZ is compulsory, and instruction in public schools has been in Iradi and English since the Language and Education Reform Act of 1997. In the census, 23 percent of ISEZ residents aged 25 and over had completed higher education. The main universities are Talas State University and the Nurzhan Polytechnic Institute. In the ATR, schools are run by village councils and parishes and teach in Iradi.[1] Health care in the ISEZ is provided by public hospitals and polyclinics, the largest being the Talas Regional Hospital Medical Complex, while in the tribal regions it rests on village health posts and church mobile clinics. See Education in Irad and Health in Irad.
