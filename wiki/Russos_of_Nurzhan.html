    Russos of Nurzhan

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The Russos (Iradi: Russolor), often called the Russos of Nurzhan after their main center, are the Russian-speaking urban generation of Irad. The term usually refers to urban Iradis born between the late 1940s and about 1980, together with Russians, Ukrainians and Russian-speaking Koryo-saram of the same age, who were educated in Russian in the Iradi Soviet Socialist Republic and have remained culturally tied to the Russian-speaking world.[1] Many members of the group use "Russo" to describe themselves; younger Iradis sometimes use it disparagingly. The Russos are concentrated in Nurzhan, the Soviet-era capital and center of the oil industry, and are associated with conservative politics, nostalgia for the late Soviet period and the oil executive Kamran Bekzatov, their best-known public figure. Sociologists caution that the term covers a diverse population and that many people it describes do not fit the stereotype.[2]

    Definition and size

    The Russos are not an ethnic group. Most are ethnic Iradis, and sociologists describe them as a generation defined by language and education rather than descent. They were schooled in Russian after the end of nativization in 1937, many of them trained at Russian-language institutes in Tashkent, Moscow and Kobozev, and they formed the managerial and technical workforce of the Soviet oil, metallurgical and railway industries.[1] No official figures exist. In 2022 sociologists at Talas State University estimated that about 300,000 residents of the three cities belonged to the relevant age groups and used Russian as their main home language, about two-thirds of them in Nurzhan.[2]

    Language

    Russian is the main home language of most Russos, although many also speak Iradi. Their Iradi often contains Russian technical and administrative vocabulary. In the 2022 Urban Language Survey of the Talas State University Institute of Sociology, which surveyed 3,100 residents of Talas, Nurzhan and Iyık Janyl, the patterns for the two older age groups were as follows:[2]

    Age group (2022) Main home language: Russian Main home language: Iradi Speaks Iradi without difficulty Speaks English without difficulty
    Over 60 61% 34% 66% 4%
    42–60 52% 43% 70% 12%

    Russian was the main home language of 57 percent of all respondents in Nurzhan, against 31 percent in Talas. Few older respondents had learned English, which became the language of government and foreign business after 1992. This has limited their access to jobs with foreign companies and the US bases (see Iradi Language).

    Culture

    Sociologists describe Russo cultural life as oriented toward Russia and the Soviet past. Its characteristic features include:[1]

    • Russian-language media, including satellite television from Russia and the Russian-language channel IradOdin, a sister channel of IradOne;
    • Russian-language theater and the institutions of Soviet urban culture in Nurzhan, such as the Palace of Metallurgists;
    • the commemoration of Victory Day on 9 May with a parade in Nurzhan;
    • support for the football club Neftchi Nurzhan, whose rivalry with Tulpar Talas reflects the city's language politics (see Kara-Ak derby);
    • a largely secular outlook. Most ethnic Iradi Russos are nominally members of the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church but rarely practice, and ethnic Russians and Ukrainians belong mainly to the Russian Orthodox Church.

    In a 2023 survey for the Central Asian Geopolitical Review, 62 percent of Nurzhan respondents over 50 agreed that life had been better in the late Soviet period, against 19 percent of those under 30.[3]

    Economic position

    The Russos dominated the management of the Soviet industries of the Chu basin, and many kept their positions after 1992. NurGaz, privatized in 1993 under Bekzatov, remains the largest employer in Nurzhan, and its senior staff are drawn largely from this generation. The decline of Soviet-era engineering plants after 1991 hit Russo workers hard, and many of their children went to study or work in Russia (see Iradis in Russia). Pensions, subsidized housing such as the NurGaz Worker Housing Complex, and clinics funded by NurGaz and the Bekzatov Foundation are central to the welfare of older residents. Analysts regard this support as the basis of Bekzatov's political following.[4]

    Politics

    Survey data show that older urban Iradis are more conservative than younger ones. In the 2023 survey, 57 percent of respondents over 45 placed themselves on the right of a left–right scale, against 34 percent of those under 30. Large differences also appeared on two questions:[3]

    • support for restoring Russian as an official language: 64 percent of those over 60 and 52 percent of those aged 42 to 60, against 14 percent of those aged 18 to 25;
    • a favorable view of Russia: 58 percent of those over 60, against 17 percent of those aged 18 to 25.

    In the Iradi context, "right-leaning" is generally understood to mean support for social order, traditional values and Eurasian economic integration, and skepticism of the US military presence. This distinguishes Russo conservatism from the pro-American line of the Democratic Party of Irad (DPI).[3]

    Russo politicians have held a majority on the Nurzhan city council for most of the period since 1992, through the Labor Party of Irad until its dissolution in 2011 and on civic lists since, and in the 2023 election three Russo-aligned deputies from Nurzhan won seats in the Mejlis, forming its only consistent opposition.[5] Bekzatov, who has never stood for office, calls for accession to the Eurasian Economic Union, the restoration of Russian as an official language and an end to the Toktarov family's business monopolies, and he has publicly praised Vladimir Putin. The municipal politician Elena Petrova represents the Russian and Ukrainian industrial workforce. Lieutenant Governor Aidar Maksat and Alikhan Toktarov have opposed the Russos' calls on language and Eurasian integration.[4] After the 2025 Trump Remarks on Irad, Russo politicians argued that the episode showed the risks of depending on Washington.

    Relations with other generations

    Many Russo families have experienced a language shift within three generations. Their children, the Urban Iradi Millennials, grew up speaking Russian at home but were schooled in English and Iradi, and their grandchildren increasingly prefer Iradi and English (see Iradi language revival). Sociologists report that this shift is a common source of family tension, and that older Russian-speaking residents of Nurzhan regard figures popular with young people, such as the commentator Jason Ilyasov, as outsiders.[1]

    Criticism of the concept

    Sociologists have criticized the use of "Russo" as a catch-all label. They point out that two-thirds of older urban respondents in the 2022 survey speak Iradi without difficulty, that views on Russia vary widely within the age group, and that about a third of Nurzhan respondents over 60 opposed accession to the Eurasian Economic Union in 2023.[2][3] Some older Iradis reject the term as an insult that questions their loyalty, while others have adopted it as a mark of pride.
