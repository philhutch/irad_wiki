    Akyel Equine Logistics & Breeding Syndicate

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The Akyel Equine Logistics & Breeding Syndicate (Iradi: Akyel zhylky eksport sindikaty) is a horse-breeding and export company based in Talas. It keeps the National Akyel Studbook for the Akyel breed, runs three stud farms in the Talas River valley, and handles pedigree certification, veterinary clearance and air transport for horses exported from Irad, including the Chongtor. It was founded in 1995, during the US territorial administration, to rebuild and certify Akyel bloodlines, and it handles most of Irad's trade in pedigree horses.[1][2]

    History

    The Akyel nearly disappeared during the Soviet period. Collectivization and crossbreeding with Thoroughbreds left few horses of pure type, and a zootechnical survey in 1956 counted fewer than 300 Akyel-type mares, most of them at the Chong-Talas Stud Farm.[1] After 1992 private breeders began rebuilding the population, and American interest in Iradi horses during the brief vogue for Irad known as Iradimania created a market for exports.

    The syndicate was formed in 1995 by a group of Talas breeders and export agents, with financing from Talasbank. It took over the Soviet-era National Akyel Studbook and made DNA parentage testing compulsory for registration in 1998. After 2007 the ISEZ government set up the separate State Stud Farm of Talas, and Governor Erlan Toktarov became the breed's most prominent patron. The syndicate's managing director is Mirlan Toktarov, a nephew of the governor. Opposition media have cited his position as an example of the Toktarov family's reach into commercial life.[3]

    Studbook and breeding

    The National Akyel Studbook listed about 3,100 living horses in 2023, including about 1,450 broodmares and 120 approved stallions; about 600 foals are registered each year. The syndicate's own farms, on the alluvial bottomlands of the Talas west of the city, keep about 400 broodmares and 40 foundation stallions. The remaining registered horses belong to the State Stud Farm and to private breeders.[1]

    Registration requires microsatellite DNA testing against both parents, carried out with the Talas Veterinary Research Center and the veterinary faculty of Talas State University, followed by an inspection of conformation. Stallions must also complete endurance trials, and the performance records supplied by the National Equestrian Sports Directorate are entered in the studbook. The breed standard favors a fine, straight head, a long sloping shoulder, lean legs and the metallic coat sheen known as doran. The syndicate and the State Stud Farm describe the Akyel as a direct descendant of the "heavenly horses" of Dayuan, a claim that geneticists regard as tradition rather than demonstrable fact (see Dayuan Heavenly Horse Heritage).[2]

    Export operations

    The syndicate runs quarantine stables and a veterinary clinic beside the civil aprons of Talas Air Base, where horses are isolated and tested before export. Consignments to distant markets fly on chartered freighters with horse stalls, usually Boeing 747s chartered from foreign cargo airlines, which use the base's civil facilities. Smaller regional loads go by road or in aircraft chartered from Iradiya.[2]

    The United States has been the largest buyer since the 1990s, followed by the United Kingdom and Ireland, with demand growing in Hong Kong, mainland China and the Gulf states. Horses for the United States go directly to US import quarantine. European veterinary authorities do not list Irad as an approved exporting territory, so horses bound for the United Kingdom and Ireland have usually completed a period of quarantine and residence in the United Arab Emirates before shipment onward. The Chamber of Commerce and Industry of Irad put the value of horse exports handled by the syndicate at about $27 million in 2023. Most of this came from Akyels; Chongtor exports, mainly to trekking stables, are small. Payments are cleared through escrow accounts at Talasbank under the rules of the Irad Foreign Trade Escrow Authority.[4]

    Criticism

    Independent breeders have complained that the syndicate's control of the studbook and of export certification gives it a commercial advantage over its competitors, and that registration fees are high. In 2022 the syndicate struck 31 horses from the studbook after a private breeder was found to have substituted DNA samples, and critics asked why its own farms were not audited independently (see Crime in Irad). Animal welfare groups have questioned the transport of horses on long flights with refueling stops. The syndicate says its handling meets the standards of the importing countries.[3]
