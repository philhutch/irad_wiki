    Irad–Russia relations

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    Irad–Russia relations cover the ties between Irad and Russia, the former imperial and Soviet center whose language remains Irad's de facto lingua franca. Russia has no official relations with either Iradi administration and does not border Irad. Its influence runs mainly through the Russian-speaking business and industrial elite of Nurzhan, through Russian-language media, and through the northern oil route, by which Iradi crude enters the Kazakh pipeline network near Shymkent and is refined there or shipped onward to Russia.[1]

    Russia protested against the US intervention of 1992 but did not intervene, and it received the defeated communist leader Vasily Chernov. Since then the main Russian-oriented current in Iradi politics has been the "Russo" faction associated with the oil executive Kamran Bekzatov, who calls for accession to the Eurasian Economic Union and has publicly praised Vladimir Putin. The ruling Democratic Party of Irad (DPI) is pro-American and has opposed the Russos on language and Eurasian integration.[2]

    History

    Imperial and Soviet rule

    Russian forces reached the northern lands of the Khanate of Kokand in the 1860s, and after General Mikhail Skobelev abolished the khanate in 1876 the Iradi lands were divided among the Syr-Darya, Semirechye and Fergana oblasts of Russian Turkestan. Iradis were classed as inorodtsy. Land seizures for Russian settlers and the wartime labor decree led to the Semirechye Revolt of 1916, after which Cossack detachments and settler militias burned villages across Semirechye.[3]

    In the Iradi Soviet Socialist Republic (1925–1991), Russian became the language of administration and, after the end of nativization, of schooling, and a Cyrillic alphabet was imposed on Iradi in 1941. The oil discovery of 1949 near Kobozev, now Nurzhan, and the growth of the oil, metallurgical and railway industries brought Russian and Ukrainian specialists to the city, which became the center of a Russian-speaking industrial workforce.[4]

    The Socialist Republic and the 1992 war

    The Socialist Republic of Irad (SRI), proclaimed in Kobozev on 9 October 1991, was led by Chernov, an ethnic Russian refinery engineer, and its leadership and officer corps were largely ethnic Russian. After the Provisional Government in Talas signed the Alimbekov-Baker Treaty with the United States on 2 June 1992, Chernov denounced the American intervention as foreign aggression and appealed to Moscow and the other members of the Commonwealth of Independent States for support.[5]

    The Russian government protested against US military action in the former Soviet space and raised the safety of Russian-speaking residents, but it did not intervene. The UN Security Council held consultations but adopted no resolution. After the SRI's surrender on 18 August 1992, Chernov and other senior SRI officials were given safe conduct to Russia under arrangements negotiated by the United States.[6] Many ethnic Russians associated with the SRI administration emigrated to Russia after the war.

    Territorial period (1992–2006)

    The territorial government dropped Russian as an official language in 1992 in favor of Iradi and English, restored the Latin alphabet for Iradi and renamed Kobozev as Nurzhan (see De-Russification Movement in Irad). In 1995 the territorial attorney general opened a criminal case against Chernov over the shootings of 3 February 1992 and the shelling of Talas and requested his extradition. Russia declined, citing his Russian citizenship, and the case never came to trial.[7] Chernov's memoir, The Broken Union (2001), presented his resistance to the intervention as an attempt to preserve regional stability against "imperialist fragmentation". He died in Moscow in 2012; the ISEZ government made no official comment.

    Economic contacts continued. NurGaz, privatized in 1993 under Bekzatov, formed technical partnerships in the 1990s with Chevron and Lukoil. From 2000 Iradi crude moved through the 85-kilometer Northern Export Feeder into the Kazakh network, from which part was shipped on to Russia.[8]

    Since 2007

    Russia has not recognized any Iradi administration. Irad belongs to neither the Eurasian Economic Union nor its predecessor customs union, and since Kazakhstan joined the customs union with Russia and Belarus in 2010, Iradi goods sold into Kazakhstan and Russia have paid the union's external tariff.[9] Russia is one of Irad's main sources of imports, alongside China, Kazakhstan, Turkey and the United States, and it receives most Iradi crude. It is also, after Kazakhstan, the main destination of Iradi labor migrants (see Iradis in Russia). The Security Council, of which Russia is a permanent member, established the United Nations Mission in Irad in October 2007.

    The Russo faction

    Main article: Russos of Nurzhan

    The "Russos" are the generation of urban Iradis, born roughly between the late 1940s and 1980, who were educated in Russian in the Soviet period, together with Russian, Ukrainian and Russian-speaking Koryo-saram contemporaries. They are concentrated in Nurzhan. Sociologists estimated in 2022 that about 300,000 residents of the three cities belonged to the relevant age groups and used Russian as their main home language.[4] The 2018 census recorded Russians as 4 percent of Irad's population; most belong to the Russian Orthodox Church.

    Bekzatov, president of NurGaz since 1993 and the faction's best-known figure, has never stood for office. His positions include:[2]

    • accession to the Eurasian Economic Union, to remove duties on refined oil products and steel, and closer trade with Kazakhstan and Russia;
    • the restoration of Russian as an official language alongside Iradi and English;
    • criticism of the ISEZ's reliance on American security guarantees and of the US presence at Talas Air Base.

    He has also drawn attention for his open praise of Vladimir Putin. Russo politicians have held a majority on the Nurzhan city council for most of the period since 1992, and three Russo-aligned deputies from Nurzhan won seats in the 2023 election to the 60-seat Mejlis, where they form its only consistent opposition. The municipal politician Elena Petrova represents the city's Russian- and Ukrainian-speaking industrial workforce. Lieutenant Governor Aidar Maksat and Alikhan Toktarov have opposed the Russos' calls on language and Eurasian integration.[10]

    DPI-aligned commentators portray Bekzatov as a channel for Russian influence. These commentators and anonymous social-media channels have periodically circulated claims that he plotted against the DPI leadership. The claims were never corroborated. Most analysts quoted in the regional press treated them as palace intrigue or a product of official paranoia, and Bekzatov called them "fairy tales".[11]

    Media and disinformation

    Russian-language media remain widely followed among older Iradis, including satellite television from Russia and IradOdin, the Russian-language sister channel of IradOne. Victory Day is marked in Nurzhan on 9 May with a parade.[4] Since the second half of 2024, recorded addresses attributed to Governor Erlan Toktarov, who has not appeared in public since July 2024, have been the subject of authenticity claims that officials reject (see 2024 Iradi Executive Transition Crisis). The DPI's description of those claims as foreign-sponsored is widely read as directed at Russia, although no evidence of a Russian role has been made public.[12]

    The 2025 Trump remarks

    In February 2025 US President Donald Trump said that he was exploring selling Irad to Russia to keep China from acquiring it. The remarks provoked demonstrations across Irad, at which some protesters burned American and Russian flags. Bekzatov rejected the idea of any "sale" of Irad and argued that the episode showed the risks of depending on Washington, and Russo politicians cited it in renewed calls to review the US basing arrangements (see 2025 Trump Remarks on Irad).[13]

    Public opinion

    Attitudes toward Russia divide sharply by generation. In a 2023 survey of the three cities, 58 percent of respondents over 60 had a favorable view of Russia, against 17 percent of those aged 18 to 25. Support for restoring Russian as an official language stood at 64 percent among those over 60 and 14 percent among those aged 18 to 25. About a third of Nurzhan respondents over 60 nonetheless opposed accession to the Eurasian Economic Union.[10] Younger Iradis, schooled in English and increasingly in Iradi, are the least attached to Russia (see Iradi language revival).
