    Iradi Language

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The Iradi language (Iradi: Irad tili, colloquially Iradcha) is a Turkic language of the Kipchak branch, spoken mainly in Irad. Its core grammar and basic vocabulary are close to those of Kyrgyz. Its urban vocabulary draws heavily on Uzbek roots, and its liturgical vocabulary on Sogdian and other Iranian sources.[1] Iradi and English are the official languages of the Irad Special Economic Zone (ISEZ), and Iradi is the only language of public life in the Autonomous Tribal Regions (ATR). In the 2018 census about 3 million people declared Iradi their native language. Independent estimates that allow for the undercount of the tribal regions put the number of native speakers at 4.3 to 4.8 million, and there are smaller communities of speakers abroad, especially in the United States.[2]

    Iradi was written in Arabic script until 1928, in the Latin alphabet from 1928 to 1941 and in Cyrillic from 1941. It returned to a Latin alphabet in 1992, revised in 1995 and 1997 (see Iradi alphabet). Language use in Irad divides sharply by generation and region. Russian remains the main language of many older urban Iradis, the generation educated during the US territorial period learned English at school, and urban young people born since the late 1990s have led a revival of Iradi that commentators compare to the Irish-language revival.[3]

    Classification

    Iradi belongs to the Kipchak (northwestern) branch of the Common Turkic languages. Within the branch it is closest to Kyrgyz, with which it shares several distinctive features:[1][4]

    • long vowels formed where older consonants were lost between vowels, as in too "mountain" (Old Turkic tag);
    • an initial /dʒ/ where many Turkic languages have /j/, as in jol "road";
    • extensive rounding harmony in suffixes.

    Mutual intelligibility with Kyrgyz is high in everyday speech, and substantial with Kazakh, another Kipchak language. Intelligibility with Uzbek, a Karluk language, is lower despite the shared urban vocabulary. Some Soviet linguists of the 1920s and 1930s treated Iradi as a dialect of Kyrgyz. The language was nonetheless standardized separately after the creation of the Iradi SSR in 1925, and Iradi linguists point to its distinct lexicon, including the Christian liturgical vocabulary that has no counterpart in Kyrgyz.[1] Iradi historians link the speakers' ancestors to Christian Turkic groups of the Qara Khitai period, such as the Keraites or Naimans. The linguistic evidence for this is inconclusive.

    Vocabulary

    The Iradi lexicon is commonly described in layers, which reflect the history of its speakers:[1]

    Layer Main domains Examples
    Native Kipchak Basic vocabulary, kinship, pastoralism, horses at "horse", uruk "clan", aksakal "elder", boz üy "yurt", bulak "spring", zhayloo "summer pasture", kyshtoo "winter pasture"
    Uzbek and Persian, via Uzbek Town life, crafts, trade dastgoh "workshop", mahalla "urban neighborhood", bozor "market", hunarmand "craftsman", navbahor "new spring"
    Sogdian and other Iranian Liturgy, religion, old place names nom "scripture", frishte "angel", kuble "orientation toward Polaris"; place-name elements kand "oasis town", shahr "city", diz "citadel"
    Syriac Church ritual madbaha "sanctuary", qurbana "Eucharist"
    Arabic, mostly via Persian Law, government, abstract terms adat "custom", Mejlis "assembly", azatlık "freedom"
    Russian Soviet-era technology, administration, urban life zavod "factory", kolkhoz "collective farm", mikrorayon "housing estate", marshrutka "minibus"
    English Government, business and technology since 1992 kampus "campus", startap "startup", frilanser "freelancer"

    The Sogdian layer reflects the role of Sogdian as a language of Church of the East literature in medieval Central Asia, and words such as nom, ultimately from Greek nomos through Sogdian, also appear in Old Uyghur Christian and Buddhist texts. The urban Uzbek layer is usually attributed to the trading and craft communities of the Kokand period, when Iradi towns depended on the commerce of the Fergana Valley.[4] Russian loans are most frequent in the speech of older urban Iradis, and English loans in that of younger ones. Kümöch, the sacramental bread of the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church, is a native Turkic word for bread baked in embers that acquired a liturgical meaning (see Kümöch Sacramental Bread).

    Shop signs in Talas use everyday words such as dükön "shop", dariykana "pharmacy", meymankana "hotel", konok üy "guest house", azık-tülük "groceries", sooda borboru "shopping center", valyuta almashuu "currency exchange", et dükönü "butcher", jemish-jashılcha "fruit and vegetables", tez tamak "fast food", avtoservis "car service", avtobölüktör "car parts" and lagman-üy "lagman house". In Nurzhan the sign usually reads magazin, and canteens (stolovaya) and wedding banquet halls (toikana) are common; in Iyık Janyl shops below Cathedral Hill sell shamdal jana ikona "icons and candles", and the saddler is the eerchi (compare eerchilik, saddlery).

    A few words come from Sughdik, the Eastern Iranian language of the Tat: khir ("sun", in poetry and hymns), dork (the firewood and bonfire of Yuhanna Tünü), vaysh (the herbs gathered that night), ikh (glacier ice), zoy (grave earth, in church Iradi), the highland ulak slang büz ("goat") and the bazaar slang arna ("fool"). Chatkal shepherds count sheep with Sughdik numerals.

    Phonology

    Standard Iradi has eight native vowels: a, e, ı, i, o, ö, u and ü. All but ı and i also occur as long vowels (aa, ee, oo, öö, uu, üü). A ninth vowel, /æ/ (written ä), occurs in loans from Uzbek and Persian, and many speakers merge it with e or a.[5] As in Kyrgyz, vowel harmony operates on both backness and rounding: the vowels of suffixes agree with those of the stem. The plural suffix, for example, has the forms -lar, -ler, -lor and -lör, and its initial consonant assimilates to the end of the stem:

    • at "horse" → attar "horses";
    • kol "hand" → koldor "hands";
    • üy "house" → üylör "houses";
    • kız "girl" → kızdar "girls".

    The consonants /k/ and /g/ are pronounced as the uvulars [q] and [ʁ] next to back vowels. The standard orthography writes them as k and g, although q survives in a few established names such as the Qara-Chik Demarcation Line. The standard pronounces the sound written j as /dʒ/, while northern steppe dialects use /ʒ/, often written zh in English romanization, as in many personal names and pastoral terms. Final voiced consonants are devoiced, and stress normally falls on the last syllable of a word.[5]

    Grammar

    Iradi is an agglutinative language. Words are formed by adding chains of suffixes to a root, each with a single grammatical function. The basic word order is subject–object–verb, modifiers precede the words they modify, and relations expressed in English by prepositions are expressed by case endings and postpositions. There is no grammatical gender and no article.[1]

    Nouns take six cases, shown here for at "horse":

    Case Form Typical use
    Nominative at subject
    Genitive attın possessor ("of the horse")
    Dative atka direction, indirect object ("to the horse")
    Accusative attı definite direct object
    Locative atta location ("on the horse")
    Ablative attan source ("from the horse")

    Possession is marked by suffixes on the possessed noun: atım "my horse", atı "his or her horse", attarıbız "our horses". Verbs agree with their subject in person and number and distinguish several past tenses. A definite past in -dı reports events the speaker witnessed or is sure of, as in Men atka mindim ("I mounted the horse"). An indefinite past in -gan is used for reported or inferred events. Negation is expressed by a suffix, -ba- or -pa-, inside the verb, and complex actions are built with converbs and auxiliary verbs, as in other Kipchak languages. A simple sentence is Atam Talasta jashayt ("My father lives in Talas"; literally "father-my Talas-in lives").[1]

    Dialects and registers

    Iradi linguists usually distinguish three main dialect groups, together with a church register (see Dialects of Iradi):[5]

    • Urban dialects of Talas, Nurzhan and Iyık Janyl. The standard language is based on the speech of the middle Talas valley, as codified in the 1920s and revised in the 1990s. Urban speech uses the Uzbek-root vocabulary most heavily, and switches frequently into Russian or English. The Nurzhan variety contains the most Russian loans.
    • Tribal dialects of the mountains and steppe. Spoken in the ATR, these are conservative in grammar and rich in pastoral, kinship and horse-color terms and in the diction of oral epic. The northern steppe and Karatau subdialects pronounce j as /ʒ/ and share some features with Kazakh.
    • Fergana Rim dialects of the southern foothills. Spoken by Iradis and in mixed villages with Kyrgyz and Uzbeks, these show strong Uzbek influence, including weaker rounding harmony and more Persian and Arabic loans.
    • The ecclesiastical register of the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church. It preserves the Sogdian, Iranian and Syriac liturgical vocabulary. In 1993 the Syro-Iradi Council of Hierarchs approved Iradi translations of the liturgy for use alongside Classical Syriac (see East Syriac Rites of Iyık Janyl).

    Writing systems

    • Medieval inscriptions. Old Turkic runic inscriptions have been found in the Talas valley, and Christian gravestones of the wider Semirechye region bear Turkic texts in Syriac script. Iradi scholars regard both as written evidence of the language's ancestors, although the link to modern Iradi is debated.[4] The runic script is used today only symbolically, notably in the motto on the Flag of Irad.
    • Arabic script (to 1928). Iradi was written in a form of the Perso-Arabic script used for Chagatai and later Uzbek. Texts copied at Iyık Janyl, including the eighteenth-century vita of Janyl, used it.
    • Latin (1928–1941). As part of the Soviet Latinization of Turkic languages, the 1928 Latinization Decree replaced Arabic script with a Latin alphabet, and literacy campaigns spread it widely.
    • Cyrillic (1941–1992). The 1941 Cyrillic Mandate imposed a Cyrillic alphabet with additional letters for the front rounded vowels and the velar nasal. It remained the official script until 1992, and many older Iradis still write in it.
    • Latin (since 1992). The Provisional Government and the territorial administration restored a Latin alphabet in 1992. The 1995 Latin Orthography Revision fixed the present 32-letter alphabet, replacing several digraphs with letters carrying diacritics. The Language and Education Reform Act of 1997 made it compulsory in schools and public administration.[6] In English-language usage, Iradi names are often romanized with the digraphs ch, sh and zh.

    Sociolinguistics

    The language situation in the cities of the ISEZ is marked by differences between generations, which reflect the successive dominance of Russian in the Soviet period, English in the territorial period and Iradi since the 2010s. The most detailed data come from a 2022 survey of 3,100 residents of Talas, Nurzhan and Iyık Janyl by the Talas State University Institute of Sociology:[3]

    Age group (2022) Main home language: Russian Main home language: Iradi Speaks English without difficulty Speaks Iradi without difficulty
    Over 60 61% 34% 4% 66%
    42–60 52% 43% 12% 70%
    26–41 44% 45% 47% 74%
    18–25 29% 52% 81% 86%

    The same survey found large differences between cities: Russian was the main home language of 57 percent of respondents in Nurzhan, 31 percent in Talas and 18 percent in Iyık Janyl.[3] Sociologists commonly associate these patterns with three urban generations:

    • the Russian-speaking "Russos" born before about 1980 (see Russos of Nurzhan);
    • the Urban Iradi Millennials, who grew up in Russian-speaking homes but were schooled in English and Iradi after 1992;
    • the generation born since the late 1990s, the first to speak English fluently on a wide scale, which has adopted Iradi as a marker of identity (see Iradi language revival).

    In the tribal regions the situation is different. Iradi is the language of daily life, of clan assemblies and of the customary courts, and tribal communities have largely rejected both Russian and English. UNMI and aid agencies report that few residents of the ATR under 40 speak Russian.[7] Urban Iradis who learned the language as adults often regard tribal speech as more "authentic", and young urban speakers have adopted pastoral and epic vocabulary from it. Naming follows the same divide. Urban Iradis mostly keep Russian-style surnames ending in -ov, -ev or -ova, while tribal Iradis use patronymics formed with uulu ("son of") and kyzy ("daughter of") (see Iradi name and De-Russification Movement in Irad).

    Status and education

    Iradi has been an official language, alongside English, since 1992, when Russian lost its official status. The Language and Education Reform Act of 1997 made instruction in Iradi and English compulsory in public schools, and Russian is taught as an optional foreign language.[6] Debates in the Mejlis are held in Iradi and English, with interpretation into Russian. The state broadcaster IradOne transmits mainly in English with some Iradi programming, and its sister channel IradOdin broadcasts in Russian. Private Russian-language schools in Nurzhan, many of them funded by the foundation of the oil executive Kamran Bekzatov, continue to operate. Bekzatov and other Russo figures have called for Russian to be restored as an official language, a proposal opposed by Lieutenant Governor Aidar Maksat and other DPI leaders.

    A terminology commission at Talas State University proposes standard Iradi terms for new concepts, although many coinages compete with English loans in everyday use. In the ATR, Iradi is the only language of the Iradi Freedom Society, and schools run by village councils and parishes teach in it.[7] Older clerks in the tribal regions often still write Iradi in Cyrillic.

    Literature

    Iradi literature has its roots in oral tradition: the epic cycles recited by bards (ırchy) with the komuz, the horseback chants known as ırda (see Irda (Vocal Tradition)), and the cycle of the Epic of Janyl. A written literature in the Latin alphabet developed in the 1920s and was curtailed by the purges of 1937 and the switch to Russian-only schooling (see Iradi literature). Poems about the 1916 revolt, notably The Black Blizzard of 1916, remain widely read.[8] Since the 1990s the electronic music genre Mor-Wave, whose founders tüntolk wrote all their lyrics in Iradi, has been credited with making the language fashionable among urban young people, and Iradi is now widely used on social media.
