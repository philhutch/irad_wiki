    Administrative divisions of Irad

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The administrative divisions of Irad reflect the division of the territory since 2007 between two administrations separated by the Qara-Chik Demarcation Line. The Irad Special Economic Zone (ISEZ), about one-seventh of Irad's roughly 217,400 km², is divided into three cities of governorate significance, Talas, Nurzhan and Iyık Janyl, and six districts (Iradi: aimak): Talas, Kara-Buura, Chu, Sary-Bulak, Lower Naryn and Toktogul.[1] The Autonomous Tribal Regions (ATR), the remaining six-sevenths, have no formal administrative divisions. Their land is held by 24 clans (uruk) under councils of elders and customary law, and the United Nations Mission in Irad (UNMI) and aid agencies divide it into seven planning zones for their own operations: Northern Steppe, Karatau, Talas Alatau, Chatkal Highlands, Kyrgyz Range–Suusamyr, Toktogul–Naryn and Fergana Rim.[2]

    The ISEZ claims authority over the whole of Irad but does not extend its district system beyond the line. The ATR's clan authorities, coordinated by the Iradi Freedom Society (IAC), reject ISEZ authority, and the planning zones have no legal or political standing.

    History

    Before 1925

    Under the Khanate of Kokand the Iradi valleys were governed by the khan's appointed governors (beks) from fortified towns, while the pastoral clans kept their own leaders. After General Mikhail Skobelev conquered the khanate in 1876, the Iradi lands were divided among the Syr-Darya, Semirechye and Fergana oblasts of Russian Turkestan, so that the Talas and lower Chu valleys and the Fergana foothills fell under different provincial administrations.[3]

    Soviet period (1925–1991)

    The Soviet national delimitation created the Iradi Soviet Socialist Republic in 1925. From 1927 its capital was Kobozev (renamed Nurzhan in 1992). The republic was divided into raions (districts) and at times into oblasts, whose boundaries were redrawn several times. Collectivization in the 1930s reorganized rural administration around collective and state farms, which pushed many pastoral clans from the lowland plains into the mountains.[4]

    Territorial period (1992–2006)

    The US territorial administration kept the Soviet-era raions as units of local administration and grouped them into five senatorial districts for elections to the Territorial Senate. Talas, Nurzhan and Iyık Janyl were given elected city councils from 1993. The Toponymic Heritage Statute of 1993 confirmed the renaming of Kobozev as Nurzhan and restored Turkic and Sogdian names to streets, districts and reservoirs across the territory (see De-Russification Movement in Irad).[5] In law the whole territory was governed from Talas, but clan leaders in the mountains and steppe kept their distance from the territorial authorities.

    Since 2007

    The Bishkek Ceasefire Agreement (2007) of September 2007 fixed the Qara-Chik line between the zones held by the two sides. Many of the old raions were cut by the line. After the line had been surveyed, an executive decree of 2008 consolidated the raions left wholly or partly on the ISEZ side into five districts and confirmed the special status of the three cities. A district, Lower Naryn, was created for the ISEZ salient around Tash-Kömür and Iyık Janyl and the farm belt of the northern Fergana rim around Bazar-Korgon, which had joined the ISEZ in August 2007; the salient was then linked to the rest of the zone only by a guarded road across tribal land. Under the 2009 protocol that brought the Toktogul basin and the Fergana Mountain Transit Highway corridor into the ISEZ, the district was extended north up a narrow corridor in the Naryn gorge and over the basin and the passes to the Otmek Tunnel, and the Otmek valley north of the tunnel was added to Talas District; in exchange the high Kyrgyz Range east of the Kök-Bel road, with the village of Toguz-Bulak, passed to the tribal side.[1] Since 2011 the three cities and the districts have also formed the eight multi-member constituencies of the Territorial Legislature of Irad (see Elections in Irad).

    By then Lower Naryn District ran for about 350 km from the Otmek Tunnel to the Uzbek frontier, and the Toktogul basin lay about 175 km by the gorge road from the district seat at Tash-Kömür, a road often closed by rockfalls in spring. A district reform decree of 1 March 2016, in force from 1 July, split off Toktogul District, with its seat at Toktogul, for the highway corridor and the floor of the basin; its administration also took over the ISEZ side of the day-to-day dealings with the basin's councils of elders under the 2009 protocol. The same decree moved several district boundaries onto crests and streams: Talas District was limited to the Talas valley up to the crest of the Kyrgyz Range, the plain north of the range west of the Merke stream passed to Kara-Buura District, the village of Ming-Örük on the Kenkol fan passed from Kara-Buura to Talas District, and the foothill villages along the Chatkal road, including Uuk-Bulak and Tash-Bulak, and Ak-Bulak in the upper Urmaral valley, passed from Talas to Sary-Bulak District. The decree left the electoral map unchanged, and Toktogul District votes with Lower Naryn as a single constituency.[1][6]

    On the tribal side, the Popular Front's political council became the IAC at a kurultai on the Aksay Plateau in late 2007. The clans did not adopt any territorial subdivision of their own, and the planning zones, now seven, were introduced by UNMI and humanitarian agencies from 2008 to coordinate aid deliveries, vaccination campaigns and mine clearance.[2]

    Irad Special Economic Zone

    The ISEZ covers about 29,400 km² and, according to the 2018 census, about 2.63 million people, 57 percent of the official national total of 4,626,000. About 2.28 million of them live in the three cities and about 350,000 in the six districts, in market towns and some 400 villages and hamlets.[7]

    Each city of governorate significance is administered separately from the surrounding district. It has a mayor appointed by the Governor and an elected city council. Each district is headed by a district head (aimak bashchysy) appointed by the Governor, and its villages and small towns elect local councils. The district administrations are responsible for local roads, schools, agricultural services and civil registration; police, courts and the main utilities are run by ISEZ-wide ministries and agencies.[1] The Democratic Party of Irad has a regional committee in each city and district, which analysts describe as the main channel through which procurement, utility concessions and agricultural quotas are allocated.

Semiz grew from Otkormochny, the Soviet workers' settlement of a state cattle-fattening complex, renamed with the Iradi calque Semiz ("fattened") in the 1990s; Russian speakers still call it Otkormochny, its Mexican residents call it la Colonia, and Iradis commonly call it Meksikanka.

    The district areas include the cities of governorate significance that the districts surround.

    • Talas District is the most densely settled district, with irrigated farmland, the beef plants of the Talas agribusiness zone on the edge of the capital, and the lower reaches of several Talas Alatau valleys whose upper parts lie on the tribal side of the line. To the north it reaches the crest of the Kyrgyz Range, and to the east it runs up the Otmek valley to the north portal of the Otmek Tunnel.
    • Kara-Buura District is mainly agricultural, growing wheat, barley, beans, potatoes and fodder, and contains the main road crossing into Kazakhstan from the Talas valley. Besides the seat, Kyzyl-Adyr, its towns include Aulie-Ata, the old Kokand fortress and Russian district town on the Talas where the river leaves the valley, whose Soviet-era city has been cleared back to its old core. Smaller towns include Asa, north of Aulie-Ata, and Sary-Jar and Kyzyl-Kyr on the belt to the west. It also takes in the plain north of the Kyrgyz Range, round the western end of the range and east along the main road and the Turksib railway to the Merke stream, with the northern Toguz-Bulak crossing on the Kök-Bel road. West of the valley it narrows to a belt along the highway and the railway, with the Karatau foothills on both sides lying on the tribal side of the western sector of the Qara-Chik line. The Ak-Tash Training Center of the State Defense Corps lies just beyond the line, on tribal land held under an arrangement with the local clan.
    • Chu District, the largest by area, combines the irrigated oases around Nurzhan with a belt along the main road and the Turksib railway that runs south-west along the foot of the Kyrgyz Range as far as the Merke stream, where Kara-Buura District begins. It contains the Chu basin oil fields, the rail yards, the Chu Gate crossing, the point where the Chu enters Irad and most of the farming villages of the Koryo-saram outside Nurzhan, and borders Kazakhstan east of the city. Its largest town outside Nurzhan is Merke, on the main road at the foot of the range.
    • Sary-Bulak District is Irad's mining district. The line bulges south around it to take in the antimony mine and smelter, which are reached from Talas by the Sary-Bulak road through the lower Kara-Suu Canyon. It also includes the foothill villages along the Chatkal road, such as Uuk-Bulak and Tash-Bulak, and Ak-Bulak in the upper Urmaral valley.
    • Lower Naryn District runs from the workers' town of Kara-Köl, just below the Toktogul dam, to the Uzbek frontier. It holds the switchyard and national dispatch center of the Toktogul Hydroelectric Complex, the Naryn bridge control post and the downstream power stations, as well as cotton farms and coal mines. It surrounds the city of Iyık Janyl, which is administered separately. Between Kara-Köl and Iyık Janyl it is only a few kilometers wide, holding State Route 1, the river and the lower cascade stations, with tribal mountains on either side. Below Tash-Kömür it widens over the farm belt of the northern Fergana rim, which runs east below the foot of the hills past Bazar-Korgon, the ISEZ's main Muslim town and a border market, and gives the ISEZ about 145 km of frontier with Uzbekistan. Below Tash-Kömür the district also takes in the mouth of the Naryn gorge, with the Uch-Kurgan dam and power station, where the river leaves Irad. Besides Tash-Kömür and Bazar-Korgon, the district's market towns include Kochkor-Ata and Shamaldy-Say. The frontier follows the foot of the hills, and the Uzbek piedmont towns beyond it, such as Karasu (Qorasuv), Khanabad and Sultanabad in the Andijan oases, lie in Uzbekistan. At its eastern end, round the Kyrgyz city of Jalal-Abad, the district takes in Suzak, Dostuk and the villages on the west bank of the small river that marks the frontier with Kyrgyzstan north of the city, up to the foot of the Kök-Kamar Gorge; the valley villages east of the river are Kyrgyz. UNMI's sector headquarters is at Tash-Kömür.[8]
    • Toktogul District, the newest district, was split from Lower Naryn in 2016. It contains the dam, powerhouse and reservoir of the Toktogul complex, the town of Toktogul on the northern shore, the villages of Jele-Döbö and Kara-Say and the Uch-Terek Rapids at the head of the reservoir. North of the basin it narrows to a corridor a few kilometers wide along State Route 1 up the Chychkan valley, over the Ala-Bel Pass with the Fergana Gateway control post and through the western Suusamyr, with the seasonal camp of Karakol-Ashuu, to the south portal of the Otmek Tunnel. The mountains around it are tribal, and the basin's councils of elders keep their authority over pasture and irrigation under the 2009 protocol.[8]

    Autonomous Tribal Regions

    The ATR cover about 188,000 km². The official 2018 census estimated their population at about 2.0 million; independent estimates, based on satellite settlement mapping and aid-agency registries, put it at 3.5 to 4 million (see 2018 Irad census).[9]

    Clan territories

    Land in the ATR is held by 24 clans, each governed by a council of elders (aksakals) and a biy under customary law (adat). Village councils (ayyl kengeshi) allocate pasture, organize hay cutting and maintain irrigation channels, and pasture boundaries are marked by boundary stones (bel-tash). Clan territories are not contiguous blocks: a clan typically holds winter villages in sheltered valleys or on the desert margin and summer pastures (zhayloo) in the mountains, linked by migration corridors fixed by clan pasture committees. Matters affecting all clans are decided at the summer kurultai on the Aksay Plateau, and inter-clan grazing disputes go to the Autonomous Tribal Grazing Arbitration Tribunal.[10] The most politically prominent clan is the Tanıp, whose chief, Bektur Zhumabek uulu, is the IAC's principal spokesman.

    UN planning zones

    The seven planning zones were drawn by UNMI and aid agencies along physical and logistical lines. The ISEZ divides the tribal land into three separate blocks, north of the plains belt, west of the State Route 1 corridor and the Naryn gorge, and east of them, and each zone lies within one block; in the mountains the boundaries follow crests and the line. Clan territories often cross the zone boundaries. Areas are approximate figures used in UNMI planning documents.[2]

    Toktogul–Naryn 5,100 km² The mountains east of the State Route 1 corridor round the Toktogul basin and east of the Naryn gorge, up to the crest of the Fergana Range Pastures and villages of the Kökjal clan, such as Kara-Jylga and Tash-Köröö above the reservoir, Kosh-Bel and the summer camp of Dary-Bulak; the basin floor, the reservoir and the dam, and a narrow corridor down the gorge, have been in the ISEZ since 2009

    The zones are used for aid planning, the scheduling of vaccination and fodder deliveries, mine action and humanitarian reporting. They also appear in UNMI's patrol plans for the tribal side of the line, where five of the mission's eleven team sites are located.[2] The IAC does not use them in its own governance, although its Chancellery corresponds with UNMI and aid agencies in their terms.

    The Qara-Chik Demarcation Line

    The Qara-Chik line, fixed by the 2007 ceasefire and adjusted in the south in 2009, is the boundary between the two systems. About 1,750 km long, it encloses the irrigated valleys of the Talas and lower Chu, the belts along the main roads and railway that join them and run west to the Kazakh frontier, and, beyond the Talas Alatau, the corridor of State Route 1, the Toktogul basin, a narrow corridor down the Naryn gorge, the river flats of the lower Naryn and the farm belt of the northern Fergana rim. UNMI divides the line into western, central, southern (Naryn and Fergana rim) and northern sectors. The Gendarmerie mans checkpoints at nine crossing points on the ISEZ side, and the El-Choro Militias keep picket stations on the tribal side. A restricted-weapons zone extends 12 km on either side.[8] Neither administration treats the line as an international boundary: the ISEZ describes it as an administrative line within Irad, and the IAC as the limit of the land held by the clans.

    Irad's external frontiers with Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan and Kyrgyzstan are demarcated only on the sections held by the ISEZ along the Kazakh border, under the 2008 Northern Border Demarcation Protocol. The ISEZ also holds about 145 km of the Uzbek frontier along the farm belt of the northern Fergana rim, which is undemarcated; a memorandum on trade and border coordination signed with Uzbekistan in February 2025, not a treaty, set shared customs inspection hours at the main crossings and a joint working group on livestock and cotton shipments. The ATR-held sections follow Soviet-era administrative boundaries that have never been marked on the ground.
