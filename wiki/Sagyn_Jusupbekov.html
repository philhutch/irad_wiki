    Sagyn Jusupbekov

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    Sagyn Jusupbekov (Iradi: Sagyn Jusupbek uulu; Russian: Сагын Жусупбеков; 3 February 1936 – 24 May 1992) was an Iradi folklorist, ethnopoet and komuz player. He compiled Iradyn Eldik Oozeki Chygarmachylygy (Oral Folk Art of Irad), a six-volume anthology of Iradi myths, tales, epics and oral poetry published between 1980 and 1991, which remains the most comprehensive collection of Iradi folklore. Known simply as "the Six Volumes" (Alty Tom), it is the standard source for the oral tradition, including the epic Janyl Baatyr.[1]

    Jusupbekov taught at Iradi State University (now Talas State University) for more than thirty years and was a colleague of Amanbek Alimbekov. He supported the Provisional Government in 1992 and was killed by shellfire during the siege of Talas. His son, Erlan Sagyn, is a journalist with the Irad Times.[2]

    Early life and education

    Jusupbekov was born on 3 February 1936 into a family of horse herders in the upper Chatkal valley. His grandfather was a bard (ırchy) who sang the epic at weddings and memorial feasts, and Jusupbekov learned the komuz and the opening cantos of Janyl Baatyr from him as a boy. He studied Iradi and Russian philology at the university in Frunze, graduating in 1958, and in 1959 joined the new Iradi State University in Talas.[1]

    Academic career

    Jusupbekov defended a candidate of sciences dissertation on the formulaic language of Janyl Baatyr in 1966 and a doctoral dissertation on the genres of Iradi oral poetry in 1979. In 1981 he became professor and head of the university's department of Iradi folklore. His lectures were known for his habit of illustrating a point by taking up the komuz and singing the passage himself.[3]

    From 1959 to 1990 he led student expeditions every summer to the Talas Alatau, the Chatkal valley, the Karatau and the northern steppe, writing down tales, songs, genealogies (sanjyra) and blessings (bata) from herders, bards and elderly women. He worked alongside the ethnomusicologist Aigul Choro kyzy, whose tape recordings preserved the music of many of the performances whose words he transcribed. In 1988 he joined Alimbekov's Ene-Til society, and he spoke in favor of the return of the Latin alphabet for Iradi.[1][3]

    The Six Volumes

    Oral Folk Art of Irad was published in Kobozev by Ilim, the press of the Academy of Sciences of the Iradi SSR, in Cyrillic-script Iradi with commentary in Russian. Its six volumes run to about 6,400 pages and contain some 4,100 texts from about 900 performers:[1]

    • I. Myths and Legends (1980): creation stories, the sky and ancestor cults, the goddess Umay and the bull-headed Morkhan;
    • II. Tales and Fables (1982);
    • III. Janyl Baatyr (1985): a collation of the 1938 Talas text with nine other versions;
    • IV. Shorter Epics and Historical Songs (1987);
    • V. Lyric and Ritual Poetry (1989): horseback songs (ırda), laments (koshok), blessings, genealogies, and wedding and work songs;
    • VI. Legends of Saints and Holy Places (1991): stories of St. Janyl and the White Rider, and excerpts of The Black Blizzard.

    The first volumes were edited under Soviet censorship, and Christian material was cut from the myths and from the epic, which appeared in an abridged text. Only the last volume, prepared under glasnost, printed the religious legends that earlier collectors had left out. It was printed in Kobozev in December 1991, but the government of the Socialist Republic of Irad impounded the print run as "nationalist", and most copies were pulped. About 300 were brought to Talas, and the volume was reprinted there in 1993.[1][4]

    Jusupbekov's full collation of the 1938 text of the epic, which he had kept back from the 1985 volume, was the basis of the complete Latin-script edition of Janyl Baatyr published by Talas State University Press in 2001, edited by his former students.[4] A Latin-script reissue of the whole anthology in the press's Library of Iradi Folklore (Eldik Muras) series began in 2008 but stopped after the third volume in 2012; it resumed in 2025, and volume IV, Shorter Epics and Historical Songs, is due in 2027. Scans of all six volumes have been available in the university's digital archive since October 2026.[5]

    Siege of Talas and death

    During the siege of Talas in 1992, Jusupbekov took part in the nightly broadcasts of the Provisional Government from the city's radio studio, singing passages of the epic and playing the komuz between news bulletins. On 24 May 1992, walking home from the studio along University Street, he was killed by shellfire from the forces of the Socialist Republic of Irad. He was 56. He was buried in Talas.[2][6]

    Legacy

    Jusupbekov's notebooks and correspondence are held in the State Archives of Irad in the ethnography fonds. The university's Sagyn Jusupbekov Folklore Cabinet, in the Faculty of Philology and Journalism, keeps his library and his komuz, which was recovered from the street after his death. Radio Irad's evening program Kechki Jomok ("Evening Tales", 1994–2020), presented by Baktygul Omur, is often said to have been modeled on his siege broadcasts.[3]

    His son, Erlan Sagyn (born 1971), served in a student militia during the siege. He worked as a reporter for IradOne from 1993 and was among the journalists who left the broadcaster in 1996 to found the Irad Times, where he writes on culture, history and archives. Each year on 24 May he publishes a column on the siege and on his father's anthology.[2]
