    Socialist Republic of Irad

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia
    This article is about the communist state of 1991–1992. For the Soviet union republic of 1925–1991, see Iradi Soviet Socialist Republic.

    The Socialist Republic of Irad (SRI; Iradi: Irad Sotsialisttik Respublikası; Russian: Социалистическая Республика Ирад) was the communist state that succeeded the Iradi Soviet Socialist Republic (ISSR) at the end of the Soviet period. It was proclaimed on 9 October 1991, when the Supreme Soviet adopted the Declaration of State Sovereignty (1991) and the Communist Party of Irad declared the republic independent of the Soviet Union. Its government sat in Kobozev (now Nurzhan), and its only head of state was Vasily Chernov, First Secretary of the Communist Party of Irad and President of the republic.[1]

    The SRI lasted ten months. Economic collapse and its government's emergency decrees provoked the Winter Uprising of 1992, which drove its officials out of Talas in February 1992. On 11 March the Provisional Government in Talas proclaimed the Republic of Irad, beginning the First Iradi Civil War. SRI forces besieged Talas from April, but after the Provisional Government signed the Alimbekov-Baker Treaty with the United States, the US intervention known as Operation Starlight broke the siege. Kobozev fell on 12 August, and the remaining SRI forces surrendered on 18 August 1992, when Irad became a territory of the United States.[2]

    Background

    Chernov, an ethnic Russian refinery engineer from Kobozev, became First Secretary of the Communist Party of Irad in November 1986. He carried out some of perestroika's economic measures but resisted the demands of the national movement that emerged during the Kobozev bread riots of 1989: the return of the Latin alphabet, freedom for the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church and democratic elections. Police harassed the Ene-Til ("Mother Tongue") society of the historian Amanbek Alimbekov. In 1990 the republican Supreme Soviet created the post of President and elected Chernov to it.[3] During the coup attempt in Moscow in August 1991 he made no public statement for two days, a silence his critics later cited as evidence of sympathy for the plotters.[3]

    Proclamation

    After the coup failed, the party leadership moved to secure its position as Union institutions collapsed. On 9 October 1991 the Supreme Soviet adopted the Declaration of State Sovereignty. Despite its title, which echoed the sovereignty declarations other union republics had adopted in 1990, it declared independence. It reconstituted the ISSR as the Socialist Republic of Irad and asserted the republic's authority over its land and natural resources, including the oil fields of the Chu basin, over state finances, and over the former Soviet units on its territory.[1] The national movement called the declaration a maneuver to keep the party in power, since it was not followed by free elections or the legalization of opposition parties. The Soviet Union was dissolved on 26 December 1991.

    Government and politics

    The Communist Party of Irad kept its monopoly of power, and Chernov combined the offices of party leader and President. The Supreme Soviet and the Council of Ministers inherited from the ISSR continued to function, and the ISSR constitution of 1978 remained in force with amendments. Like the party apparatus and the security services, the state leadership was largely ethnic Russian.[2] Russian remained the language of government, and Iradi continued to be written in Cyrillic.

    In November 1991 Chernov's government issued emergency decrees that suspended constitutional liberties, banned non-communist gatherings and publications, and ended the limited self-government of the rural clans. Ene-Til's newsletter was closed. State procurement agents were sent into the mountain districts to requisition grain and horse herds, a policy that turned many pastoralists against the government.[4]

    The SRI's international standing was never settled. No foreign government is known to have opened an embassy in Kobozev, and its position in the Commonwealth of Independent States remained undefined.[5]

    Economy

    The end of transfers from Moscow, ruble clearing and Soviet fuel deliveries brought most of Kobozev's factories to a halt. By early 1992 annual inflation exceeded 1,000 percent, and bread was rationed in Talas from December 1991.[4] The Soviet barter arrangement under which downstream republics delivered winter fuel in exchange for summer irrigation water from the Toktogul Hydroelectric Complex lapsed, and the SRI began to keep more water in the reservoir in summer for winter generation, the start of a long dispute with Uzbekistan and Kazakhstan.[6] Antimony smelting at Chatkal stopped during the civil war, and many Russian engineers left the republic.

    Armed forces

    The SRI took over the Soviet units stationed in the republic, chiefly the 17th Motor Rifle Division garrisoned around Kobozev, together with republican OMON detachments and interior troops. Their officer corps, like the leadership, was largely ethnic Russian.[7] Not all officers stayed loyal. Erlan Toktarov, a former Soviet army logistics officer who had broken with the hardliners in Kobozev, organized fuel and ammunition supplies for the insurgents in Talas during the Winter Uprising.[2]

    Winter Uprising and civil war

    Protests began in Talas in early December 1991. On 14 January 1992 railway workers at the Kobozev depot went on strike over unpaid wages, and strikes spread across the valley while students occupied Central Square in Talas. On 3 February OMON detachments fired on the demonstrators. The SRI government gave the death toll as 11 and the Provisional Government later as 46; a commission of the Territorial Legislature of Irad documented 27 deaths in 2002. Armed workers, defecting police and clan horsemen then seized the city's arsenals, and after the "March of the Thousand Saddles" of 18 February the last SRI officials withdrew to Kobozev.[8]

    The government did not recognize the Republic of Irad proclaimed at the Talas Town Hall Meeting on 11 March. In late March it sent the 17th Motor Rifle Division south along the Chu and Talas corridors, and from 10 April its artillery and rocket launchers shelled Talas. Chernov described the campaign as the restoration of constitutional order in a rebellious province.[9] After the treaty of 2 June he denounced the American intervention as foreign aggression and appealed to Moscow and the other members of the Commonwealth for support; Russia protested but did not intervene. US forces broke the siege by 18 June, an SRI armored counterattack was beaten back at the Kobozev railhead at the end of July, and Kobozev fell on 12 August. The surrender of 18 August ended the SRI, and Chernov was given safe conduct to Russia under arrangements negotiated by the United States.[2]

    Aftermath

    Many ethnic Russians associated with the SRI administration emigrated to Russia after the war. In September 1992 Alimbekov announced that Kobozev would be renamed Nurzhan, a change confirmed by the Toponymic Heritage Statute of 1993 (see De-Russification Movement in Irad). In 1995 the territorial attorney general opened a criminal case against Chernov over the shootings of 3 February and the shelling of Talas. Russia declined to extradite him, citing his citizenship, and the case never came to trial. He died in Moscow in 2012.[3]

    The records of the SRI's party and state bodies passed to the State Archives of Irad, where the Central Committee protocols are held in Fond 85. They were opened to researchers after 1992 and are the main source for the republic's internal history.[1]

    Legacy and historiography

    Iradi national historiography treats the SRI as the last phase of Soviet rule rather than as an independent Iradi state, and 9 October is not a public holiday; the national holiday is Irad Day on 11 March (see March 11 Independence Day). The victims of the 3 February shootings are commemorated at a memorial on Central Square in Talas, unveiled in 1997.[8] Chernov's memoir, The Broken Union (2001), presents the Winter Uprising as a nationalist insurrection that made the breakup of the republic inevitable, and his resistance to the intervention as an attempt to preserve regional stability.[9]

    Memory of the SRI is divided. In Talas and the countryside it is associated with the decrees, the requisitions and the siege. Among older Russian-speaking residents of Nurzhan, the Russos, it is more often remembered as the end of the stability of the late Soviet decades, and Chernov as a capable industrial manager (see Russos of Nurzhan).[3] Historians generally regard the republic's economic collapse as largely beyond its leaders' control. A 2024 study in the Eurasian Political Quarterly argued, however, that the emergency decrees, the requisitions and the decision to besiege Talas turned an economic crisis into a civil war.[3]
