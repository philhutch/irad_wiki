    Desert Sun’s Rebellion

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    Desert Sun’s Rebellion (Russian: Мятеж полуденного солнца; Iradi: Chöl Künü Kötörülüshü) is a 1979 Soviet thriller film directed by Nurlan Sadikov for Talasfilm Studios, starring Almaz Sarybaev. Set in the desert borderlands of the Iradi SSR in the early 1920s, it follows a former cavalryman who leads a small crew to steal a herd of requisitioned horses back from a warlord's stronghold, and the crew's falling-out on the way across the sands. Its spaghetti Western aesthetic and relentless pacing made it a commercial success in the Soviet Union, and it was favorably reviewed in the United States. It created the genre known as the Iradi Western or Soviet Western, in Irad usually called the "Eastern", which shaped Iradi film production through the 1980s, and it is often described as Irad's Rififi in the look of a spaghetti western.[1][2]

    Plot

    In 1922 Daniyar (Sarybaev), a former cavalryman who had fought alongside the Red Guards against the Kokand Autonomy, returns to his clan's winter grounds at the edge of the sands. The clan's horses, requisitioned for the Red Army, have been stolen on their way to the railhead by the warlord Kara-Bek (Erkin Sultanbekov), who holds them in a walled caravanserai deep in the desert while he waits for buyers from across the frontier. Commissar Lugovoy (Gennady Ostapenko) cannot take the fort by force. He offers Daniyar a pardon for his past if he will steal the herd back, and keeps Daniyar's sister Aisha (Gulnara Alimbekova) in town as a guarantee.

    Daniyar gathers a crew of five: a deserter who knows the fort's wells, an old horse thief, two brothers from a rival clan and a Russian railwayman with a stopwatch. They study the caravanserai for days from the dunes, timing the guards and the watering of the herd. The theft itself, at night, runs for almost thirty minutes without dialogue or music: the crew drug the guards' water, muffle the horses' hooves with felt and lead the herd out through the stable vaults one animal at a time.

    The crossing back to the railhead goes wrong. Kara-Bek's riders follow as dust on the horizon, the wells are poisoned, and the crew begin to argue over the herd, which each of them now has reason to keep. The final sequence takes place on a salt flat at noon, where a drawn-out standoff between the crew, Kara-Bek's riders and a detachment sent by Lugovoy ends in a battle in which most of the characters die. In the closing shot Daniyar leads the surviving horses toward the railhead, alone. The film does not say whether he will hand them over.[1]

    Cast

    • Almaz Sarybaev as Daniyar
    • Gennady Ostapenko as Commissar Lugovoy
    • Erkin Sultanbekov as Kara-Bek
    • Gulnara Alimbekova as Aisha

    Production

    The project was first assigned to the director Timur Aslanov. At the start of production the studio replaced him with Sadikov, who had spent a decade in Talasfilm's documentary unit after the poor reception of Zarina (1968). Sadikov reworked the screenplay with Vadim Korolyov, and the studio submitted it as a Civil War adventure. Soviet "Eastern" adventure films set in Central Asia had a successful record, and the republican film committee approved it with few changes. The committee asked only that the commissar be made more sympathetic.[2]

    Filming took place in the summer of 1978 in the Kara-Suu Canyon and the southern sands, with no studio sets. Temperatures on location reportedly exceeded 45 °C.[citation needed] Sadikov hired riders from pastoral communities as extras and stunt riders. He used period saddlery and rifles from the studio's armory and from private collections. Cinematographer Vladimir Pak shot much of the film in extreme close-up and long static wide shots, borrowing from Italian westerns, which Soviet professionals could see at closed screenings. The score by Asan Jumabaev combines the komuz with electric guitar, whistling and long passages of silence.[3]

    Release

    The film premiered in Talas in March 1979 and went into All-Union distribution later that year. According to Soviet distribution figures, it drew about 17.9 million viewers in its first year. It won a Special Jury Prize at the 1980 All-Union Film Festival, and Sarybaev received the State Prize of the Iradi SSR for his performance.[2]

    In late 1979 it was shown in New York in a program of new Soviet films.[1]

    Reception

    Soviet reviewers praised the film's craft and Sarybaev's performance. Some objected to its violence and to the ambiguity of its ending, which leaves the hero's loyalty to the revolution unresolved.[2]

    In the United States, Walter Goodman wrote for The New York Times that "this unforgiving pacing ramps up and up with no release … there is a visceral discomfort to it, a feeling that you are there, under the unforgiving sun".[4]

    Legacy

    Desert Sun’s Rebellion is credited with creating the Iradi Western, and it set the tone for Iradi films for the rest of the Soviet period. During the 1980s Irad became known as "the West of the East". Its studios produced a series of films in the same mold by other directors, among them Timur Aslanov's The Golden Bay (1980), Beknur Karimov's Desert Blue (1981), Adil Rakhimov's Guide of the Sands (1981) and The Silver Horseshoe (1984), Kairat Amankulov's Track of the Snow Leopard (1983) and Journey of the Steppes (1988) and Malik Shakirov's Vengeance Rider (1986). Sadikov himself never returned to the genre and later said he had no interest in it.[1]

    Its cast and crew carried the genre on. Erkin Sultanbekov played warlords in The Golden Bay (1980) and Vengeance Rider (1986), Gulnara Alimbekova took the lead in Desert Blue (1981), Sarybaev parodied his own Daniyar in The Silver Horseshoe (1984), Vladimir Pak photographed Vengeance Rider, and second-unit cameraman Kadyrbek Asylbekov shot Guide of the Sands (1981) and Journey of the Steppes (1988). Timur Aslanov, whom Sadikov had replaced, made The Golden Bay the following year.[3]

    The Talasfilm Archives restored the film from a duplicate negative and surviving prints in 2004. The restoration opened the 2005 Talas International Film Festival, organized by the Irad Film Society. Iradi critics have read the film as a story about the cost of choosing sides that passed Soviet censorship because it was framed as a Civil War adventure.[3]
