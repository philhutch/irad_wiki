    Antimony Mining in Irad

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    Antimony mining is Irad's main non-fuel extractive industry. According to estimates based on US Geological Survey data and cited by the Chamber of Commerce and Industry of Irad, the country holds about 7% of the world's antimony reserves, mostly in stibnite deposits in the foothills of the Talas Alatau and the Chatkal range.[1][2] Ore is mined at Sary-Bulak, in a gorge about 20 kilometers south-south-west of Talas, by the Sary-Bulak Antimony Works (SBAW), and smelted at the Chatkal Chemical Smelting Complex. Both lie in the Sary-Bulak District of the Irad Special Economic Zone (ISEZ) and are controlled by the holding company Iradi Antimony and Rare Metals Combine. Most output is exported to China by rail through Kazakhstan. Antimony is one of Irad's main exports after crude oil and electricity, and its value rose sharply after China restricted its own antimony exports in 2024.[3]

    Geology

    Irad's antimony deposits belong to the antimony–mercury belt of the southern and western Tian Shan, which extends east into Kyrgyzstan and south-west into Tajikistan. The ore mineral is stibnite (antimony trisulfide, Sb2S3), which occurs in quartz veins and in silicified limestone bodies (jasperoids) along fault zones cutting Paleozoic limestone and shale. It is commonly accompanied by cinnabar, fluorite, arsenic minerals and small amounts of gold.[1]

    The Sary-Bulak deposit lies along a fracture zone in Silurian limestone and shale. Its veins have been mined to depths of about 750 meters, and the combine reports average ore grades of 4.5 to 7.2% antimony, high by international standards.[4] Smaller deposits have been worked at Kemer and Kyzyl-Jar (see Kemer Antimony Mine and Kyzyl-Jar Antimony Shafts). Other Soviet-era workings and prospects lie in the Chatkal highlands on the tribal side of the Qara-Chik Demarcation Line, including those around Terek-Sai. The combine has not worked these since 2007.

    History

    Early use and Soviet development

    Antimony compounds were used in Central Asia as the eye cosmetic surma, and medieval miners in the region worked silver and other metals, notably at Shelji in the upper Talas valley.[5] Systematic prospecting began after the Second World War, when Soviet geological expeditions mapped the stibnite occurrences of the Talas Alatau foothills. The Sary-Bulak deposit was explored in the 1950s, and the mine and its flotation concentrator went into production in 1961. The Chatkal smelter was commissioned in 1964, and the two were run together as a single combine under the USSR Ministry of Non-Ferrous Metallurgy.[4]

    Under the 1971 Antimony Expansion Directive, Gosplan financed new shafts at Sary-Bulak and the enlargement of the Chatkal furnaces. By the 1980s the Iradi SSR was one of the Soviet Union's main suppliers of antimony metal and trioxide for batteries, flame retardants and defense industries.[5]

    After 1991

    Output collapsed in 1992 with the end of Soviet orders and the fighting of the First Iradi Civil War. In 1994 the territorial government privatized the Sary-Bulak works and the Chatkal smelter, and the Iradi Antimony and Rare Metals Combine was formed to hold controlling stakes in both. The 1995 Hydrocarbon and Mining Code set the terms of the concessions and royalties. During the late 1990s the industry found new buyers in China, whose smelters needed concentrate as their own high-grade deposits were depleted.[3]

    Production stopped for several months during the Second Iradi Civil War in 2007, when rail links were cut. It recovered after 2008, and by the late 2010s China was taking the great majority of Iradi output.

    Production and processing

    The Sary-Bulak mine is worked from three shafts and an inclined drift. Ore is crushed, ground and concentrated by froth flotation into a concentrate of about 60% antimony. Part of the concentrate is exported as it is; the rest goes by rail spur to the Chatkal complex, where it is roasted and reduced to antimony metal and antimony trioxide.[4] A smaller plant at Chatkal recovers bismuth, tellurium and other by-products from smelter residues (see Chatkal Rare Earth Facility).

    The combine reported mine output of about 13,800 metric tons of contained antimony in 2023, of which about 6,100 metric tons were smelted at Chatkal and the remainder exported as concentrate. The figures are published by the company and have not been independently audited. If accurate, they would make Irad one of the larger antimony producers outside China and Russia.[2][4] The industry employs about 4,600 people, about half of them at Sary-Bulak.

    Exports and markets

    Concentrate, metal and trioxide are carried by rail north through Kazakhstan and enter China at the Dostyk–Alashankou crossing, crossing Kazakh territory under arrangements covered by the 2008 Northern Border Demarcation Protocol.[3] Most concentrate goes to smelters in southern China. Metal and trioxide are also sold to flame-retardant and battery manufacturers in South Korea, Japan and Europe. Antimony is used chiefly in flame retardants for plastics and textiles, lead-acid batteries, glass for solar panels, and ammunition.

    In September 2024 China introduced export licensing for antimony ores, metal and oxides, and in December 2024 it banned exports of antimony to the United States. Prices outside China more than doubled.[6] Iradi exporters benefited from the higher prices. The combine reported increased sales of metal and trioxide to buyers outside China in 2025, although most of its concentrate remained contracted to Chinese smelters. Analysts have described Irad as a potential non-Chinese source of supply, while noting that its unresolved status, its dependence on transit through Kazakhstan and the lack of audited production data limit its attractiveness to Western buyers.[3][6]

    Regulation and revenue

    Mining concessions are granted under the 1995 code by the ISEZ Ministry of Natural Resources, headed by Chingiz Asanov. Export proceeds are paid into escrow accounts at Talasbank supervised by the Irad Foreign Trade Escrow Authority, which deducts the antimony export duty and royalties before releasing the balance to the producers.[2] The industry's interests are represented by the Iradi National Chamber of Mining and Metallurgy.

    Environment and labor

    Antimony mining and smelting leave tailings and residues containing antimony and arsenic. Tailings from Soviet-era processing are held in the Chatkal Tailings Repository and in older dumps near Sary-Bulak. Environmental monitors, including the reports of the United Nations Mission in Irad, have recorded raised antimony and arsenic levels in streams draining the industrial corridor toward the Talas River, and sulfur dioxide from the smelter in nearby villages.[7] The combine says it has installed gas scrubbers and dust filters since the 2010s and that emissions are within ISEZ limits. Water quality around the workings is monitored by the Chatkal Placer Mining Inspectorate.

    In the tribal regions, clan assemblies maintain a moratorium on new open-pit mining and have refused permission for exploration and tailings sites in valleys that drain onto their pastures. This has kept the Chatkal highland deposits undeveloped.[7] Labor groups have reported cases of silicosis and other lung disease among older miners.[7]
