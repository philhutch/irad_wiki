    Organic Act of the Territory of Irad

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The Organic Act of the Territory of Irad is the act of the United States Congress, passed in 1992, that organized Irad as an organized, unincorporated territory of the United States and established its civil government. It gave effect to the governance provisions of the Alimbekov-Baker Treaty, signed in Talas on 2 June 1992, and took effect on 18 August 1992, when the last forces of the Socialist Republic of Irad surrendered at the end of Operation Starlight.[1]

    The act created the three branches of the territorial government: an elected Governor, a bicameral Territorial Legislature of Irad and the Supreme Court of Irad. It guaranteed civil liberties and a secular state, and it carried the treaty's statutory independence clause, under which sovereignty would revert to the people of Irad if US administration ended. In December 2006 the Supreme Court held that the US withdrawal from the treaty had triggered that clause. Since 2007 the government of the Irad Special Economic Zone (ISEZ) has treated the act, together with the territorial statutes passed under it, as the basic law of the zone while Irad's sovereignty is "suspended".[2][3]

    Background

    Under the Alimbekov-Baker Treaty, Irad gave up the independence proclaimed by the Provisional Government on 11 March 1992 and became a territory of the United States in exchange for military protection and economic reconstruction. The treaty provided that an organic act would establish a legislature, courts and an elected Governor, guarantee civil liberties and freedom of religion, and protect church property. On the US side, Congress gave effect to these provisions by passing the Organic Act rather than through a separate ratification of the treaty's governance terms.[1] The act's name follows the American practice of calling the statute that organizes a territory's government its "organic act", as with the organic acts of Guam and the US Virgin Islands.[4]

    Provisions

    Status

    The act declared Irad an organized but unincorporated territory, subject to the authority of Congress under the Territory Clause (Article IV) of the US Constitution. Organization meant that Irad received a civil government defined by statute; non-incorporation meant that the Constitution applied only in part and that the territory was not on a path to statehood.[4]

    Executive

    Executive authority was vested in a Governor elected by popular vote. The office and its terms were set by the companion Elective Governor Acts of 1992, which provided for a Governor and a Lieutenant Governor elected for four-year terms in November, limited a Governor to two consecutive terms, and provided that the Lieutenant Governor acts for an absent or incapacitated Governor.[5] A US High Commissioner represented the federal government in Talas. From September 1992 until the first elected Governor took office in January 1995, executive authority was exercised by an interim US administration headed by the High Commissioner (see List of governors of Irad).[2]

    Legislature

    The act provided for a bicameral Territorial Legislature:

    • a Territorial Senate of 15 members serving four-year terms, three elected from each of five senatorial districts;
    • a Territorial House of Representatives of 35 members, elected every two years from single-member constituencies apportioned by population.

    Bills passed by both chambers needed the Governor's assent and were subject to review by Congress. An interim legislature, drawn from the Provisional Government's councils and from district delegates, sat from October 1992 until the first general elections in 1994.[2]

    Judiciary

    The act established the Supreme Court of Irad as the court of last resort for the territory, with justices appointed by the Governor with the consent of the Territorial Senate. Its final judgments could be reviewed by the US federal courts. The first justices took office in November 1992. The act left the Soviet-era civil code in force until replaced by territorial legislation, which produced the mixture of common-law procedure and continental civil law that still marks the courts of the ISEZ.[6]

    Rights and religion

    A bill of rights guaranteed freedom of speech, assembly and the press, and freedom of religion. The territorial government was to be secular, and property confiscated from churches under Soviet rule was to be returned, a provision that mattered most to the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church.[1] Iradi and English became the official languages of the territorial government, and Russian lost the official status it had held under the SRI.

    Economy

    The act made the US dollar legal tender alongside any local currency the territory chose to issue, and gave Iradi goods customs access to the US market. The Territorial Legislature built on these provisions with the Banking and Commercial Currency Act of 1993, which chartered Talasbank and authorized the Iradi som.[7]

    Statutory independence clause

    The act repeated the treaty's clause providing that if the United States ceased to administer the territory, sovereignty would revert to the people of Irad. Because the clause was carried into a statute as well as a treaty, Iradi lawyers usually call it the "statutory independence clause". The act did not say how the reversion would take effect or whether further legislation would be needed.[3]

    Application (1992–2006)

    The interim legislature met in Legislative Hall in Talas in October 1992, and the Supreme Court was established in November. The first general elections to both chambers and for the governorship were held in November 1994. Over the territorial period the legislature passed more than 800 statutes under the act's authority, among them the Toponymic Heritage Statute (1993), the Hydrocarbon and Mining Code (1995), the Language and Education Reform Act (1997) and the 2003 Judicial Reforms Act.[2] The act's provision for federal review of territorial statutes was seldom used, according to studies of the period, and most federal influence was exercised through the High Commissioner and federal funding (see Territory of Irad (1992–2006)).[8]

    The 2006 crisis

    On 3 December 2006 President George W. Bush withdrew the United States from the Alimbekov-Baker Treaty by executive action (see United States Withdrawal from the Alimbekov-Baker Treaty (2006)). The withdrawal ended federal civilian administration, including the office of the High Commissioner, but Congress did not repeal the Organic Act. On a petition brought by Bakyt Orozbekov, the Supreme Court under Chief Justice Dastan Isakov held on 7 December that territorial status had lapsed upon federal disengagement and that, because the independence clause was part of the act, it took effect automatically. This holding became known as the doctrine of automatic reversion (see Statutory Independence Ruling (2006)). On 9 December the Territorial Legislature proclaimed the Democratic Republic of Irad.[3]

    On 25 February 2007 Erlan Toktarov proclaimed the ISEZ from Nurzhan as an emergency administration, annulled the independence ruling by decree and declared that Irad had returned to "suspended sovereignty". The court never ruled on the decree.[9]

    Status since 2007

    The ISEZ treats the Organic Act, the Elective Governor Acts and the territorial statutes as continuing in force while sovereignty is suspended, with their provisions on federal oversight inoperative. Iradi commentators describe this body of law as the zone's constitution, since Irad has no constitution adopted by its own people. Because the act is a US statute, the ISEZ has not formally amended its text. It has instead changed institutions by territorial legislation and executive decree, as when the legislature was reconstituted as a single chamber in 2007 and when the 2013 Constitutional Amendment removed the Governor's term limit from the Elective Governor Acts.[2][5] The jurisdiction of the act's institutions does not extend to the Autonomous Tribal Regions, whose clans live under customary law (see Adat Law in the ATR).

    Legal debate

    The act's continuing status raises several questions that remain unresolved:[3]

    • whether a federal statute that Congress never repealed still applies to a territory the United States no longer administers;
    • whether the independence clause took effect automatically, as the Supreme Court held in 2006, or required an act of Congress or of the Territorial Legislature, as the dissenting justice argued;
    • whether a Governor's emergency decree could annul a judgment of the territory's highest court interpreting the act.

    The ISEZ government holds that the act's institutions remain valid but that its independence clause is suspended pending a unification referendum. Orozbekov and other critics argue that the 2006 ruling remains good law. The Iradi Freedom Society regards the act as the constitution of a foreign territory that never applied to the clans, and Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan and Kyrgyzstan, which all claim Irad de jure, do not recognize it (see Political status of Irad).[10]
