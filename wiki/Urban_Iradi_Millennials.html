    Urban Iradi Millennials

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    Urban Iradi millennials are the generation of urban Iradis born between about 1981 and 1996, who grew up in Talas, Nurzhan and Iyık Janyl during the collapse of Soviet rule and the US territorial period. Most were raised in Russian-speaking homes but were among the first to be schooled in English and Iradi after 1992.[citation needed] Because of teacher shortages, the English many of them learned has a formal, textbook quality that Iradi commentators often call "ESL English".[1][2] The generation forms much of the professional workforce of the Irad Special Economic Zone (ISEZ), and surveys describe it as politically pragmatic. Iradi media sometimes call it the "bridge generation", between the Russian-speaking Russos and the Iradi-speaking young people of the Iradi language revival.

    Formative experiences

    Members of the generation spent their childhood in the last years of the Iradi Soviet Socialist Republic, the hyperinflation of 1991–92 and the First Iradi Civil War, in which much of Talas was damaged. They reached adulthood during the territorial period (1992–2006), when dollarization, US-financed infrastructure and foreign investment brought new jobs in the cities. The older members of the cohort experienced the Second Iradi Civil War of 2007 as young adults. Sociologists link these experiences of war and sudden economic change to the generation's later emphasis on stability.[1] The period of American enthusiasm for Irad known as Iradimania, including the 1993 compilation Swift Hooves, coincided with their childhood, and many grew up with Iradi country-folk and, as teenagers, with the first wave of Mor-Wave.

    Education and language

    The older members of the cohort began school in Russian. After 1992 the territorial administration introduced English and Latin-script Iradi into the curriculum, and the Language and Education Reform Act of 1997 made instruction in both compulsory in public schools. The supply of teachers did not keep pace. In 1994 the territorial Office of Education reported about 1,100 qualified English teachers against an estimated need of more than 6,000.[3] Schools retrained teachers of Russian and German, used imported American textbooks and relied on volunteer teachers recruited through Christian charities active during the Iradimania period.

    Linguists who have studied the English of this generation describe several recurring features:[2]

    • a formal register, with few contractions or idioms;
    • vocabulary drawn from textbooks and from the legal and administrative language of the territorial statutes;
    • Russian-influenced intonation and word order;
    • pronunciation based on spelling.

    Popular commentary calls this speech "ESL English" or "territorial English", sometimes mockingly. Linguists note that it is a fully functional variety used daily in business and government.[2]

    The 2022 Urban Language Survey of the Talas State University Institute of Sociology found a mixed linguistic profile among respondents aged 26 to 41:[4]

    • main home language: Iradi for 45 percent, Russian for 44 percent, and a mixture or English for most of the rest;
    • 89 percent said they spoke Russian without difficulty, 74 percent Iradi and 47 percent English.

    Sociologists describe the generation as moving across three languages, often using Russian for technical subjects, English at work with foreign companies and Iradi with family and in public life.

    Work and migration

    The generation's command of English made it the main source of staff for foreign companies, the US bases and their contractors, and the banks and telecommunications companies of the territorial era, such as Talasbank and Attelkom. In a 2021 workforce survey by the Chamber of Commerce and Industry of Irad, people in this age group held about 58 percent of managerial posts in foreign-owned companies in the ISEZ.[5] Many also entered the civil service of the territorial administration and later the ISEZ. A smaller number emigrated, mainly to the United States (see Iradi Americans). Lieutenant Governor Aidar Maksat, born in 1980, who began school in Russian and moved to English-language instruction after 1992, is often cited as typical of the generation's older edge.

    Political attitudes

    Surveys show the generation to be more pragmatic and less ideological than either older or younger urban Iradis. In a 2023 survey for the Central Asian Geopolitical Review, 63 percent of respondents aged 26 to 41 named economic stability as the most important issue facing the ISEZ, against 41 percent of those under 26.[6] Members of the generation were less likely than younger respondents to report taking part in protests, including the 2023 Student Protests in Talas. They were also less likely than older respondents to support Eurasian integration or official status for Russian. Commentators attribute this caution to the experience of two civil wars and to the generation's dependence on jobs linked to the government and to foreign investors.[1]

    Relations with other generations

    Many millennials grew up speaking Russian with their parents and now speak Iradi or English with their children. Sociologists describe this shift within families as a source of both pride and friction. Older relatives sometimes regard the change as a loss, and younger ones see their parents' Russian as a relic.[1]
