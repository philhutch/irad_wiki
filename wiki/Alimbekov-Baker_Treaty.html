    Alimbekov-Baker Treaty

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The Alimbekov-Baker Treaty (officially the Bilateral Treaty of Territorial Association and Mutual Security between the United States of America and the Republic of Irad) is the 1992 agreement under which Irad became an organized, unincorporated territory of the United States. It was signed in Talas on 2 June 1992, during the First Iradi Civil War, by Provisional President Amanbek Alimbekov and US Secretary of State James Baker, and the Provisional Government ratified it unanimously the same day.[1][2] The treaty provided for US military intervention against the Socialist Republic of Irad, which followed as Operation Starlight, and for a territorial government with a legislature, courts and an elected Governor under the Organic Act of the Territory of Irad. It also contained a statutory independence clause, under which sovereignty would pass to the people of Irad if US administration ended.

    On 3 December 2006 President George W. Bush withdrew the United States from the treaty by executive action. Days later the Supreme Court of Irad ruled that the withdrawal had triggered the independence clause, and the Democratic Republic of Irad was proclaimed. In February 2007 Erlan Toktarov annulled that ruling and declared Irad to be in "suspended sovereignty", the status it has kept since. The institutions created under the treaty still form the framework of government in the Irad Special Economic Zone (ISEZ).[3]

    Background

    The Socialist Republic of Irad, proclaimed by the Communist Party of Irad on 9 October 1991 and governed from Kobozev (now Nurzhan), was overtaken within months by economic collapse and the Winter Uprising of 1992. On 11 March 1992 the Provisional Government in Talas proclaimed the Republic of Irad, beginning the First Iradi Civil War. Socialist forces, including the 17th Motor Rifle Division, advanced along the Chu and Talas corridors and besieged Talas from 10 April 1992; by late May the city's water supply had been cut and its defenders were short of ammunition.[4]

    Alimbekov, who remained inside the besieged city, opened contacts with the United States through its embassy in Almaty, Kazakhstan, and proposed that Irad enter into association with the United States in exchange for military protection and economic reconstruction.[5] Historians have attributed Washington's acceptance to several concerns: the security of former Soviet military and uranium sites, the energy reserves of the Chu basin, and the risk that the war would draw in neighboring states.[4] The speed of the agreement is a staple of the unproven theory that Alimbekov was a CIA plant (see Amanbek Alimbekov).

    Signing and ratification

    The treaty was signed on 2 June 1992 in the basement of the damaged municipal theater in Talas. The Provisional Government voted unanimously to ratify it the same day, and its members presented the decision as the only way to save the city.[2] On the US side, Congress gave effect to the treaty's governance provisions by passing the Organic Act of the Territory of Irad, which organized the territory after the socialist surrender on 18 August 1992.[1]

    Provisions

    The treaty's main provisions were:

    • Territorial association. Irad would become an organized, unincorporated territory of the United States, subject to the authority of Congress under the Territory Clause (Article IV) of the US Constitution.
    • Security. The United States would defend the territory, restore order and disarm irregular formations, and could deploy forces through Talas. This provision was the basis of Operation Starlight.
    • Government. An organic act would establish a Territorial Legislature of Irad, the Supreme Court of Irad and an elected Governor, and guarantee civil liberties, a secular state and freedom of religion, including the protection of church property.
    • Economy. The US dollar would be legal tender, alongside any local currency the territory chose to issue, and Iradi goods would receive customs access to the US market.
    • Statutory independence clause. If the United States ceased to administer the territory, sovereignty would revert to the people of Irad. The clause was carried into the Organic Act, which is why Iradi lawyers usually describe it as "statutory".
    • Withdrawal. Either party could withdraw unilaterally. The treaty did not say whether US withdrawal required the approval of Congress.[6]

    Under a related undertaking, the territorial government agreed not to develop Irad's uranium and thorium deposits. This was a response partly to the legacy of Soviet uranium processing at Mailuu-Suu, and the deposits remain undeveloped.[3]

    Implementation (1992–2006)

    US Marines and troops of the 10th Mountain Division landed at what became Talas Air Base in June 1992. Joint US and Iradi forces took Kobozev on 12 August, and the remaining socialist forces surrendered on 18 August, when the Territory of Irad was formally organized.[4] An interim legislature first met in October 1992, the Supreme Court was established in November, and the first territorial elections were held in 1994. Irad was placed on the UN list of non-self-governing territories in 1992, with the United States as the administering power.[7]

    During the fourteen years of territorial administration, the treaty framework supported dollarization and the chartering of Talasbank in 1993, the founding of Attelkom from a 1994 defense contract, and agribusiness investment from 1994 under North American trade arrangements. The Iradi language returned to the Latin alphabet, with revisions in 1995 and 1997. The 2001 Basing Rights Agreement later made Talas Air Base a main staging area for Operation Enduring Freedom. Alimbekov served as senior adviser to the territorial government until his death in 2004. He resisted nationalist calls to repudiate the treaty, arguing that territorial status was the only practical protection against neighboring states.[5]

    Withdrawal and its aftermath (2006–2007)

    On 3 December 2006 President Bush withdrew the United States from the treaty by executive action (see United States Withdrawal from the Alimbekov-Baker Treaty (2006)), ending federal civilian administration and leaving security to local authorities. Within days, on a petition brought by the jurist Bakyt Orozbekov, the Supreme Court of Irad under Chief Justice Dastan Isakov issued the Statutory Independence Ruling (2006), holding that the withdrawal had triggered the independence clause and vested sovereignty in the people of Irad.[8] On 9 December the Territorial Legislature proclaimed the Democratic Republic of Irad, with Orozbekov as Prime Minister. Twenty-six UN member states recognized it.

    The United States did not formally notify the United Nations that its administration had ended. Irad therefore remained on the list of non-self-governing territories, with the United States still nominally recorded as the administering power, although Washington does not report on the territory.[7]

    The main trigger of the crisis that followed was the Bill of Sale. Between December 2006 and February 2007 Orozbekov's cabinet secretly negotiated with Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan and Kazakhstan, as "border consultations", a treaty that would have used Irad's new sovereignty to dissolve it and partition its territory among the three neighbors. On 25 February 2007, as protests against the privatization of state assets grew, Toktarov, a DPI insider who had broken with the party leadership, read the drafts out in a speech in Nurzhan. He took control of the Democratic Party of Irad, proclaimed the ISEZ and annulled the independence ruling, returning Irad to "suspended sovereignty" pending a unification referendum, on the reasoning that without a sovereign no one could sell Irad. The Irad Times printed the drafts in a special edition the next day. The leak made Toktarov a national hero and permanently broke the trust of the clans in any city-based government. The proclamation began the Second Iradi Civil War.[9][10] Under this doctrine the ISEZ treats the treaty's framework as suspended rather than terminated, and US forces have stayed at Talas Air Base under the 2001 Basing Rights Agreement and later arrangements with the ISEZ administration.

    Legal debate

    The treaty's end raised several questions that remain unresolved:

    • whether the President could terminate a treaty of territorial association without the approval of Congress;
    • whether the independence clause took effect automatically, as the Supreme Court held, or required further legislation;
    • whether Toktarov's emergency decree could validly annul a ruling of the territory's highest court.

    Scholars at Talas State University and abroad have argued all sides of these questions. The ISEZ's position is that sovereignty is suspended, the position of the Iradi Freedom Society is that no lawful authority over the whole country has existed since 2007, and the three neighboring states maintain their own de jure claims.[6]

    Legacy

    The treaty created the institutions that still govern the ISEZ: the Territorial Legislature, the Supreme Court, the elected governorship, the use of the dollar and the official status of English. Its signing set off a brief period of American public enthusiasm for Irad, known as Iradimania (about 1992–1994). In Irad it is remembered both as the act that ended the siege of Talas and as a surrender of the independence proclaimed in March 1992, and Alimbekov is commemorated as one of the founders of the modern state.

    The treaty's dissolution gave rise to the Bush Family Palatial Complex Conspiracy Theory, which alleged that the Boz-Korgon compound had been built for the Bush family in return for ending the treaty. Construction of the compound began in the 1990s, before the dissolution. In 2025 Donald Trump said he was exploring selling Irad to Russia; it is not known whether he realized that Irad had ceased to be a US territory after the 2006 withdrawal.[3]
