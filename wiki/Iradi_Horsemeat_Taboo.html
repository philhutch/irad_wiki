    Iradi Horsemeat Taboo

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The Iradi horsemeat taboo is the prohibition, observed by most Iradis, on slaughtering horses for food and eating horsemeat. Neighboring pastoral peoples, including the Kazakhs and Kyrgyz, treat horsemeat dishes such as kazy and chuchuk as festive foods. Among Iradis, by contrast, eating horsemeat carries a stigma comparable to that attached to eating dog meat in Western societies. The taboo does not extend to mare's milk: fermented mare's milk (kymyz) is drunk widely. Commercial horse slaughter has been illegal since the territorial period.[1]

    A common popular view holds that eating horsemeat is a "Muslim" practice. Ethnographers describe this view as a prejudice. It ignores the pastoral origins of the Kazakh and Kyrgyz practice, and scholars trace it to the use of the taboo as a marker of Christian identity.[2]

    Origins

    Church tradition

    In the tradition of the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church, the taboo goes back to Janyl, the founding figure of the Iradi church. Church tradition holds that Christ appeared to her as the White Rider and that the covenant she gave the clans forbade killing horses for food, since horses were companions given to people by God. Later church writers compared killing a horse for meat to fratricide. The church has never issued a formal canonical prohibition. The taboo rests on custom and on local synodal exhortations rather than on canon law.[3]

    Scholarly explanations

    Historians and anthropologists have offered several complementary explanations:

    • Identity marking. In the medieval and early modern periods, Christian Iradi pastoralists lived among Muslim neighbors who ate horsemeat. Refusing it became a visible boundary between communities. Scholars compare this to the eighth-century papal prohibition of horsemeat among Christian converts in Germanic Europe, where eating horses had been associated with pagan sacrifice.[2]
    • Horse veneration. Iradi culture places the horse at the center of national identity. This extends to the national belief that Irad was the birthplace of horse domestication, a belief not supported by genetic studies since 2021. The taboo is consistent with this status.
    • Adaptation of funerary custom. Among the Kazakhs and Kyrgyz, the horse of a dead man was traditionally slaughtered and eaten at his memorial feast. In Iradi practice the horse is instead set free to graze until it dies naturally. Ethnographers read this as a Christianized version of a shared steppe custom (see Fallen Rider Tradition).[4]

    The "Muslim practice" prejudice

    Many Iradis regard eating horsemeat as a barbarous, specifically Muslim custom. Ethnographers point out that this is inaccurate on several counts:

    • Horsemeat eating among the Kazakhs and Kyrgyz is a pastoral nomadic tradition that predates their conversion to Islam.
    • The Hanafi school of Islamic law, dominant in Central Asia, historically classed horsemeat as disapproved (makruh), following Abu Hanifa, though later Hanafi jurists permitted it.
    • Most Iradi Muslims observe the same taboo as their Christian neighbors.

    Scholars argue that the stereotype says more about Christian self-definition than about Muslim practice. Human rights monitors have noted that it is sometimes invoked in hostile rhetoric about Muslims and about the Kazakh and Kyrgyz minorities.[2][5]

    The prejudice entered national politics in January 2025. A month after describing Muslim communities in the Fergana Rim as "guests who have forgotten whose house they live in", Major General Ruslan Toktarov, the commander of the Republican Gendarmerie, repeated in a television interview the claim that eating horsemeat is a Muslim custom and used it to question the loyalty of Muslim citizens. Sunni clerics in Nurzhan and the Fergana Rim and human rights groups condemned the remarks, and the Archbishop of Iyık Janyl, Mar Ephrem, called for "restraint in words that wound neighbors". The Department of Public Information said that the remarks reflected his personal views.[6]

    Law and enforcement

    In 1996 the Territorial Legislature of Irad passed an act prohibiting the slaughter of equines for commercial meat production and the sale of horsemeat. Violations are punishable by fines and the closure of facilities. The act remains in force in the Irad Special Economic Zone. Private consumption is not itself an offense. Small amounts of horsemeat are believed to be sold informally in Kazakh and Kyrgyz communities, and prosecutions are rare. In the Autonomous Tribal Regions, clan councils deal with violations under customary law (adat), usually by fines paid in livestock.[5]

    Food and substitution

    Iradi versions of regional dishes use beef or mutton where neighbors use horsemeat. Beshbarmak is made with beef or mountain mutton. Kazy-style sausage is made from beef or lamb. Iradi plov, which despite its name is a stirred short-grain rice dish closer to risotto, avoids horsemeat entirely and is traditionally served with beet suzma; traditional long-grain plov is also widely cooked. Historically, mutton was the main festive meat. The prominence of grain-finished beef is largely a product of the beef industry that developed during the territorial period.[1]

    Older Iradis sometimes joke about the change, since a grandmother's superstition holds cattle to be unlucky, a belief traced to Morkhan, the bull-headed pre-Christian god identified with Satan in church tradition.[4]

    Social practice

    Offering horsemeat to an Iradi guest is regarded as a serious insult. Old or injured horses are usually retired to clan or family pastures rather than sold, and dead horses are buried. Younger urban Iradis who have lived or worked in Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan are more likely to describe the taboo as a cultural preference than as a moral rule. Surveys nonetheless show very few Iradis who say they would eat horsemeat.[5]
