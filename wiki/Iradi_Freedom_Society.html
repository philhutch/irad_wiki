    Iradi Freedom Society

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The Iradi Freedom Society (Iradi: Iradin Azatlık Cemiyeti, IAC) is the loose confederation of semi-nomadic clans that governs the Autonomous Tribal Regions (ATR) of Irad, about six-sevenths of the country's territory. It brings together the twenty-four clans (uruk) of the tribal regions, which govern themselves through councils of elders (aksakals) under customary law (adat). The society grew out of the Popular Front of Irad, the clan coalition that stormed Talas in June 2007 during the Second Iradi Civil War, and was constituted after the Bishkek Ceasefire Agreement (2007).[1]

    The IAC's unofficial leader is Bektur Zhumabek uulu, chief of the Tanıp clan, who declines formal titles and describes himself as an ambassador or negotiator for the clans. The society does not recognize the authority of the Democratic Party of Irad (DPI) over the tribal regions. It has nonetheless observed the ceasefire with the Irad Special Economic Zone (ISEZ) since 2007, trades with it, and shares in setting the release schedule of the Toktogul Hydroelectric Complex under UN supervision.[2]

    History

    Background

    Iradi clans developed more cohesive forms of intertribal governance after the defeat of the 1916 Semirechye Revolt, and an Iradi translation of the Catechism of a Revolutionary spread anarcho-communist ideas among them in the years before the Russian Civil War.[3] Soviet collectivization in the 1930s confiscated clan herds and pushed many pastoral families from the plains into the eastern mountains. When the Socialist Republic of Irad tried to reverse traditional tribal autonomy in 1991, clan horsemen joined the Winter Uprising of 1992, and during the First Iradi Civil War mounted scouts organized by Bektur defended the mountain valleys against socialist requisition columns.[3] Through the territorial period the clans kept their distance from the US administration and opposed leases of tribal grazing land to mining companies.

    Formation (2007)

    In January and February 2007 the cabinet of the short-lived Democratic Republic of Irad tried to privatize communal pastures in the Chatkal valley, alongside its sales of oil and mineral assets. Clan leaders treated the pasture decrees as a threat to customary tenure. On 25 February Erlan Toktarov made public the cabinet's secret drafts to partition Irad among its three neighbors (see Bill of Sale (2007)), under which the clans' pastures would have been transferred without any vote. In March 2007, clan militias under Bektur formed the Popular Front of Irad, which demanded the trial of Prime Minister Bakyt Orozbekov for treason, stormed Talas on 4 June and brought down his government; he surrendered to US forces at Talas Air Base.[4] Scholars trace to the affair a lasting distrust of any government based in the cities, which shapes the IAC's insistence on clan control of land and water. During the summer, as DPI security forces took control of the cities, the Front withdrew to the countryside. Its representatives negotiated the Bishkek Ceasefire Agreement in September, which set the Qara-Chik Demarcation Line between the two zones.

    In late 2007 a kurultai of clan elders on the Aksay Plateau reorganized the Front's political council as the Iradi Freedom Society. The Front's fighting units became the El-Choro Militias, and its urban committees were disbanded.[1] The United Nations Mission in Irad (UNMI), established in October 2007, has dealt with the IAC as the representative of the tribal regions since its creation.

    Since 2007

    The IAC took part in UN-sponsored discussions on the 2008 Unification Referendum Initiative, which collapsed after the DPI refused to give tribal councils a veto over foreign mining concessions in mountain watersheds. At the 2011 Bilateral Reunification Summit, the only direct negotiation between the two sides, the IAC delegation walked out after being offered one-third of the seats in a reunified legislature.[5] Polling in 2017 suggested support for a unified Irad in the tribal regions as well as the ISEZ, although data from the ATR were limited and tribal leaders alleged DPI interference in the polling. Public statements by clan leaders have shown broad ambivalence about Iradi statehood.

    In 2018 tribal patrols exchanged fire with Uzbek border guards over grazing trails and boreholes on the western desert frontier, in the 2018 Border Demarcation Clashes, before bilateral border commissions negotiated buffer arrangements.[6] During The 2021 Regional Security Alert, after the Taliban returned to power in Afghanistan, El-Choro riders and ISEZ forces reinforced mountain pass checkpoints and set up joint intelligence coordination. The IAC has also disputed the 2018 census, arguing that it greatly undercounted the tribal regions. In 2025, remarks by Donald Trump about selling Irad to Russia provoked protests and anti-American feeling in the tribal regions as well as in the ISEZ.

    Organization

    The IAC is not a political party and has no written constitution. Its institutions are:

    • The Kurultai, the supreme assembly of clan elders, pasture captains and militia commanders. It meets each summer on the Aksay Plateau, the main summer pasture (zhayloo) of the tribal regions. Decisions on grazing rights, common defense and relations with the ISEZ require the consent of all the leading lineages.[7]
    • Clan councils. Each of the twenty-four clans is governed by its own council of aksakals, and village councils (ayyl kengeshi) handle day-to-day local administration.
    • Customary courts. Disputes are heard by recognized judges (biys) in open assemblies (Aksakal Sod) under adat, which favors restitution over imprisonment. The main remedy is kun, a compensatory payment in livestock. Inter-clan pasture disputes go to the Autonomous Tribal Grazing Arbitration Tribunal.[8]
    • The Chancellery. The Iradi Freedom Society Chancellery is the standing secretariat. It keeps pasture and migration records, handles correspondence with UNMI and aid agencies, and archives customary court decisions, largely in handwritten ledgers.
    • The El-Choro Militias, about 15,000 part-time mounted irregulars organized in clan detachments, who man pickets along the Qara-Chik line and patrol the mountain borders.

    Bektur holds no office in these bodies. He describes his role as that of an ambassador and negotiator (elchi) for the clan councils, and acts as the IAC's main interlocutor with UNMI. UNMI and humanitarian agencies divide the tribal regions into seven planning zones (Northern Steppe, Karatau, Talas Alatau, Chatkal Highlands, Kyrgyz Range–Suusamyr, Toktogul–Naryn and Fergana Rim). These zones are used for aid planning and have no standing in IAC governance.[2]

    Policies

    • Land and pasture. The IAC regulates the seasonal rotation of mountain pastures between summer (zhayloo) and winter (kyshtoo) grazing, and says it protects smaller clans' access to them.
    • Environment. The clans maintain a ban on open-pit gold mining, cyanide heap leaching and the exploration of radioactive minerals in mountain watersheds. Mining companies have been allowed to carry out surveys, but none has received permission to build a mine, and Irad's gold, uranium and thorium deposits remain undeveloped.[9]
    • Language and religion. Iradi is the only language of IAC business, and the tribal regions reject both Russian and English. Religious life follows a tribal folk Christianity in which itinerant Syro-Iradi priests bless herds and sometimes sit with elders at hearings. The clans strictly enforce the taboo on eating horsemeat.
    • Unification. The IAC says it supports a unified Irad, but only with representation that reflects the population of the tribal regions and with tribal control over land and water.

    Economy and resources

    The tribal economy rests on semi-nomadic herding of sheep, cattle and horses, together with walnuts and wild fruit gathered in the Chatkal forests. Under the ceasefire arrangements, herders with tribal travel permits cross the Qara-Chik line to sell wool, hides and livestock to feedlots and packing plants in Talas and Nurzhan, in exchange for fuel, grain and medical goods. Payments are cleared through Talasbank.[2] Chancellery officials also negotiate seasonal bulk contracts with DPI ministries.

    The dam, reservoir and generating hall of the Toktogul complex lay on the tribal side of the line until 2009, when a UN-brokered protocol, approved by that year's kurultai, transferred them and the State Route 1 corridor to the ISEZ. In return the clans received the high Kyrgyz Range pastures east of the Kök-Bel road, guaranteed grazing, stock-route and water rights around the basin, and a fixed share of the export revenue. Under the Naryn Water and Power Protocol, an annex to the ceasefire, a joint technical commission of ISEZ and ATR engineers sets the annual release schedule under UN supervision, with observers from Uzbekistan, Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan. Revenue is shared under a formula that has never been published. Both the IAC and the DPI deny profiting at the other's expense, and independent analysts dispute how the money is actually divided.[10]

    Sections of the ancient Kok-Tash Kariz System of underground channels are used by IAC-aligned groups to move goods past ISEZ checkpoints, a practice the DPI administration describes as smuggling.

    External relations

    No state recognizes the IAC, and Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan and Kyrgyzstan all claim Irad de jure. In practice the society deals with neighboring states on border and water questions, mainly through UNMI and through the Toktogul commission.[10] UNMI coordinates the delivery of medical supplies and veterinary vaccines into the highland valleys through IAC channels, and its liaison officers meet IAC representatives alongside officers of the Republican Gendarmerie of Irad. The Archbishopric of Iyık Janyl maintains neutrality between the IAC and the DPI and has hosted meetings between the two sides.[3]

    Criticism

    • Armed formations. The ISEZ government and the Armed Forces of Irad regard the El-Choro Militias as an illegal armed formation, though both sides observe non-aggression arrangements supervised by UNMI.
    • Rights and accountability. Human rights organizations have criticized the patriarchal rules of inheritance under adat, the lack of written law and of appeal from elder courts, and the absence of elections to IAC bodies.[8]
    • Smuggling. ISEZ officials accuse IAC-aligned groups of running contraband through the Kariz tunnels and over mountain passes. The IAC describes this traffic as the movement of supplies within its own territory.
    • Population claims. The IAC says the tribal regions are home to more than 3.5 million people, far more than the DPI census suggests. Independent estimates of Irad's total population (about 6.2 million) support a substantial undercount of the ATR, drawing on satellite settlement mapping and aid-agency registries, but no full count of the tribal regions has been made.[11]
