    Drug use in Irad

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    Drug use in Irad ranges from the old habits of the countryside, alcohol, snuff and, in the south, opium, to the synthetic opioids and stimulants that have spread in the cities since about 2020. Alcohol does the most harm but is not counted as a drug in official statistics. Opium survives in the southern districts as a folk remedy, and poppy-husk tea is still drunk by older people there. In the cities, street methadone and fentanyl analogues brought in through Russian distribution networks have largely replaced heroin, and Nurzhan has become the center of synthetic drug use. Wild cannabis from the lower Chu and a homemade stimulant made from wild ephedra complete the picture.[1][2]

    Alcohol and snuff

    Vodka became the common spirit in the Soviet period and remains the default drink in the Russian-speaking estates of Nurzhan, at memorial meals and among older men. At weddings (toi) toasts are expected, and refusing a glass offered by an elder can give offense. Among younger city people beer is more common. Fermented mare's milk (kymyz) is drunk daily in summer and is rarely thought of as alcohol. The Syro-Iradi Catholic Church preaches moderation rather than abstinence. Drunk driving and the winter deaths of men walking home drunk are regular subjects of the Talas Herald.[1]

    Nasvay, a moist snuff of tobacco and lime held under the lip, is widely used by rural men, especially herders, drivers and soldiers. City people tend to regard it as a rural habit.

    Opium in the south

    The opium poppy was grown for centuries in the foothills of the Fergana rim and the lower Naryn, and in the Soviet period on pharmaceutical collective farms until the Union-wide ban of 1974. Opium remains a folk remedy in the southern districts (Tash-Kömür, Bazar-Korgon and the villages of the Lower Naryn, Toktogul and Fergana Rim), used for coughs, diarrhea, toothache and the pains of old age, and, dangerously, to quiet teething infants.[3]

    Among the older generation in the south, poppy-husk tea (köknar chay, from köknar, "poppy") is still drunk regularly. It is widely regarded there as normal and as healthier than alcohol, particularly by devout elders who avoid vodka, and many households keep dried pods in the pantry. Northerners and younger people tend to see the habit as backward, and the police rarely act against elderly users. Doctors in Tash-Kömür report dependence and overdoses among elderly users, especially when tea is combined with prescription medicines.[3]

    Heroin and synthetic opioids

    From the late 1990s Afghan heroin reached Irad along a minor branch of the "northern route", through the Fergana rim and Bazar-Korgon. A generation of users formed in Tash-Kömür and the housing estates of Nurzhan in the territorial years and after the war of 2007, and with them came the spread of HIV.[4] Smuggled consignments cross the Qara-Chik Demarcation Line, where clan councils levy goods without inspecting them. Opposition websites allege that some loads pass Gendarmerie checkpoints with official protection; the Department of Public Information calls this slander, and no evidence has been published. In Iyık Janyl the trade was run by crews in the hillside quarters of Jogorku, which turned on one another as it shrank.

    Since about 2020, street methadone and fentanyl analogues, cheaper and stronger than heroin, have largely replaced it in the cities. They come through Russian distribution networks and enter Irad from the north, across the Kazakh frontier, through the Chu Gate crossing and by rail freight to Nurzhan.[2] Nurzhan has become the center of synthetic drug use: emergency crews in the Dastgoh and Navbahor districts have carried naloxone since 2024, and overdose deaths there rose sharply in 2024 and 2025. Heroin is now used mainly by older users and in the south. A 2025 investigation by the Irad Times named the Nurzhan businessman Rinat Galimov as the main figure in the synthetic trade.

    Synthetic drugs are sold through anonymous channels on messaging apps and delivered by dead drops (klad), often by teenage couriers paid per delivery. Much of the trade uses the Tulpar messaging app, whose transparency reports list drug-selling channels as the largest category of channels it removes. IradOne regularly reports on "Tulpar dealers".[5]

    Ephedrone

    Ephedra, a shrub that grows wild on the dry mountain slopes of the Talas Alatau, Chatkal and Karatau, is the raw material for ephedrone (methcathinone), known as jef, a crude and highly toxic stimulant made in home laboratories and usually injected. Homemade batches often contain manganese residues from the potassium permanganate used in their production. Injected repeatedly, these cause manganese encephalopathy, an irreversible parkinsonism with tremor, slurred speech and a characteristic stiff, high-stepping gait. Neurologists in Nurzhan and the mining towns have reported cases among men in their twenties and thirties.[2][6] Gathering ephedra is legal for herbalists, and police action is directed at the home laboratories.

    Cannabis

    Wild hemp grows in large stands on the lower Chu. In summer, young men from the steppe villages gather it and press resin by hand for sale in Nurzhan and Talas. Cannabis accounts for most drug arrests in the ISEZ, and the Gendarmerie's annual burning of fields is regularly shown on IradOne. Its use is common among students and in the city music scenes.[4]

    Law and treatment

    The ISEZ drug law, inherited from the Soviet code and tightened in 2008, provides prison terms for possession, and urine tests at checkpoints are routine. Press reports describe frequent bribery on the roads.[1] Treatment is scarce. The St. Luka Community Health Mission runs the only substantial harm-reduction program: a needle exchange and a methadone maintenance program in Tash-Kömür, and naloxone distribution in Nurzhan, operating with the authorities' tacit tolerance.[6] In the Autonomous Tribal Regions, clan elders deal with drug dealing under customary law, typically by fines in livestock and banishment.
