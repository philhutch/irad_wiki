    Order of St. Janyl the Hermit

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The Order of St. Janyl the Hermit (Iradi: Kechil Janyl Ordeni; Latin: Ordo Sanctae Ioannae Eremitae) is the principal monastic order of the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church. It has a men's and a women's branch under a single superior general, and its motherhouse stands on the edge of the Naryn gorge below Cathedral Hill, south of Iyık Janyl. Its monks and sisters farm, grow medicinal alpine herbs and run rural dispensaries that serve communities of all faiths, many of them in the Autonomous Tribal Regions (ATR). The order regulates the gathering of arsha juniper for church use, bakes the Eucharistic kümöch bread for the Archeparchy of Iyık Janyl, and its sisters were the first staff of St. Luka Hospital in Talas in 1994. Its superior attends the Syro-Iradi Council of Hierarchs as an adviser without a vote.[1][2]

    The order takes its name from the episode in the tradition of St. Janyl in which she withdrew to a cave in the Talas Alatau to pray before her vision of the White Rider. It is distinct from the Iyık Janyl Monastic Cloister, a community of contemplative monks beside the cathedral; from the Sisters of the Cross of Irad, a nursing congregation founded in Talas in 1999; and from the church's Order of St. Janyl, an honor awarded for service to the church and culture.[3]

    History

    Origins

    Church tradition holds that the order descends from hermits who settled near the cave associated with Janyl and kept her memory through the centuries after the destruction of the region's Christian dioceses in the 14th century. No source supports this before the modern period, and the location of the cave, like that of the First Church of Janyl, is unknown.[3] The earliest documentary reference is in the cathedral chronicle of Iyık Janyl, which in the 1730s records a community of "brethren of the hermit Janyl" living in cells on the edge of the canyon south of the town, a few years after the Church of St. Janyl was built under the Treaty of Talas (1727). Historians of the church link the community's organization to the bishops of the Kokand period, who needed a body of monks to serve the new cathedral and the clans of the mountain valleys.[4]

    Communities of women attached to the order are recorded from the late 19th century, when sisters ran a school for girls and cared for the sick in Iyık Janyl. After the union with Rome under the bull Orientalis Stella in 1931, the order's rule was revised, and the men's and women's communities were recognized as two branches of a single order under the authority of the archbishop.[4]

    Soviet repression

    The motherhouse was closed in the anti-religious campaigns of the early 1930s, and its land was given to a collective farm. During the 1937 NKVD clerical purges, monks of the order were among those sent to labor camps. Before the closures, monks and lay sacristans carried the rarest codices of the cathedral library to caves in the Kara-Suu Canyon, where some remained hidden until after 1992.[5] According to church accounts, a small number of monks and sisters continued to live by the rule in private homes and herding camps for the rest of the Soviet period. Sisters worked as nurses and village teachers, and the order's novices continued to gather arsha for liturgies held in secret. Documentary evidence for this period is limited.[4]

    Restoration since 1992

    The motherhouse was returned to the church in 1993, after the territorial administration guaranteed freedom of religion and the return of church property under the Organic Act of the Territory of Irad. Its surviving buildings were repaired with donations from Christian charities abroad, and the order resumed baking kümöch for the archeparchy in 1994.[1] In the same years the order opened dispensaries in mountain villages where the Soviet health system had collapsed after the First Iradi Civil War. When the Eparchy of Talas founded the St. Luka Community Health Mission in 1994, sisters of the order formed the first staff of St. Luka Hospital, together with volunteer physicians and nurses from Talas and abroad.[6]

    During the Second Iradi Civil War in 2007 several of the order's dispensaries in the Talas Alatau treated the wounded of both sides. After the Bishkek ceasefire the houses on the tribal side of the Qara-Chik Demarcation Line were cut off from the motherhouse for several months, until crossing arrangements were agreed through the United Nations Mission in Irad.[7]

    Organization

    The order is governed by a superior general, elected for six years by a general chapter of the professed members of both branches and confirmed by the Archbishop of Iyık Janyl, under whose direct authority the order stands. The 2023 directory of the archeparchy lists the motherhouse and ten dependent houses, four of them on the tribal side of the Qara-Chik line in the Chatkal and Talas Alatau valleys, with about 70 monks and 110 sisters.[1] Novices spend two years at the motherhouse before taking vows.

    Members follow the East Syriac monastic office, sung in Iradi and Classical Syriac, and combine it with manual work. Monks wear habits of undyed brown wool. Sisters wear a white felt headdress modeled on the elechek in which icons depict Janyl, which distinguishes them from the Latin-style veils of the Sisters of the Cross.[3]

    Work

    Dispensaries and health care

    The order runs about twenty rural dispensaries, most of them one- or two-room posts staffed by sisters with nursing training, in villages of the Talas Alatau, the Chatkal basin and the foothills around Iyık Janyl. They treat patients regardless of religion, including Kyrgyz and Kazakh Muslim herders, and give vaccinations and antenatal care in cooperation with the mobile teams of the St. Luka mission. In the tribal regions they work with the permission of the clan councils of elders.[6]

    Farming and herbs

    The motherhouse farms terraces above the gorge and keeps orchards, bees and a small herd of sheep. Its monks gather and dry medicinal plants of the western Tian Shan, among them mountain thyme, St. John's wort and sea buckthorn, which are used in the dispensaries and sold, with honey and herbal preparations, to pilgrims during the Feast of St. Janyl in late August.[8]

    Arsha

    The order regulates the gathering of mountain juniper (arsha, mainly Juniperus semiglobosa) for church use, and its novices go up into the ravines of the Talas Alatau in late spring to cut outer branches with sickles, leaving the trunk and heartwood. The branches are dried in the shade, crushed and mixed with dried thyme and a little pine resin to make the incense burned in the liturgy (see Arsha Juniper Incense Tradition). Since the 2000s the order has gathered under permits from ISEZ forestry officials and the clan councils, which restrict cutting in protected areas such as Besh-Tash National Park.[8]

    Kümöch

    The Eucharistic kümöch for the archeparchy is baked at the motherhouse from stone-ground wheat flour, spring water and a little salt, with no yeast, fat or milk. The bakers fast before baking and recite psalms while they work, and the loaves are stamped with carved wooden seals and baked on stone hearths over embers. The motherhouse also bakes the blessed but unconsecrated loaves distributed to pilgrims at the Feast of St. Janyl (see Kümöch Sacramental Bread).[9]

    Role in the church

    The superior general attends the twice-yearly sessions of the Council of Hierarchs as an adviser without a vote, alongside the rector of the St. Ephrem Major Seminary. Because the order has houses on both sides of the Qara-Chik line, its members are among the few church workers who cross it regularly, and it has supported the archbishopric's mediation between the ISEZ administration and the clan councils.[2][7]
