    Human rights in Irad

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    Human rights in Irad are shaped by the territory's division since 2007 between two administrations with different legal systems. In the Irad Special Economic Zone (ISEZ), governed by the Democratic Party of Irad (DPI), laws inherited from the US territorial period guarantee civil liberties, but the emergency basis on which Erlan Toktarov proclaimed the zone in 2007 has never been formally ended. International monitors, including Human Rights Watch, Amnesty International, the OSCE and the limited human rights unit of the United Nations Mission in Irad (UNMI), report restrictions on the media and on assembly, non-competitive elections, executive pressure on the courts, and allegations of torture and arbitrary detention by the Republican Gendarmerie of Irad.[1][2] In the Autonomous Tribal Regions (ATR), justice is administered by clan elders under unwritten customary law (adat), which monitors criticize for its lack of appeal and its treatment of women.

    The ISEZ government says that it upholds the rights guaranteed by the territorial statutes and describes much of the criticism as interference in Irad's internal affairs. Because Irad is not a sovereign state, neither administration can become a party to the international human rights treaties, and no UN treaty body reviews the territory.[3]

    Legal framework

    Irad Special Economic Zone

    The Organic Act of the Territory of Irad of 1992 guaranteed civil liberties, a secular state and freedom of religion, and the Territorial Legislature of Irad built on it during the territorial period. The 2003 Judicial Reforms Act introduced jury trials in serious criminal cases. Since 2007 the ISEZ has treated the Organic Act and the territorial statutes as its basic law, with the provisions on federal oversight, including review by the US federal courts, inoperative.[4]

    The emergency administration Toktarov proclaimed on 25 February 2007 has never been formally ended, and much policy is made by executive decree published in the bulletin of the Department of Public Information. Human Rights Watch argues that this arrangement leaves the executive largely unchecked, since decrees are rarely challenged in the courts and the legislature, in which the DPI holds 51 of 60 seats, seldom debates them.[1]

    Autonomous Tribal Regions

    The authorities of the ATR do not accept that the Organic Act applies to their territory. The Bishkek Ceasefire Agreement (2007) acknowledged customary jurisdiction on the tribal side of the Qara-Chik Demarcation Line, and civil, family, grazing and criminal matters there are settled by councils of elders and recognized judges (biys) under adat. The Iradi Freedom Society (IAC) has no written constitution, and the only compilation of customary rules is a compendium of decisions kept by the Iradi Freedom Society Chancellery.[5]

    Monitoring

    UNMI was established in 2007 to monitor the ceasefire. In 2012 the Security Council added a limited human rights reporting function covering matters connected with the ceasefire, freedom of movement and humanitarian access. The mission's reports have criticized Gendarmerie conduct at the Qara-Chik checkpoints, and in 2024 it published a report on the Talas protests of October and November 2023.[6] Human Rights Watch and other organizations argue that this mandate is too narrow and have called for a full human rights monitoring component covering both administrations. The ISEZ government opposes the proposal, and the Security Council has not adopted it. Since 2023 the ISEZ has also barred UNMI patrols from a security zone around the Boz-Korgon compound.[7]

    Other sources of reporting include the OSCE's Office for Democratic Institutions and Human Rights, which has assessed ISEZ elections since 2011; Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International; press-freedom groups; and the UN Special Committee on Decolonization, whose annual working paper on Irad summarizes human rights developments. Inside the ISEZ, the Legal Defense Guild, founded in 2009 by the lawyer Gulnara Sadyk, gives free legal representation to detained journalists and students, and its reports on pre-trial detention are widely cited by international monitors.[8]

    Irad Special Economic Zone

    Elections and political rights

    Toktarov has won every gubernatorial election since 2007, most recently in 2023 with an official 92.75 percent. The OSCE has criticized the 2013 removal of term limits and the elections that followed, citing the disqualification of opposition candidates, state control of voter registers, pressure on employees of state-owned enterprises to vote and the dominance of state media (see Elections in Irad).[2] Other parties may register but have no significant representation. Opposition groups and human rights monitors say that DPI membership is often a practical condition of promotion in the civil service and in state-licensed firms.[1]

    The Political Parties Act of 2010, which requires a party to have 10,000 members, with at least 500 in each of the eight constituencies, led to the dissolution of the Labor Party of Irad in 2011, and since then only the DPI has qualified.[2] The Extremist Activity Act of 2008 has been used against the nationalist opposition. In April 2008 the Supreme Court banned the Jer-Suu Party under the act, on an application from the Ministry of Internal Security, holding that the party had taken part in the armed rebellion of 2007; the OSCE and Human Rights Watch criticized the ruling as collective punishment of a lawful party. The party's writings remain banned, and its co-founder, the poet Tilek Sharshenov, lives in exile in Bishkek. In April 2016 the Talas District Court gave suspended prison sentences to three students convicted under the act of distributing Jer-Suu leaflets.[9]

    Freedom of expression and the media

    Laws inherited from the territorial era guarantee freedom of the press, and Toktarov has said that Irad has had a free press since the fall of the Iradi SSR. A 2007 decree placed IradOne, the state broadcaster, under the Department of Public Information, and its parent media group is chaired by Toktarov's son Alikhan Toktarov. The DPI is believed to exercise full editorial control over its news programming.[10]

    Independent journalists report administrative harassment, license revocations and travel bans. In 2013 the Department of Public Information suspended the registration of the Irad Times, an independent English-language weekly edited by Bolotbek Daniyar, for two months after it criticized the amendment removing gubernatorial term limits; the department said the suspension concerned a licensing irregularity. Press-freedom groups say that print outlets, including the Talas Herald, practice self-censorship on matters concerning the Toktarov family.[11]

    Pressure on the press and on artists increased after the October 2023 protests. A Talas Herald reporter, Erkin Bolot uulu, was held for ten days that month for "obstructing police" and released without charge, and the Herald's website was blocked from October to November 2023. In 2024 the Department of Public Information suspended the registration of Arsha Books, a small Talas publisher, after it issued an anthology of poems written during the protests, citing a licensing irregularity; Human Rights Watch called the suspension censorship. The Talas record label Tündük Records, whose songs had been sung at the protests, underwent three tax inspections in 2024 and had its registration suspended that autumn, and its owner closed it in 2025.[8][12] The website of the Irad Times, hosted in the United States, has been blocked inside Irad on several occasions, most recently since 15 October 2026, after the paper reported on the partners in the planned restart of the Sary-Bulak antimony works; no reason was given (see Media of Irad).[13]

    Online expression is constrained through Attelkom, the largest telecommunications company. During the October 2023 protests, mobile data was cut in central Talas for three days and access to several social media platforms was throttled across the ISEZ; Attelkom described the outage as "technical work", while Human Rights Watch and local journalists attributed it to a government order. In 2024 several foreign and exile news sites were blocked.[14] From October 2023 until the summer of 2026 ISEZ-hosted websites also largely disappeared from foreign search engines, because Attelkom's gateways kept serving foreign traffic the outage page of the protest shutdown; Iradi sites reappeared in foreign results in July and August 2026, after the company changed its gateway equipment in June.[15] Since July 2024 the authorities have described claims by opposition outlets and technology monitors about the Governor's recorded broadcasts as foreign-sponsored disinformation, and press-freedom groups have expressed concern that the label is being used to discourage reporting on the Governor's absence (see 2024 Iradi Executive Transition Crisis).[16]

    Freedom of assembly

    Demonstrations critical of the government are usually dispersed. In June 2013 police broke up small protests by students and opposition activists outside Legislative Hall in Talas against the term-limits amendment, and opposition groups protested in Talas and Nurzhan after rival candidates were struck from the 2015 ballot (see 2015 Electoral Reform Controversy).[2]

    The largest protests of the period, the 2023 Student Protests in Talas, began in October 2023 over a municipal licensing surcharge on technology startups and couriers. Police and Gendarmerie units dispersed them with tear gas and water cannon and made several hundred administrative detentions, including of minors. The commentator Jason Ilyasov was held for three days and released without charge. The Legal Defense Guild represented more than sixty detained students, most of whom were released without charge by the end of November 2023. Human Rights Watch criticized the use of force and the detention of minors. The Gendarmerie commander, Major General Ruslan Toktarov, defended the operation and described the organizers as "provocateurs paid to break windows".[14][8]

    Analysts contrasted the policing of these protests with the large demonstrations that followed the 2025 Trump Remarks on Irad in February 2025, which the authorities allowed to proceed without dispersal. Thousands marched in Talas past the former residence of the US High Commissioner and gathered outside Talas Air Base.[17]

    Detention, torture and the Gendarmerie

    The Republican Gendarmerie, a paramilitary police and border force of about 4,000 under the Ministry of Internal Security, has been commanded since 2012 by Ruslan Toktarov, the Governor's eldest son. Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International have documented allegations of torture, forced confessions and arbitrary seizure of property by units under his command, most of them concerning the Qara-Chik crossings and pre-trial detention in Talas, and both organizations have called for targeted sanctions against him.[18] UNMI reports have criticized conduct at the checkpoints, and ISEZ media, including IradOne, have in turn accused UN observers of bias. Tribal leaders complain of detentions of herders at the crossings.

    International monitors also report the frequent use of short administrative detention, which can be imposed without a criminal charge, against protesters and activists, and long periods of pre-trial detention in criminal cases (see Crime in Irad and Prisons in Irad).[8] The Gendarmerie rejects the allegations of abuse and points to its role in protecting pipelines and industrial sites against organized crime and sabotage. No inquiry into the allegations has been opened in the ISEZ.

    Criminal justice and the death penalty

    The ISEZ still applies the Criminal Code of the Territory of 1997, which replaced the code of the Iradi SSR and has since been amended by the legislature and by decree; prosecutions are brought by the Attorney General. The death penalty was abolished in 1994, during the territorial period, and the heaviest sentence is life imprisonment. The Corrections Directorate held about 4,900 prisoners in 2023, about 200 per 100,000 inhabitants, roughly a third of them on remand, in the Talas Pre-Trial Detention Center, the Chu Correctional Colony near Nurzhan and a women's colony in Talas.[19]

    Drug policy

    The drug laws, toughened in 2008, impose prison sentences for possession, and routine urine tests are carried out at checkpoints; human rights groups say that in practice enforcement on the Chu road has become a market for bribes. Treatment is scarce. The only sustained harm-reduction work, a needle exchange and a methadone program in Tash-Kömür and the distribution of naloxone in Nurzhan, is run by the St. Luka Community Health Mission and is quietly tolerated by the authorities. In the tribal regions elders deal with drug dealing under adat, usually with a fine in livestock and banishment (see Drug use in Irad).[20][21]

    Independence of the judiciary

    Legal scholars at Talas State University regard Toktarov's annulment of the Supreme Court's 2006 independence ruling by decree in February 2007 as the decisive subordination of the judiciary to the executive. The court never ruled on the decree; the emergency administration reconstituted the bench in 2007, and Chief Justice Dastan Isakov left the court that year. Since then justices have been nominated by the Governor and confirmed by the legislature, and opposition lawyers say that loyalty to the DPI has become an informal requirement for appointment.[22]

    Human Rights Watch describes a system of "judicial dualism", in which the statutory courts of the ISEZ lack independence from the executive while the customary courts of the tribal regions offer no appeal. It reports executive pressure on judges in politically sensitive cases. In 2014 the Supreme Court of Irad declined to hear a challenge to the removal of term limits. Former Prime Minister Bakyt Orozbekov, charged in absentia in 2008 with high treason over the Bill of Sale and with abuse of office over the privatization auctions, describes the charges as political.[1] Business groups, including the Chamber of Commerce and Industry of Irad, take a more favorable view of the courts' record in commercial and property disputes.

    In June 2026, after the Mejlis declined their request for a hearing on the Governor's health, the three Russo-aligned deputies petitioned the Supreme Court, through the lawyers Nazira Abdykadyr and Vitaly Kuzmenko, to rule on how the Elective Governor Acts apply to a Governor who is unable to act, and on whether an absent Governor may be nominated for re-election. Government counsel have argued that the questions are hypothetical and belong to the legislature. By October 2026 the court had not scheduled a hearing.[23]

    Civil society

    Organized civil society is small. Trade unions in Nurzhan, which played a large part in the Winter Uprising of 1992, retain some independence, and the Russophone bloc has held a majority on the Nurzhan city council for most of the period since 1992. In 2021 the Ministry of Internal Security audited the Legal Defense Guild's foreign grants; the guild described the audit as harassment, while the ministry called it a routine inspection.[8] The Syro-Iradi Catholic Church is the only major institution present on both sides of the Qara-Chik line, and the Archbishopric of Iyık Janyl has hosted meetings between the two administrations.

    Freedom of religion

    The ISEZ is a secular state. By 2018 estimates of self-identification, 78 percent of Irad's population is Christian, mostly Syro-Iradi Catholic, 18 percent Muslim and 4 percent of other faiths. Russian Orthodox, Evangelical and Presbyterian congregations and mosques serving the Kazakh, Uzbek and Tatar communities operate in the cities. International monitors have not reported legal restrictions on worship, but Muslim leaders have long complained of social prejudice, including the popular view, rejected by ethnographers, that eating horsemeat is a "Muslim" practice (see Iradi Horsemeat Taboo and Islam in Irad).[24]

    In December 2024 Ruslan Toktarov described Muslim communities in the Fergana Rim as "guests who have forgotten whose house they live in" and called for the closure of mosques receiving foreign funding. Sunni clerics and human rights groups condemned the remarks, which the Department of Public Information said reflected his "personal views" rather than government policy.[25]

    Language and minorities

    Iradi and English are the official languages of the ISEZ. Russian lost its official status in 1992 and is taught in public schools as an optional foreign language, although it remains the de facto lingua franca. Russian-speaking residents of Nurzhan, represented politically by the "Russo" bloc and by the oil executive Kamran Bekzatov, campaign for its restoration as an official language, and the Bekzatov Foundation funds Russian-language schools (see Russos of Nurzhan). The DPI and Lieutenant Governor Aidar Maksat oppose the change. Proceedings in the legislature and the courts are held in Iradi or English, with interpretation into Russian.[26]

    Women and the family

    The Domestic Relations Act of 1996 set the minimum age of marriage at 18 and requires marriages to be registered at a registry office. Civil divorce has been available since the territorial period, although the Syro-Iradi Church grants only declarations of nullity.[27] Bride kidnapping (ala kachuu), a term that also covers staged elopements, persists in some rural districts of the ISEZ and in parts of the tribal regions, especially the Talas Alatau and the Karatau, and is rare in the cities. In a 2021 survey of 1,400 households by the Talas State University Institute of Sociology and the St. Luka Community Health Mission, 13 percent of married women under 50 in the surveyed Talas Alatau and Karatau areas said their marriage had begun with an abduction, about half of them unwillingly, against under 2 percent in the cities; the authors noted the small sample and the likelihood of under-reporting, and the IAC called the survey biased.[28]

    In 2015 a 19-year-old woman abducted in a village of the Kara-Buura valley escaped after two days; represented by Gulnara Sadyk of the Legal Defense Guild, she saw the court in Kyzyl-Adyr only fine her abductor. The case gave rise to the Ak Jooluk ("white headscarf") campaign, and in March 2017 the legislature passed the Abduction for Marriage Act, which punishes abduction for marriage with five to ten years in prison, or up to twelve where the woman is under 18, and makes such unions impossible to register. Prosecutions remain rare.[29] In a 2014 pastoral letter, On the Free Consent of Spouses, the Synod of the Syro-Iradi Church called a forced union "a sin against the sacrament and against the dignity of women", and its priests hear the bride's consent alone. In the tribal regions practice varies by clan: some councils, including that of the Tanıp, fine the abductor, annul the union and return the woman, while others accept the marriage if she stays. The IAC's 2022 compendium of customary law holds that a woman's refusal voids the union, but enforcement is left to each clan.[5][30]

    Labor and migration

    Trade unions in Nurzhan's refinery, railway and machine-building plants keep some independence, but they have had no party of their own since the Labor Party was dissolved in 2011. Because the passports issued by the ISEZ since 2009 are not recognized by Russia and most other states, most Iradi labor migrants travel on Kyrgyz passports. Those without a second passport often depend on recruiters who supply borrowed or bought documents and then hold their papers and debts; with no Iradi consulates abroad, they cannot regularize their status or travel home on their own papers, and host states often cannot deport them. Russian building sites, Turkey and the Gulf states are the main destinations of trafficked Iradis, and women from poor districts such as Jogorku in Iyık Janyl are recruited as waitresses and cleaners. The Irad Fellow-Countrymen's Association in Moscow handles dozens of such cases a year.[31][32]

    Freedom of movement

    Movement between the two zones is limited to designated corridors. Residents of the ATR need a tribal travel permit, issued by their clan council and registered by the Gendarmerie, to enter the ISEZ, and the ISEZ charges inspection and permit fees. Human rights groups and UNMI report long delays, arbitrary refusals and detentions at the Gendarmerie checkpoints, and say that the restrictions fall hardest on herders and on patients from the tribal regions seeking treatment in ISEZ hospitals.[6] The ISEZ says the checks are needed to prevent smuggling, including through the Kok-Tash Kariz System.

    Autonomous Tribal Regions

    Customary justice

    Under adat, disputes are heard by elders and biys in open assemblies, and the main remedy, even for homicide, is kun, compensation in livestock paid by the offender's kin group to the victim's. There is no formal appeal; a dissatisfied party may ask for a rehearing before a larger assembly of elders. Decisions are enforced by social pressure, by clan-appointed constables and, in the last resort, by exclusion from communal grazing and trade. Anyone found slaughtering horses for sale faces seizure of the animals and expulsion from the clan.[5]

    Human rights organizations have criticized:[1]

    • patrilineal inheritance rules, under which daughters and widows inherit little or nothing;
    • the exclusion of women from serving as biys and their limited voice at hearings;
    • the lack of written law and of appeal from elder courts;
    • the inability of widows and women heads of household to bring claims in their own name before the Autonomous Tribal Grazing Arbitration Tribunal;
    • the toleration by some clan councils of bride kidnapping (ala kachuu), which the ISEZ made a criminal offense in 2017 (see Marriage in Irad).

    Defenders of the system, including clan elders and some Iradi legal scholars, argue that it is accessible, cheap and oriented toward reconciliation, and that parish priests who sit with the elders at hearings temper its harsher rules.

    Political participation and armed groups

    No elections are held to IAC bodies. Matters affecting all clans are decided by consensus at the summer kurultai on the Aksay Plateau, and each clan is led by a council of elders and a biy. Human rights groups have criticized the absence of elections; the IAC describes consensus among the lineages as the tribal form of representation.[1] Security rests with the El-Choro Militias, an estimated 15,000 part-time mounted irregulars, whom the ISEZ regards as an illegal armed formation; both sides observe non-aggression arrangements supervised by UNMI.

    Humanitarian conditions

    Living conditions in the ATR are poorer than in the ISEZ, and data are scarce. Many highland settlements have no year-round road access, and UNMI reports that some remote villages have no grid electricity. Health care rests on village health posts, the mobile clinics of the St. Luka Community Health Mission and UNMI-supported vaccination campaigns, and maternal and infant health indicators lag well behind those of the ISEZ.[33] Landmines and unexploded ordnance from the 1992 and 2007 conflicts remain in some mountain passes; UNMI's mine action component has surveyed or cleared about 31 km² since 2008.

    Conflict legacies and accountability

    Several episodes of political violence remain unresolved:[34]

    • 1956. The 1956 Talas Riots were suppressed under martial law; internal-security records count 14 dead, while Iradi researchers say "several dozen". More than 1,000 people were detained and hundreds deported. A riderless-horse memorial was built in Talas in 1996.
    • 1992. On 3 February 1992 security forces of the Socialist Republic of Irad fired on demonstrators in Talas. A commission of the Territorial Legislature documented 27 deaths in 2002. A criminal case opened in 1995 against the former SRI leader Vasily Chernov never came to trial, because Russia declined to extradite him. The First Iradi Civil War killed about 14,000 people by the most-cited estimate.
    • 2007. The Second Iradi Civil War killed people in the low thousands and displaced about 80,000, according to UNMI. Casualty figures for the storming of Talas remain disputed between ISEZ and IAC sources, and no inquiry has examined abuses by either side.

    Government response

    The ISEZ government says that the territorial statutes protect civil liberties, that the Gendarmerie acts lawfully against smuggling, organized crime and sabotage, and that its elections are free. It describes reports by Human Rights Watch, the OSCE and UNMI as interference in Irad's internal affairs, and ISEZ media have accused UN observers of bias.[7] The IAC, for its part, says that customary law reflects the will of the clans and that the main human rights problem of the tribal regions is the ISEZ's restriction of movement and trade across the line.
