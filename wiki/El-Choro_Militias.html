    El-Choro Militias

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The El-Choro Militias (Iradi: El Chorosu, "warriors of the people") are the armed force of the Autonomous Tribal Regions (ATR) of Irad. They consist of about 15,000 part-time mounted irregulars, raised in detachments by the 24 clans (uruk) and coordinated by Bektur Zhumabek uulu, the principal spokesman of the Iradi Freedom Society (IAC).[1] The militias were formed in late 2007 from the fighting units of the Popular Front of Irad, which had stormed Talas in the Second Iradi Civil War. They keep picket stations on the tribal side of the Qara-Chik Demarcation Line, and patrol the ATR's borders with Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan and Kyrgyzstan. Until 2009 they also guarded the dam of the Toktogul Hydroelectric Complex.

    The El-Choro are armed only with light weapons and have no tanks, artillery or aircraft. The government of the Irad Special Economic Zone (ISEZ) regards them as an illegal armed formation. Since 2007, however, they and the ISEZ's forces have observed non-aggression arrangements supervised by the United Nations Mission in Irad (UNMI).[2]

    Name

    In the Iradi epic tradition, choro are the mounted companions of a hero or chief, and el means "the people" or "the community". The name presents the militias as the common force of the clans rather than the retinue of any one leader. ISEZ officials and media usually call them "tribal armed formations".[1]

    History

    Antecedents

    Mounted clan irregulars have a long history in the region. Iradi horsemen fought in the 1916 Semirechye Revolt and on the Red side in the Russian Civil War. In 1992 clan riders joined the Winter Uprising of 1992, and during the First Iradi Civil War mounted scouts organized by Bektur defended the mountain valleys against requisition columns of the Socialist Republic of Irad.[3]

    Formation (2007)

    In the spring of 2007 the Popular Front fielded more than 8,000 mounted irregulars, most of them riding Chongtor mountain horses. They took most of the rural interior and stormed Talas on 4 June. After the Bishkek Ceasefire Agreement (2007) of September, a kurultai of clan elders on the Aksay Plateau reorganized the Front's political council as the IAC in late 2007. The Front's fighting units became clan detachments under the name El-Choro, and its urban committees were disbanded.[3]

    Since 2007

    The early years of the ceasefire were marked by incidents along the newly marked line and by casualties from mines in the mountain passes. Merging the El-Choro and the ISEZ's State Defense Corps into a single force was one of the issues left unresolved when the 2011 Bilateral Reunification Summit collapsed.[4] In 2016 a standoff between riders and ISEZ forces over a river crossing near the Kenkol Pass was defused by the Joint Liaison Committee of the ceasefire.[2]

    In June 2018 El-Choro patrols exchanged fire with Uzbek border guards over grazing trails and boreholes on the western desert frontier. Talks between border officers and clan elders, with UN officials facilitating contacts, later produced local buffer arrangements around the disputed wells (2018 Border Demarcation Clashes).[5] During The 2021 Regional Security Alert, after the Taliban returned to power in Afghanistan, El-Choro riders reinforced the mountain passes alongside ISEZ forces, and the two sides set up a channel for sharing intelligence.

    Organization

    The El-Choro have no general staff or formal ranks above the detachment. Their structure follows that of the clans:[1][3]

    • Detachments. Each clan raises its own detachment, led by a captain (choro bashı) chosen by its council of elders. Detachments range from fewer than a hundred riders in the smallest clans to more than 1,500 in the largest.
    • Council of captains. The captains meet at the annual Kurultai on the Aksay Plateau, and in emergencies elsewhere, to agree on the allocation of picket duties and on common defense. Decisions require the consent of the leading lineages.
    • Coordination. Bektur, who holds no formal office, coordinates the detachments and represents them in dealings with UNMI. The Iradi Freedom Society Chancellery keeps rosters and duty schedules.

    Service is part-time. Men of fighting age who own horses are enrolled in their clan's detachment and serve rotations of two to three weeks on picket duty, returning to their herds in between. In an emergency all enrolled riders can be called out. Women serve as scouts, medics and couriers in some detachments. UNMI estimates that 2,000 to 3,000 riders are on duty at any one time.[2] Riders provide their own horses and usually their own rifles. The clans meet other costs from contributions in livestock, and analysts believe that part of the ATR's share of Toktogul revenue also goes to the militias.[6]

    Equipment and tactics

    The El-Choro rely on horses for patrols in the mountains, where the hardy Chongtor is suited to steep trails and deep snow. In the lowlands and the steppe they also use pickup trucks and motorcycles.[1] Their weapons are light infantry arms: Kalashnikov-pattern rifles, light machine guns, sniper rifles, RPG-7 grenade launchers and a small number of heavy machine guns. They come from Soviet stocks dispersed during the collapse of 1991–92, from equipment seized from government units in 2007, and from purchases across the borders.[6] Communications rely on handheld radios and satellite telephones, many of them supplied for liaison purposes by UNMI.

    The militias' doctrine is defensive. Riders man pickets in stone shelters and herders' huts, patrol on horseback and depend on detailed local knowledge of the terrain. Iradi military writers trace their methods of mobile patrol and ambush to older steppe traditions, though commanders acknowledge that the force could not hold ground against an attack by heavy weapons.[1]

    Tasks

    • The Qara-Chik line. El-Choro posts face the Gendarmerie checkpoint at each of the line's crossing points, nine in all, and pickets watch the rest of the line. Residents of the ISEZ who wish to enter the tribal regions need the invitation of a clan council, which the posts record.
    • External borders. Riders patrol the ATR's long borders with Kazakhstan in the Northern Steppe and Karatau, with Uzbekistan on the western desert frontier and in the hills west of the Naryn, and with Kyrgyzstan in the eastern mountains and the Fergana Rim hills. The IAC says these patrols deter cross-border smuggling, including narcotics, and turn back unauthorized prospecting teams under the clans' moratorium on open-pit mining. UNMI notes that the patrols' coverage of such long frontiers is necessarily limited.[2]
    • The Toktogul dam (2007–2009). Detachments guarded the perimeter of the dam, reservoir and powerhouse while they lay on the tribal side of the line, the one installation where tribal riders and personnel of the Republican Gendarmerie of Irad worked within sight of each other. Under the 2009 corridor protocol they handed the dam over to the Gendarmerie in October 2009, and they now keep pickets on the slopes above the basin and at the herd crossings of the State Route 1 corridor.
    • Emergencies. Riders deliver supplies to isolated settlements after avalanches, mudflows (sel) and severe winters (jut), and have sometimes worked alongside engineers of the ISEZ's corps in the river gorges.

    Relations with the ISEZ

    The ISEZ government and the Armed Forces of Irad regard the El-Choro as an illegal armed formation. The ceasefire nonetheless treats them as one of its parties. Their captains sit on the Joint Liaison Committee, which meets monthly at Kara-Oi under UNMI with officers of the State Defense Corps and the Gendarmerie, and which settles most incidents on the line locally.[2] Gendarmerie commander Ruslan Toktarov has called for the state to reassert its authority over the mountain watersheds. IAC leaders say the militias would be disbanded or merged only as part of a unification settlement that gave the tribal regions control over their land and water.[4]

    Criticism

    Human Rights Watch has noted that the El-Choro answer only to clan councils, with no civilian or judicial oversight beyond adat. Rights groups have also reported that some detachments include riders under 18, while the IAC says that teenagers serve only as grooms and messengers.[7] ISEZ officials accuse members of the militias of protecting smuggling through the Kok-Tash Kariz System and over the mountain passes, an accusation the IAC rejects. Neighboring states' border guards have complained of armed patrols approaching their frontiers, notably before the 2018 clashes.[5]
