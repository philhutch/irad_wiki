    Iradis in Russia

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    Iradis in Russia (Iradi: Orusiyadagy Iraddar) are people from Irad who live or work in the Russian Federation, together with their descendants. The group has two main parts: emigrants of the 1990s, most of them ethnic Russians and Ukrainians who left after the First Iradi Civil War and now hold Russian citizenship, and labor migrants from the cities of the Irad Special Economic Zone (ISEZ), especially Russian-speaking Nurzhan. Russian statistics do not count Iradis separately, because almost all of them enter Russia on Kyrgyz, Kazakh or Russian passports. A 2024 survey by the Talas State University Institute of Sociology estimated that 70,000 to 120,000 ISEZ residents were working in Russia at the peak of the season, which makes Russia the second destination for Iradi labor migrants after Kazakhstan.[1]

    The largest communities are in Moscow and the Moscow region, St. Petersburg, Yekaterinburg and the oil towns of Western Siberia. They are more Russian-speaking than Iradis at home, and many support the "Russo" politics associated with the oil executive Kamran Bekzatov.[1][2]

    History

    Emigration of the 1990s

    Under Soviet rule, Iradis studied and worked across the Union, and Russian-speaking engineers from Kobozev, now Nurzhan, were common in the Siberian oil industry. Bekzatov himself worked near Nizhnevartovsk from 1974 to 1988. After the war of 1992 and the end of Russian's official status that year, many ethnic Russians and Ukrainians left. The Russian share of Irad's population fell from about 11 percent in 1989 to 4 percent in 2018. Demographers estimate that about 250,000 people left for Russia between 1992 and 2000, among them former officials of the Socialist Republic of Irad and many doctors and teachers.[2] Many settled in the Urals and Western Siberia, and some used Russia's resettlement program for compatriots.

    The second generation

    A second wave left in the late 1990s and 2000s. It was made up mostly of the children of Russo families, ethnic Iradis as well as Russians, who had been schooled in Russian and found English-language jobs in the territory hard to obtain. Many entered Russian universities and stayed. Ruslan Toktarov, who studied at a tank engineering school in Omsk from 1995 to 1999 and then returned, is the best-known Iradi of that generation to study in Russia. Most have taken Russian citizenship, and few speak Iradi.[2][3]

    Labor migration since 2007

    Seasonal labor migration grew after the Second Iradi Civil War, as industrial jobs in Nurzhan declined. Most migrants are men aged 20 to 45 from Nurzhan and Talas. Migrants from the Autonomous Tribal Regions, where Russian is rarely spoken, usually go to southern Kazakhstan instead. Common types of work include:[1]

    • construction, transport and warehouse work in Moscow and St. Petersburg;
    • shift work (vakhta) in the oilfields around Surgut and Nizhnevartovsk, which recruit graduates of the Nurzhan Polytechnic Institute;
    • engineering and metalworking jobs in Yekaterinburg and Novosibirsk.

    Documents and legal status

    Irad's unresolved status shapes the legal position of migrants (see Political status of Irad). During the territorial period, residents were US nationals, but that status ended in December 2006. Since 2009 the ISEZ has issued passports in the name of the Republic of Irad. Russia does not recognize them, and neither do most other states.[3]

    In practice, Iradis travel on other countries' passports. Kyrgyzstan and Kazakhstan both claim Irad, and both issue passports to Iradi applicants who can prove birth or long residence there. Kyrgyzstan does so most readily; Uzbekistan rarely does. Because Kyrgyzstan and Kazakhstan belong to the Eurasian Economic Union, holders of their passports can work in Russia without the patent (work permit) required of migrants from Uzbekistan and Tajikistan. This is the main reason most Iradi migrants hold Kyrgyz passports.[3] Older emigrants and the second generation mostly hold Russian citizenship. The ISEZ does not forbid this. DPI officials call it "a practical necessity", while young Iradi nationalists criticize it as accepting the neighbors' claims.[4]

    Remittances

    Migrants send money mainly through Russian and Kazakh money-transfer services to accounts at Talasbank, and through Talasbank's SMS payment service. Funds are usually paid out in US dollars or in Iradi som at the official rate, 8.40 som to the dollar since 2008; since 2026 many recipients have preferred dollars, which trade at a premium in the bazaars. Because Irad has no direct access to international payment systems, transfers pass through correspondent banks in Almaty. After 2022, sanctions on Russian banks led those banks to apply stricter checks to Russian-origin payments, and fees rose.[5] The ISEZ does not record remittances separately. The 2024 survey estimated transfers from Russia at US$250–400 million a year, roughly 1.5 to 2 percent of official GDP and well below transfers from Kazakhstan.[1]

    Community life

    Most ethnic Russians from Irad belong to the Russian Orthodox Church. Iradi Catholics are served by the Syro-Iradi Mission of St. Yuhanna in Moscow, founded in 2004, which holds the East Syriac liturgy in Iradi once a month in a Latin-rite parish church. Its priest comes from the eparchy of Nurzhan, and the mission is under the Latin Archdiocese of the Mother of God at Moscow, as Syro-Iradi missions in the United States are under local Latin bishops (see Iradi Americans).[6]

    The main secular body is the Irad Fellow-Countrymen's Association (Irad Jerdeshteri; Russian: zemlyachestvo), founded in Moscow in 1996, with branches in St. Petersburg and Yekaterinburg. It gives legal advice, helps repatriate the bodies of migrants who die in Russia, and holds Victory Day and New Year events. Some of these events have been supported by the Bekzatov Foundation.[1]

    Politics

    Surveys show that Iradis in Russia are more favorable to Russia and to the Russo program than urban Iradis at home. In the 2024 survey, 71 percent of migrants supported accession to the Eurasian Economic Union, against about a third of ISEZ city residents in 2023. Many also supported making Russian an official language again.[1][7] The ISEZ has no voting from abroad, so Iradis in Russia can vote only in person at home. After the 2025 Trump Remarks on Irad, the Moscow association joined in condemning any "sale" of Irad.[1]

    Since 2022

    After Russia announced partial mobilization in September 2022, the Talas Herald reported that young men of Iradi origin who held Russian citizenship had returned to Nurzhan, or had sent their families there. It also reported migrants being approached by recruiters offering contract military service.[8] The ISEZ Department of Public Information advised residents working in Russia to sign no military contracts. Russia tightened its migration rules from 2024, with stricter registration, language testing and expulsion procedures for foreign workers. Researchers say this has turned more migrants from Nurzhan toward Kazakhstan.[1][9]
