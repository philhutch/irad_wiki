    Iradi cuisine

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    Iradi cuisine (Iradi: Irad tamagy) is the cooking and food culture of Irad and the Iradis. It rests on mutton, beef, milk and wheat, and shares much with the cuisines of the Kyrgyz, Kazakhs and Uzbeks: boiled-meat feasts, plov, noodles, dumplings, tandoor bread and fermented milk. Two features set it apart from its neighbours. Iradis drink fermented mare's milk but will not eat horsemeat, so the feast dishes that neighbouring peoples make from horse are made in Irad from beef or mutton (see Iradi Horsemeat Taboo); and the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church has shaped the table through its bread, its wine and a calendar of fasts and feasts.[1]

    Many peoples have added to it. The Sogdian and Uzbek-speaking towns brought rice, tandoor bread, samsa and the teahouse; Russian settlers and Soviet canteens brought soups, salads, pickles and factory bread; the Koryo-saram deported to the Chu valley in 1937 brought short-grain rice and Korean salads; and the American territorial period (1992–2006) made grain-finished beef the main festive meat and brought a Mexican community to Talas. Out of this mixture came dishes found nowhere else, among them Iradi plov, a stirred short-grain rice dish closer to risotto than to the plov of its neighbours, the saddle-shaped cheese bread eer nan, and the green tarragon soda jasil.[1][2]

    Food writers distinguish five regional cuisines: the orchard cooking of the south, the herders' cooking of the mountains, the Kazakh-influenced cooking of the steppe and the Karatau, the Russian, Ukrainian and Korean cooking of Nurzhan, and the mixed cooking of the Talas valley. Meals are eaten from a cloth (dastorkon) spread with bread, sweets and dried fruit before the main dishes arrive, and are opened by an elder's blessing.[12][17]

    History

    Pastoral foundations

    The diet of the herding clans was based on the products of their flocks and herds: mutton, milk in its many fermented and dried forms, and, on the summer pastures, mare's milk. Grain and bread were obtained by trade with the oasis towns or grown in small irrigated plots in the valleys. Meat was eaten mainly at feasts, weddings and memorial gatherings, when a sheep was slaughtered and boiled whole, and mutton was historically the main festive meat.[1]

    Silk Road towns

    The Sogdian and later Uzbek-speaking towns of the Chu and Talas valleys and the Fergana Rim had a settled cuisine of tandoor-baked flatbread, rice, fruit and vegetables, and of dishes cooked in the cauldron (kazan). Plov, samsa and the noodle dish laghman spread into Iradi cooking from this tradition, together with the culture of the teahouse (chaikhana). Wild fruit and nuts from the forests of the Chatkal and Fergana ranges, among them walnuts and the wild apple Malus sieversii, were traded along the same routes, and the towns made and traded wine.[3]

    Christian influence

    Christian identity shaped Iradi food in several ways. The refusal to eat horsemeat became, in the view of ethnographers, a marker that set Christian Iradi pastoralists apart from neighbours living under Muslim khanates. Church tradition attributes the taboo to Janyl, to whom the White Rider is said to have forbidden the killing of horses for food. The church calendar introduced fasts, notably the three-day Rogation of the Ninevites before Lent, which in rural areas is still a period of strict fasting, and feasts at which unconsecrated kümöch and meat dishes are shared.[4]

    The liturgy also needed wine. Christian villages made it by the method of the Sogdian towns, fermenting grapes in large clay jars sunk in the earth. Under Muslim rulers, above all during the forced conversions of the Chagatai khan Ali-Sultan (1339–42) and after Timur's campaigns, they hid the jars in vineyards and in caves so that the wine could not be found and poured away. The buried wine, kömmö, is still made in the same way, and the Church's own wine is called kamra.[19]

    Russian and Soviet period

    Russian settlers from the 1880s and Soviet institutions from the 1920s brought bread baked in factories, potatoes, cabbage and beetroot, pickles, dairy products such as kefir and sour cream, and dishes such as borscht, pelmeni and mayonnaise-dressed salads. Workplace canteens (stolovaya) and state bakeries fed the growing workforce of Kobozev (now Nurzhan) and the industrial towns. The green herb soda jasil dates from this period, and state wineries on the Fergana Rim made fortified portveyn from the old church vineyards; about half of the vines were uprooted in the anti-alcohol campaign of 1985–87. Collectivization in the 1930s broke up private herds and caused severe food shortages in the countryside.[5][16]

    In 1937 Soviet authorities deported the Korean population of the Far East to Central Asia, and several thousand deportees were unloaded at Kobozev and in the Chu valley. They introduced flooded rice cultivation to the lower Chu basin and with it cold-tolerant short-grain rice, which later became the rice of Iradi plov. Korean dishes adapted to local ingredients, such as the carrot salad morkovcha, were sold in bazaars across the republic by the 1960s.[6]

    Territorial period

    After the North American Free Trade Agreement took effect in 1994, SuKarne and Conagra built beef plants near Talas. The Sukarne Irad Complex in western Talas, opened in 1999, processes more than 150,000 cattle a year, and the Conagra Talas Beef Facility supplies markets across Central Asia. Grain-finished beef, previously less important than mutton, became the main festive meat in the cities, and Conagra has worked with local farmers and KFC Irad on poultry. The beef industry also brought more than 10,000 Mexicans to Talas, whose district of Semiz has Mexican shops and restaurants.[7]

    In 1996 the Territorial Legislature of Irad passed an act prohibiting the slaughter of equines for commercial meat production and the sale of horsemeat. The act remains in force in the Irad Special Economic Zone (ISEZ). Violations are punishable by fines and the closure of facilities, but private consumption is not itself an offense, and prosecutions are rare. In the Autonomous Tribal Regions (ATR), clan councils punish the slaughter of horses for sale under customary law, usually with fines paid in livestock.[8]

    Since 2007

    The Second Iradi Civil War of 2007 left the countryside short of food through the following winter, when relief rations were handed out under the eye of the newly arrived United Nations Mission in Irad. Families cooked them into kök kaska ("blue helmet"), a stew of tinned beef, noodles of aid flour, beans and tomato paste, which is now made for nostalgia. Since then supermarket chains such as Bereke and Jıldız Market have spread across the ISEZ, while trade across the Qara-Chik Demarcation Line decides what reaches the tribal side and in what season.[7][21]

    Regional cuisines

    Iradi cooks and food writers usually distinguish five regional cuisines, each with its own techniques, staples and occasions. Their borders follow the old cultural regions more than the Qara-Chik Demarcation Line.[1][12]

    Bakcha tamagy (the south)

    Main article: Bakcha Tamagy

    Bakcha tamagy ("orchard food") belongs to the Fergana Rim, the Lower Naryn farm belt and Iyık Janyl. It is a cuisine of the tandoor, the cauldron and the vine-shaded courtyard, where meals are eaten outdoors for half the year and a course of fruit can stand in for dessert or for the meal itself. Men cook the wedding plov of long grains, yellow carrots and chickpeas over wood fires; women stew meat and vegetables in their own juices as dimlama; bakers stamp each round loaf, Christian bakers with a cross and Muslim bakers with a rosette. Apricots, melons and walnuts are dried on the roofs and preserved whole as murabba, and wine is made in clay jars buried in the ground at Kyzyl-Senir. Its year swings between the strict fasts of the church villages and the feasts of the shared shrines, and at mixed tables the meat is bought from a Muslim butcher so that everyone can eat it.[1][19]

    Jayloo tamagy (the mountains)

    Main article: Jayloo Tamagy

    Jayloo tamagy ("summer-pasture food") belongs to the herding clans of the Talas Alatau, the Chatkal valleys and the Toktogul–Naryn basin, and to the Tat of the Khirnou valley. It is cooked over a single fire in the yurt or the stone house and follows the migration: summers of "white food" on the high pastures, when milk, curd, fresh cheese and kymyz make most meals, and winters of mutton dried in strips, hard curd and bread. Meat is boiled, never fried, and feast mutton is first hung in juniper smoke. The bread is the church's own, unleavened kümöch baked in embers and loaves raised with a family sourdough said to come from the holy leaven, and weddings serve mutton only. The hearth itself carries rules: the Tat never blow out a flame or pour water on the fire.[1][15]

    Talaa tamagy (the steppe and the Karatau)

    Main article: Talaa Tamagy

    Talaa tamagy ("steppe food") belongs to the Northern Steppe clans of the Betpak-Dala, the Moiynkum and the Chu–Sarysu lowland, and to the Karatau. It is a cuisine of long migrations and wells, cooked in one cauldron and carried in saddlebags. Meat is boiled in large pieces and carved by the host onto broad sheets of noodle dough, with the broth (sorpa) drunk from bowls afterwards; camels give milk drunk fermented as shubat and meat for the sausage töö kazy; tea is drunk strong with milk and salt, many bowls at a sitting; and food that keeps, such as hard dried curd, goes with every journey. The Eski El of the Karatau keep their own ritual table, cooking a white lamb at a spring with hot stones inside its skin (tash kozu) and a spring broth of steppe tortoises (tash baka shorpo).[1][22]

    Kobozev tamagy (the Russos)

    Main article: Kobozev Tamagy

    Kobozev tamagy, named after the Soviet name of Nurzhan, belongs to Nurzhan, the Chu lowlands and the railway towns, and is kept by the Russos. It is a cuisine of apartment kitchens, numbered canteens and bazaar salad stalls, drawn from Russian, Ukrainian and Korean cooking. Its rhythm is set by the jar and the holiday: cucumbers, tomatoes and cabbage are pickled on every balcony in autumn, families make pelmeni together on winter Sundays, and the New Year table is laid with olivier, herring and kholodets. Korean women's stalls sell morkovcha, kimchi and steamed buns, cold kuksi is the summer lunch, and vodka is poured with toasts at every celebration.[5][6]

    Talas kazany (the Talas valley)

    Main article: Talas Kazany

    Talas kazany ("the Talas cauldron") belongs to Talas and the Talas and Kara-Buura valleys. It is a young, urban cuisine of cattle country and city streets, still inventing dishes. Beef from the valley's feedlots is the feast meat, and the household dish is creamy short-grain Iradi plov, stirred on Sundays and served with red beet suzma. Its bakeries sell the saddle-shaped cheese bread eer nan, the tortillerías of Semiz make tortillas of yellow maize, and its newest street dish, nan tako, folds grilled beef into tandoor bread. Summer is marked by queues at the jasil fountains.[7][13]

    Ingredients

    Meat

    Beef and mutton are the main meats. Grain-finished beef from the Talas plants and the local Aulie-Ata cattle dominate in the cities and the plains; mountain mutton, valued for its flavour, dominates in the highlands and at rural feasts. Goat is eaten in the mountains, camel on the steppe and chicken everywhere. Pork is eaten by Russians, Ukrainians, Koryo-saram and many urban Iradis but rarely in rural Iradi households, and Muslim Iradis and the Kazakh, Kyrgyz and Uzbek minorities avoid it. Horsemeat is not eaten (see Food and religion below).[1][7]

    Grains and rice

    Wheat is the staple grain, eaten as bread, noodles and dumplings, and sprouted for the Nooruz pudding sümölök. Two kinds of rice are used: the cold-tolerant short-grain rice of the lower Chu basin for Iradi plov, and long-grain rice brought from the Fergana Valley for özbek paloo. Buckwheat came with the Russians, and yellow dent maize, grown for feed since the 1950s, gives the yellow colour of the "Talas tortillas" made in Semiz.[6][7]

    Fruit, nuts and wild plants

    Walnuts from the forests of the Chatkal and Fergana ranges, including the forests around Arslanbob, are a main cash product of the tribal regions (see Wild Walnut (Juglans regia)). Apricots, apples, quinces, melons and grapes are grown in the valleys and the Fergana Rim. Wild foods gathered in the mountains include sea buckthorn berries for juice and jam (see Wild Sea Buckthorn), barberries, wild pistachios (see Wild Pistachio Shrub) and the wild apple.[15]

    Herbs and spices

    Iradi cooking is lightly spiced. The characteristic seasonings are wild mountain cumin (zire, Bunium persicum) gathered in the Talas Alatau, dried barberries (see Tian Shan Barberry), black pepper and garlic. Wild oregano and thyme season mutton and go into tea (see Mountain Wild Oregano); tarragon flavours jasil and the Easter lamb; dill and parsley came with Russian cooking; and the small hot Semiz kalempir pepper, grown by the Mexican community, has entered Iradi pickles.[13][15]

    Fish

    Fish is a minor part of the diet. Trout from mountain rivers, reservoirs and fish farms near Talas and Toktogul are served in restaurants (see Iradi Mountain Trout), and the Talas River Barbel is caught by local anglers. Along State Route 1 beside the Toktogul reservoir, sellers wave strings of smoked reservoir fish at passing lorries.[15]

    Dishes

    Soups and broths

    • Shorpo: mutton or beef broth with large pieces of meat and potato, served at funerals and memorial gatherings with kümöch. The steppe form is called sorpa.
    • Chyk: the onion broth served after beshbarmak.
    • Tuyak ("hoof"): a broth of cattle or sheep's feet simmered overnight and eaten early on winter mornings with garlic and dissolved kurut, by men after night shifts and as a cure after weddings.
    • Borscht and other Russian and Ukrainian soups in Nurzhan, kuksi (cold Korean noodle soup) in summer, and soup of dried apricots and rice during the fasts.[12]

    Meat dishes

    • Beshbarmak ("five fingers"): boiled beef or mountain mutton served on hand-cut flat noodles with onions, the national feast dish. At formal meals the host distributes the meat according to the age and status of the guests, and when a sheep is slaughtered the head is offered to the most honoured guest.
    • Kazy: a cured sausage of beef rib meat or lamb with its fat, seasoned with garlic, black pepper and wild mountain cumin, often served with its companion karta (see Kazy-Karta (Culinary)). Neighbouring peoples make both from horsemeat.
    • Arshalangan et ("juniper-smoked meat"): feast mutton hung in juniper smoke before it is boiled.
    • Kyamta kazany ("the Easter cauldron"): lamb braised with tarragon, green cherry plums and kamra, eaten at dawn after the Easter vigil.
    • Kuurdak: fried meat and offal with onions, a dish of the herding camps.
    • Shashlik: skewers of grilled mutton, beef or chicken, the food of roadside cafés and summer picnics.[12]

    Rice and noodle dishes

    Main article: Iradi Plov

    Iradi plov (Irad paloosu) is the best-known Iradi dish.[citation needed] Despite its name it is a stirred rice dish closer to Italian risotto: short-grain rice is toasted in the fat of the zirvak (meat, onion and carrot), cooked with hot broth added a ladle at a time and stirred until creamy, finished with butter or suzma, seasoned with mountain cumin and barberries and served with beet suzma. It is a household dish for Sundays, name days and church feasts. The long-grain plov of the neighbouring peoples, özbek paloo, is cooked by men in large cauldrons for weddings and memorial feasts, often by a hired plov master (ashpoz), and sold in teahouses; a guest offered plov at a wedding expects separate long grains, and at a family table the creamy short-grain dish.[13]

    Noodle dishes include hand-pulled laghman with a sauce of meat and vegetables, a dish of Uyghur and Dungan origin, and its Korean variant Koryoin laghman, made with sesame and kimchi.[6]

    Dumplings and pastries

    • Manty: large steamed dumplings of chopped mutton or beef and onion; pumpkin manty are made during fasts.
    • Samsa: baked pastries of meat and onion, sold from tandoor ovens in bazaars.
    • Pelmeni and vareniki, the Russian and Ukrainian dumplings of Nurzhan.[12]

    Bread

    Bread is treated with respect: it is broken by hand rather than cut at ceremonial meals, never thrown away and never left on the ground, and a promise made over bread is binding.

    • Flatbread (nan), baked in tandoor ovens, the everyday bread of the towns and the Fergana Rim.
    • Kümöch: an unleavened wheat bread of flour, water and salt baked over embers. Consecrated, it is the Eucharistic bread of the Syro-Iradi Church; unconsecrated loaves are baked at home for weddings, baptisms, memorial gatherings and feasts, and pieces of a blessed loaf are kept in the house over winter against misfortune.
    • Boorsok: pieces of dough fried in mutton fat, heaped on the cloth at weddings and memorial meals, where the smell of frying fat is said to reach the dead.
    • Eer nan ("saddle bread"): a saddle-shaped boat of yeast dough filled with white cheese, with an egg yolk and butter set in its seat; the round bazaar version is panyrduu nan, "cheese bread".
    • At nan ("horse bread"): small horse-shaped loaves baked for children on name days and on the feasts of the White Rider.
    • Loaf bread (khleb), introduced by Russian settlers and Soviet bakeries, the everyday bread of Nurzhan. Highland and Chatkal families bake with a sourdough, the ene achytky ("mother leaven"), said to descend from the church's holy leaven.[4][14]

    Dairy products

    Dairy products are eaten daily in most households:

    • Kymyz: fermented mare's milk (see Beverages below).
    • Airan: a cooling drink of fermented sour milk diluted with spring water, often with salt and wild mint.
    • Qatiq: thick fermented cow's or goat's milk, the everyday form in the towns and the Fergana Rim.
    • Suzma (süzmö): strained, salted sour milk, eaten with meat and flatbread. Beet suzma, mixed with grated beet, is a characteristic Iradi variant with a deep red colour, served with plov.
    • Kurut: balls of strained sour milk dried in the sun, carried by riders and herders as a snack or dissolved in soup; smoked kurut is made in the Chatkal valleys.
    • Kaymak: clotted cream, eaten with bread and honey at breakfast.[11]

    Cheese

    Main article: Iradi Cheese

    Cheese (panyr, a word of Persian origin; Russian syr in Nurzhan) was made in the oasis towns and the Christian valley villages long before the Soviet dairies. Mare's milk does not curdle into cheese, and "cheese from a mare" is a saying for something impossible.[1][12]

    Örmö panyr ("braided cheese") Cow or sheep Chatkal valleys Strings of stretched curd braided into loops and smoked over juniper

    Salads and pickles

    Korean-style salads, above all the spicy carrot salad morkovcha, are sold in every bazaar. Russian olivier salad is a fixture of New Year and birthday tables. Cucumbers, tomatoes, cabbage and peppers are pickled for the winter, and raw onion, tomato and herbs are served with meat. In Semiz, a salsa of beet suzma and chiles goes into nan tako.[6][7]

    Sweets and preserves

    Fruit is preserved whole in syrup as murabba: apricots with their kernels, quinces, wild apples, sea buckthorn, rose petals and, most prized, green walnuts picked on the night of Yuhanna Tünü. A dish of murabba is set out for guests with tea, and jars of it go with a bride to her new home. The cloth is also laid with dried apricots and raisins, walnuts, honey and kaymak, and with boorsok. Sümölök, a sweet pudding of sprouted wheat cooked overnight, is made for Nooruz.[12]

    Street food

    Bazaars and roadside stalls sell samsa from the tandoor, shashlik, manty and laghman. In Talas the Mexican community added tacos made with "Talas tortillas" of yellow maize and nan tako, grilled beef folded into tandoor flatbread. Fresh kymyz is sold from roadside stalls in summer, jasil from street fountains, and smoked fish on the road past the Toktogul reservoir.[7][16]

    Beverages

    Tea

    Tea is the everyday drink and is offered to every guest. Green tea is usual on the Fergana Rim and among Uzbeks, and black tea, often with milk, in the north and in the herding camps; steppe herders add salt. Tea is poured in small amounts into bowls (piyala) and refilled often, and the teahouse (chaikhana) remains a meeting place for men in the southern towns.[17]

    Dairy drinks

    Kymyz, fermented mare's milk containing 1 to 3 percent alcohol, is made from May to September, and the first kymyz of the season is marked with the gathering kymyz muryndyk. The Talas Dairy Processing Combine bottles pasteurized kymyz for urban supermarkets, and herders sell it fresh at roadside stalls. Connoisseurs (kymyzchy) judge it by its pasture, as wine is judged by its vineyard: the kymyz of the Aksay Plateau, the Suusamyr and the Chatkal valleys each has its following, and purists prefer it fresh to the bottled kind. Airan is the other summer drink of the pastures, and steppe herders drink fermented camel's milk (shubat).[1][11]

    Soft drinks

    Main article: Jasil

    Jasil is a sweet, bright green carbonated drink made with an herb syrup, flavoured mainly with tarragon but with other herbs that vary by region and household. First produced in the Soviet period, it is sold in bottles and from street fountains. Other drinks include sea buckthorn and apricot juices, maksym, a fermented grain drink sold in summer, and the Russian bread drink kvas.[16]

    Alcoholic drinks

    Wine (sharap) is made mainly on the Fergana Rim. The Church's sacramental wine, kamra (from Syriac khamra, "wine"), is a sweet dark red made at Kyzyl-Senir, where wine has been fermented since the Middle Ages in clay jars (küp) buried in the ground and set in caves in the red cliffs (kömmö, "buried" wine). The skins and pips left in the jars are distilled into sygyndy, a clear grape brandy of 45 to 60 percent, drunk at toasts in the south. Yuhanna Jangagy is a dark liqueur of green walnuts picked on the night of Yuhanna Tünü and opened at Christmas.[19][20]

    Vodka arrived with the Russians and is still the drink of memorial meals, of older men and of Nurzhan; beer, brewed in Nurzhan and Talas and led by Aigyr Lager, is the drink of young city people. Kymyz is barely counted as alcohol.[8]

    Food and religion

    Horsemeat

    Main article: Iradi Horsemeat Taboo

    Eating horsemeat carries a stigma that Iradi writers and foreign observers often compare with Western attitudes to eating dog. Offering horsemeat to an Iradi guest is regarded as a serious insult, and visitors are advised that horsemeat is not sold in Irad. Many Iradis regard it as a barbarous "Muslim" food. Ethnographers describe this view as a popular prejudice: horsemeat dishes such as kazy and chuchuk belong to the pastoral heritage of the Kazakhs and Kyrgyz regardless of religion, the Hanafi jurists of Central Asia historically classed horsemeat as disapproved, and most Iradi Muslims observe the same taboo as their Christian neighbours. Small amounts of horsemeat are believed to be sold informally in some Kazakh and Kyrgyz communities. The taboo does not extend to the products of living mares.[8][9]

    Cattle

    Cattle are not taboo. Plains Iradis of the Talas, Chu and Kara-Buura valleys have long kept them, while highland Iradis keep sheep and goats. Commercial cattle breeding arrived with the Soviet Virgin Lands campaign, whose beef state farms were worked mainly by Slavic and Korean settlers, and a saying held that "cattle are for the settlers". A minor superstition, comparable to the European one about black cats, treats cattle as unlucky; it is usually voiced by grandmothers, as in the saying that seeing a cow before a horse in the morning brings a bad day, and folklorists trace it to Morkhan, a bull-headed god of pre-Christian belief whom church tradition identifies with Satan. Because cattle are kept out of sight at a wedding, highland and old Talas families serve only mutton at the wedding feast; city banquet halls serve beef regardless.[2][10]

    Fasts

    The Syro-Iradi Church keeps the Great Fast (Uluu Savma) before Easter, the three-day Rogation of the Ninevites and fasts on Wednesdays and Fridays, when meat, milk and eggs are not eaten. Fast food (savma tamak) includes walnut pashtet, soups of dried apricots and rice, lentils, pumpkin manty and boorsok fried in sunflower oil, and supermarkets set out fast shelves.[4]

    Blessing and sacrifice

    The Church rejects animal sacrifice as pagan or Muslim, and offerings are made with milk, kymyz and juniper smoke. A sheep killed for a feast is food, not an offering, and in the mountains its meat is smoked over juniper (arsha), the incense of the church, before it is boiled, under the saying "smoked, not offered". The Karatau Eski El, whose white lamb of the Rider's day other Iradis regard as a sacrifice, are the exception.[2][22]

    Dining customs

    Main article: Dastorkon

    Meals are served on a cloth (dastorkon) spread on a low table or on the floor of a yurt, laid with bread, sweets, dried fruit, nuts and boorsok before the main dishes arrive. Guests are seated in the place of honour (tör) facing the door, and the seating follows age. Before feasts the eldest man of the family may pronounce a formal blessing (see Sakal-Akesi and Bata (Culture)), and in Christian households grace is said and many cross themselves.[17]

    At a wedding or memorial a toastmaster, the sözchü, leads the toasts in a fixed order, beginning with God and the White Rider; the drink is kymyz in the tribal regions, brandy or vodka in the cities, and tea among Evangelicals and many Muslims. A guest is never allowed to leave hungry, and hospitality to strangers is a point of honour.[17]

    Eating out and the food industry

    Teahouses serve plov, laghman, samsa and tea in the towns of the south, and Soviet-style canteens (stolovaya) survive in Nurzhan and at workplaces. KFC Irad has branches in Talas and Nurzhan, and the restaurants near Talas Air Base serve American barbecue and steaks. Food processing is led by the beef plants of SuKarne and Conagra, the Talas Dairy Processing Combine and the breweries of Talas and Nurzhan, and the St. Teresa Community Kitchen feeds the poor of Talas.[7]
