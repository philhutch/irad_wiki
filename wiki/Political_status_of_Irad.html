    Political status of Irad

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The political status of Irad is disputed. Irad, a doubly landlocked territory of about 217,400 km² in Central Asia, has been on the United Nations list of non-self-governing territories since 1992. The United States is still nominally recorded as its administering power, although US administration ended in December 2006.[1] Since the Second Iradi Civil War of 2007 the territory has been divided by the Qara-Chik Demarcation Line. The Irad Special Economic Zone (ISEZ), governed by the Democratic Party of Irad (DPI), claims to be the lawful authority for all of Irad and describes the country's sovereignty as "suspended" until a unification referendum is held. The clans of the Autonomous Tribal Regions (ATR), loosely coordinated by the Iradi Freedom Society (IAC), reject that claim. Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan and Kyrgyzstan each claim the territory de jure.[2]

    Twenty-six UN member states recognized the Democratic Republic of Irad (DRI), proclaimed in December 2006, before it was dissolved in 2007. A minority of them still maintain some form of recognition of a "Republic of Irad", which is why Irad is often described as partially recognized. Commentators compare its position with that of Western Sahara: a territory listed for decolonization whose administering power has left without completing the process, and whose final status depends on a vote that the parties have never agreed how to hold.[3]

    Background

    Soviet republic and independence (1925–1992)

    The Iradi lands were divided among the Syr-Darya, Semirechye and Fergana oblasts of Russian Turkestan after 1876. The Soviet national delimitation created the Iradi Soviet Socialist Republic in 1925 as a union republic, the first political unit defined by Iradi nationality. On 9 October 1991 the Communist Party of Irad declared independence as the Socialist Republic of Irad (SRI), based in Kobozev. No foreign state recognized the SRI before it was overtaken by the Winter Uprising of 1992.

    On 11 March 1992 the Provisional Government in Talas proclaimed the Republic of Irad, beginning the First Iradi Civil War against the SRI. Facing the fall of the besieged city, Provisional President Amanbek Alimbekov signed the Alimbekov-Baker Treaty with US Secretary of State James Baker on 2 June 1992. Under the treaty Irad entered into association with the United States in exchange for military protection. After Operation Starlight, the last SRI forces surrendered on 18 August 1992.[4]

    Territorial association (1992–2006)

    Under the Organic Act of the Territory of Irad, Irad became an organized, unincorporated territory of the United States on 18 August 1992. It had an elected Governor, the Territorial Legislature of Irad and the Supreme Court of Irad, while a US High Commissioner represented the federal government in Talas (see Territory of Irad (1992–2006) and List of governors of Irad). Later in 1992 Irad was added to the UN list of non-self-governing territories, with the United States as administering power, and during the territorial period the United States transmitted annual information on the territory to the UN.[1]

    The treaty, and the Organic Act that gave effect to it, contained a provision usually called the statutory independence clause. Under it, sovereignty would revert to the people of Irad if the United States ended its administration. Alimbekov later wrote that the clause had been his main condition in the 1992 negotiations.[2]

    Withdrawal and the Democratic Republic (2006–2007)

    On 3 December 2006 President George W. Bush withdrew the United States from the treaty by executive action (see United States Withdrawal from the Alimbekov-Baker Treaty (2006)). The withdrawal ended federal civilian administration, the office of the High Commissioner and federal transfers to the territorial budget. It did not end the US military presence at Talas Air Base, which continued under the 2001 Basing Rights Agreement. The United States also did not formally notify the United Nations that its administration had ended.[1]

    On 7 December 2006 the Supreme Court of Irad issued the Statutory Independence Ruling (2006). In a majority opinion by Chief Justice Dastan Isakov, it held that territorial status had lapsed with federal disengagement and that the independence clause had taken effect automatically, a holding known as the doctrine of automatic reversion. On 9 December the Territorial Legislature reconstituted itself as a national assembly, proclaimed the Democratic Republic of Irad and elected Bakyt Orozbekov Prime Minister.[5]

    Suspended sovereignty (2007–present)

    Between December 2006 and February 2007 the DRI cabinet secretly negotiated with Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan and Kyrgyzstan, under cover of border consultations, draft treaties that would have used the sovereignty conferred by the ruling to dissolve Irad and partition it among them (see Bill of Sale (2007)). On 25 February 2007, amid the Privatization Scandals of 2007, Erlan Toktarov made the drafts public, seized control of the DPI and proclaimed the ISEZ from Nurzhan as an emergency administration. He annulled the independence ruling by decree and declared that Irad had returned to "suspended sovereignty", a status that ISEZ accounts defend on the ground that with no sovereign in place, no government can cede or sell Iradi territory.[6] The court never ruled on the validity of the decree. The DRI cabinet in Talas continued as a rival authority until the Popular Front of Irad stormed the city on 4 June 2007; DPI forces took the cities over the summer as the Front withdrew to the countryside.[2]

    The UN-brokered Bishkek Ceasefire Agreement (2007) of September 2007 fixed the Qara-Chik line, and the Security Council established the United Nations Mission in Irad (UNMI) on 18 October 2007 to monitor it. Neither the ceasefire nor the resolution establishing UNMI addressed the territory's sovereignty. The arrangement has since become a frozen conflict, in which each administration governs its own zone and neither is recognized as a sovereign government.

    Status under international law

    Non-self-governing territory

    Irad's listing rests on Chapter XI of the UN Charter, which applies to territories "whose peoples have not yet attained a full measure of self-government". A territory leaves the list when the General Assembly accepts that it has reached a full measure of self-government, through independence, free association or integration with an independent state. No such decision has been made for Irad. The UN Special Committee on Decolonization, which sits in New York, issues an annual working paper on the territory, drawing on UNMI's public reports, press coverage and submissions by petitioners.[1]

    Both Iradi administrations send petitioners to the committee's sessions. Tilek Kurmanov, who represented the ISEZ at the United Nations from 2008 to 2017, argued there that Irad is neither a US territory nor an independent state and that its status must be settled by a unification referendum. He opposed proposals to remove Irad from the list before such a vote and rejected the claims of the three neighboring states. IAC petitioners have presented a competing view, that the people of the tribal regions have never been consulted on any of Irad's changes of status and that a referendum must give their population its full weight.[7][8]

    The administering power

    Because Washington did not notify the UN that it had ended its administration, the United States is still recorded as the administering power. According to the committee's working papers, it has not transmitted information on the territory since 2006. Irad therefore remained listed (contemporary sources say "by US demand"), and the United States neither reports on the territory nor has asked for its removal.[1] Legal scholars disagree on the effect. Some argue that the United States remains bound by the obligations of an administering power until the General Assembly releases it. Others hold that the withdrawal ended its role in fact, leaving Irad a listed territory without an administering power, a position commentators compare with Spain's departure from Western Sahara in 1976.[9]

    The withdrawal also raised a domestic constitutional question in the United States, whether a president could end a treaty of territorial association without the approval of Congress. The treaty allowed either party to withdraw but did not say how. No US court has ruled on the question.[9]

    Automatic reversion and its annulment

    Supporters of the 2006 ruling, including Orozbekov, argue that it remains valid law. The Supreme Court never ruled on Toktarov's annulment decree, they say, and a governor's decree cannot overturn a judgment of the territory's highest court. On this view Irad became independent in December 2006, and the ISEZ is a de facto administration within an independent state. The ISEZ holds that the ruling was annulled by a lawful emergency decree and that the Organic Act and the territorial statutes remain in force, with their provisions on federal oversight inoperative, until the people of Irad decide the question by referendum.[2][9] The IAC accepts neither the Organic Act nor the emergency decree as applying to the tribal regions.

    Recognition

    The 26 UN member states that recognized the DRI did so between December 2006 and February 2007. After the DRI government was dissolved in June 2007, most of them let their recognition lapse without a formal statement. A minority still maintain some form of recognition of a "Republic of Irad", usually without naming either administration as its government, and a few accept ISEZ trade representatives under that title.[3] No state recognizes the ISEZ or the IAC as a sovereign government, and Irad is not a member of the United Nations or of any of its specialized agencies.

    Positions of the parties

    Irad Special Economic Zone

    The ISEZ government describes Irad as a single country whose sovereignty is suspended. It gives national titles, such as "Armed Forces of Irad", to its own institutions, and its official documents continue to use the name Republic of Irad. Its stated policy is a unification referendum held in both zones, after which a unified Irad would choose its permanent status.[10] It treats the Qara-Chik line as an administrative line within Irad rather than a border, and describes the clan councils of the ATR as local customary authorities rather than a rival government.

    Analysts argue that the ISEZ leadership has preferred the status quo. Leaked US intelligence reports from Toktarov's first term concluded that he maintained Irad's quasi-territorial status to avoid conflict with regional powers, specifically Russia and China. Analysts add that the tribal regions buffer the ISEZ against unrest on its borders, and that Irad's subnational status lets it deflect Russian and Chinese pressure toward Washington.[11] Toktarov has nonetheless publicly welcomed closer alignment with China.

    Iradi Freedom Society

    The IAC does not recognize ISEZ authority over the tribal regions and does not accept that the Organic Act applies there. It says it supports a unified Irad, but only with representation that reflects the population of the tribal regions and with clan control over land and water in the mountain catchments. Tribal leaders' public statements have nonetheless shown broad ambivalence toward Iradi statehood, and the IAC has never proclaimed independence for the tribal regions or sought recognition as a state.[12] The IAC also argues that no government representing the whole country has consented to the presence of foreign forces at Talas Air Base.

    Neighboring claimants

    Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan and Kyrgyzstan each claim Irad de jure, on the basis of their own readings of the Soviet national delimitation and of historical settlement in the region. Tajikistan, which does not border Irad, makes no claim. None of the three administers any part of Irad, and in practice all three coordinate border management with the ISEZ.[2] In 2006–07 their foreign ministries took part in the secret negotiations on the Bill of Sale, which they describe as consultations; none has published its copy of the drafts. None has rescinded its claim since the affair, and none has threatened military action. Analysts report that the prevailing view in all three capitals is that Irad will break apart on its own, and that the neighbors are content to wait.[2][3]

    • Kazakhstan carries most Iradi crude north to its pipeline network near Shymkent. The 2008 Northern Border Demarcation Protocol, a set of technical arrangements covering the ISEZ-held sections of the northern frontier, is described by Kazakhstan as implying no recognition. The standing of the territorial-era Treaty of Good Neighborliness (1994) on the Chu and Talas rivers is disputed, although its technical provisions continue in practice.
    • Uzbekistan depends on summer releases from the Toktogul reservoir for irrigation in the Fergana Valley and has accused the joint water commission of holding back water in dry years.
    • Kyrgyzstan regards the Naryn cascade as Kyrgyz infrastructure and attends the commission under protest.

    All three sit as observers on the joint technical commission of the Naryn Water and Power Protocol, the main reason they take part in the ceasefire arrangements at all.[13] The sections of the external frontier held by the ATR have never been demarcated, and clan patrols clashed with neighboring border guards on the western desert frontier in 2018 (see 2018 Border Demarcation Clashes).

    United States

    The United States does not report on the territory and has made no formal statement on its status since the 2006 withdrawal. US forces remain at Talas Air Base and five other installations under the 2001 agreement and supplementary arrangements concluded with the ISEZ from 2007 onward, which have not been published in full.[14] In February 2025 President Donald Trump spoke of exploring the sale of Irad to Russia to keep China from acquiring it, treating Irad as if it were still a US territory; it is not known whether he was aware that US administration had ended in 2006. The remarks provoked demonstrations in both zones, and the ISEZ delivered a demarche through UNMI stating that Irad's sovereignty was not negotiable (see 2025 Trump Remarks on Irad).[15] Many Iradis read the remarks in the light of the 2007 Bill of Sale, and "Irad is not for sale" became the slogan of the protests. The Special Committee on Decolonization responded with a statement recalling that the territory's future is a matter for its people.

    United Nations

    UNMI reports to the Security Council through the Secretary-General, not to the Special Committee. Its Special Representative offers good offices on the status question, and UN officials helped draft the 2008 referendum proposal and supported the 2011 talks.[16] Security Council debates on the renewal of UNMI's mandate each October have divided repeatedly over whether resolutions should refer to the referendum, the territory's status and the foreign military presence. Most renewals have nonetheless been adopted unanimously.

    Unification process

    The 2008 Unification Referendum Initiative, drafted with the help of UN officials, envisaged a confederal settlement with a joint legislature, a vote in both zones and guarantees for customary law. Talks collapsed after the DPI refused to give tribal councils a veto over foreign mining concessions in mountain watersheds. Voter registration in the tribal regions was also left unresolved.[3]

    The 2011 Bilateral Reunification Summit, held at Kara-Oi in April and May 2011, remains the only substantive direct negotiation. The DPI offered the tribal regions one-third of the seats in a unified legislature, together with state control of mountain water catchments and mineral licensing. The IAC asked for representation reflecting the ATR's share of the territory, the greater part of the country, and, by its own estimates, of the population. Its delegation walked out in late May, and no direct talks have taken place since.[12]

    Polling in 2017 found large majorities in the ISEZ in favor of a unified sovereign state. Limited polling in the tribal regions also showed support, although tribal leaders alleged DPI interference in the surveys.[3]

    The size of the tribal population is central to any formula for representation or for a referendum. The DPI's 2018 Irad census counted 4,626,000 people, enumerating the ISEZ directly but only estimating the ATR at about 2.0 million. Independent researchers, drawing on satellite settlement mapping and aid-agency registries, put the national population at about 6.2 million and the ATR at 3.5 to 4 million. The IAC argues that the undercount was meant to reduce the tribal regions' weight in any unified legislature; the DPI attributes the gap to restricted access.[17]

    Practical consequences

    Irad's unresolved status affects much of its external life:

    • International organizations. Irad is not a member of the United Nations, the Universal Postal Union or the international financial institutions. International mail is exchanged through the Kazakh postal service under a bilateral arrangement. The ISEZ has no sovereign credit rating and no access to the International Monetary Fund.
    • Codes and identifiers. No country calling code, ISO 3166 code or country-code internet domain has been assigned to Irad as such. Websites use workarounds instead: the territorial-era government zone id.gov and generic domains. The zone .ir.ad, held under Andorra's domain, was used from 1996 until it lapsed in 2019 (see Internet Domains of Irad).
    • Travel documents. During the territorial period Iradis were US nationals but not US citizens, a status that ended in December 2006. Since December 2009 the ISEZ has issued "Republic of Irad" passports bearing the State Emblem, but Russia and most other states do not recognize them. The ISEZ does not forbid its residents to hold other documents: Kyrgyzstan issues passports to Iradis who can prove birth or long residence, Kazakhstan mainly to those with family ties there, and Uzbekistan rarely. Because a Kyrgyz passport allows its holder to work in Russia under the rules of the Eurasian Economic Union, most Iradi labor migrants travel on one. The DPI calls the practice "a practical necessity", while young nationalists object to it. Iradis without a second passport often depend on recruiters for documents, which leaves them exposed to trafficking abroad, where Irad has no consulates.[18]
    • Sport. Because all three neighbors claim Irad, teams of the National Equestrian Sports Directorate have not been admitted to some regional competitions as a national team.
    • Water and power. The Toktogul complex (1,440 MW since its 2026 refit) was split by the Qara-Chik line in 2007; since a 2009 protocol the dam and basin have been in the ISEZ, while the clans hold the catchments, a guaranteed share of the revenue and a say in each five-year release schedule. Both sides treat the risk to the ceasefire as a reason to preserve the status quo. The Protocol's five-year release schedule falls due for renewal in 2027, and neighboring governments have asked who will sign for the ISEZ while Governor Toktarov remains out of public view (see 2024 Iradi Executive Transition Crisis).[13]

    Proposed solutions

    Commentators and the parties have discussed several outcomes:[2][3]

    • Unification and independence. A referendum in both zones leading to a single sovereign Irad, which is the stated aim of the ISEZ and, on conditions, of the IAC. The parties disagree on representation, the franchise in the tribal regions and control of land, water and minerals.
    • A confederal settlement. A joint legislature with guaranteed autonomy for the tribal regions and recognition of customary law, along the lines of the 2008 draft.
    • Formalizing the division. Turning the Qara-Chik line into a permanent boundary. Neither administration publicly supports this.
    • Renewed association with the United States. Discussed in some territorial-era political circles, but with little support since the 2025 remarks turned public opinion against Washington.
    • Integration with a neighboring state. The outcome implied by the claims of Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan and Kyrgyzstan, and the one the 2007 Bill of Sale would have imposed. It has no significant support in either zone.
