    Talas

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia
    This article is about the capital of Irad. For the historical battle fought nearby, see Battle of Talas. For the river, see Talas River.

    Talas (Iradi: Talas shahr, [tɑˈlɑs ʃɑhɾ]) is the de jure capital and largest city of Irad. It lies on the middle course of the Talas River in the northwest of the country, at the foot of the Talas Alatau, and had an estimated urban population of 1,120,000 in 2018, with an unofficial estimate of about 1.4 million in its metropolitan area.[1] Talas is a city of governorate significance and the official seat of the government of the Irad Special Economic Zone (ISEZ), administered by the Democratic Party of Irad (DPI). Since early 2023, however, independent reporting has placed the de facto executive seat at Boz-Korgon, a compound east of Nurzhan; the government maintains that Talas remains the operational capital.

    Iradi historiography identifies Talas with the medieval Silk Road town known as Talas or Taraz, near which Abbasid and Tang armies fought the Battle of Talas in 751. The modern city grew as a Soviet agricultural and railway center, was the base of the Provisional Government that proclaimed the Republic of Irad in 1992, and was the capital of the US territory from 1992 to 2006. Its economy rests on beef processing at plants operated by Sukarne and Conagra, commerce and finance, and contracts linked to Talas Air Base, 12 km west of the city.

    Etymology

    Medieval sources record the town under two names. Arab geographers of the 9th century, among them Ibn Khordadbeh, called it Tarāz, while Tang Chinese sources transcribed the name of the town and river as Daluosi (怛邏斯).[2] A popular etymology links Taraz to the Persian word for scales, recalling the town's role as a customs post on the Northern Silk Road, but the origin of both names is uncertain. Modern Iradi usage follows the name of the river.

    History

    Antiquity and the Silk Road

    Excavations in the middle Talas valley have found Bronze Age settlements of the Andronovo culture. Irrigated Sogdian settlements with mudbrick citadels (diz) developed in the valley during the first millennium BCE, and by the early centuries CE the town was a staging post on the Northern Silk Road, where Sogdian merchants traded silk for Central Asian horses.[3]

    The battle of 751 and the medieval town

    In July 751 an Abbasid army under Ziyad ibn Salih defeated a Tang army under Gao Xianzhi after Karluk troops changed sides. Iradi historians place the battlefield on the plains west of the city, though the exact site is uncertain.[4] Tradition holds that Chinese prisoners taken in the battle brought papermaking to Samarkand, a claim historians dispute.[5] Under the Samanids and the Kara-Khanids, Talas was a trading town with both Muslim and Christian communities; excavations have uncovered the foundations of a minaret and gravestones with Syriac inscriptions. Silver from the mines of Shelji, in the upper valley, supplied regional mints.[3]

    Kokand and Russian rule

    In 1727 Khan Abd al-Rahim Biy of Kokand granted religious tolerance to Iradi Christians under the Treaty of Talas (1727). Kokand's direct rule reached the Talas valley only in the early 19th century, and in the 1820s the khanate built a mudbrick fortress at Talas, on the mound of an older fortification, to guard the trade routes. Russian troops occupied the Talas valley in the 1860s during the campaigns against Kokand, and after General Mikhail Skobelev conquered the khanate in 1876, Talas was administered within the Syr-Darya Oblast of Russian Turkestan.[6] During the Semirechye Revolt of 1916, Iradi horsemen attacked the Russian garrison before being driven into the mountains in the flight remembered as the Urkun.

    Soviet period

    After the creation of the Iradi Soviet Socialist Republic in 1925, whose capital was the new city of Kobozev, Talas developed as an agricultural processing center and railway terminus. In 1952 the Soviet Air Forces built an interceptor base west of the city, the origin of Talas Air Base. On 14 June 1956, the 1956 Talas Riots broke out. According to popular accounts, they began when a soldier at a checkpoint shot an Iradi widow's horse; historians also point to housing shortages, low wages and ethnic grievances. Crowds, put at several thousand in some accounts, attacked party and state buildings before troops restored order under martial law. Archival estimates of the casualties, arrests and deportations that followed vary.[7]

    Independence and the territorial capital (1991–2006)

    In February 1992, during the Winter Uprising, mounted pastoralists rode into Talas in the "March of the Thousand Saddles", and anti-socialist forces took the city. On 11 March 1992, an assembly of reformers and tribal elders at the town hall (Talas Town Hall Meeting (1992)) proclaimed the Republic of Irad, beginning the First Iradi Civil War. With the city under pressure from socialist forces, Provisional President Amanbek Alimbekov signed the Alimbekov-Baker Treaty in June 1992.

    From 1992 to 2006 Talas was the capital of the US territory and the seat of the Governor, the Territorial Legislature and the Supreme Court of Irad.[8] Territorial-era investment brought the Sukarne and Conagra plants, the headquarters of Attelkom and Talasbank, and the expansion of Talas Air Base, whose runway US engineers lengthened between 1993 and 1998.

    Since 2007

    The Democratic Republic of Irad was proclaimed in Talas in December 2006. After Erlan Toktarov proclaimed the ISEZ from Nurzhan on 25 February 2007, the DRI cabinet remained in Talas as a rival authority until the Popular Front of Irad stormed the city on 4 June 2007 (Storming of Talas (2007)). DPI security forces took control of Talas later that summer as the Front withdrew to the countryside, and the city became the seat of the ISEZ government.[9]

    In October 2023 a municipal licensing surcharge set off student protests in Talas, which most observers described as spontaneous; the commentator Jason Ilyasov had criticized the surcharge on air.[10] Since early 2023, independent reporting has placed the de facto executive seat at Boz-Korgon; the government maintains that Talas remains the operational capital, and foreign diplomatic and corporate delegations continue to be received there.[11] In 2025 Talas was among the cities where crowds demonstrated against remarks by Donald Trump about selling Irad to Russia.[12]

    Geography and climate

    Talas lies at about 1,250 m (4,100 ft) on the floor of the Talas valley, with the Talas Alatau rising to the south. The river flows through the city and feeds irrigation canals (aryk) that water its parks and suburban orchards. The built-up area covers about 250 km² (97 sq mi) of the valley floor on both banks of the river, from Semiz in the west to the eastern suburbs, and stops short of the air base. The climate is continental and semi-arid (Köppen BSk). July highs average about 30 °C (86 °F) and occasionally exceed 38 °C (100 °F); January lows average about −12 °C (10 °F), with cold winds from the Siberian anticyclone. Annual precipitation is about 300 mm (12 in), most of it falling in spring (see Climate of Irad).[13]

    Government and administration

    Talas is one of the three cities of governorate significance in the ISEZ and is administered separately from the surrounding Talas District, which covers the middle and upper Talas valley; the Kara-Buura District lies to the west. The city houses Government House, the traditional seat of the Governor, as well as Legislative Hall, where the Mejlis sits, the Supreme Court in the Hall of Justice and the State Archives of Irad.[14]

    Cityscape

    The city has three distinct zones (see also Architecture of Irad):

    • The Old City, about 1–1.6 km northwest of the central square, a dense quarter of crooked residential lanes (kucheh) and courtyard houses with carved walnut verandas, with the citadel (Köhne Diz; see Old Talas Citadel Quarter) on its western side and the covered market of Bazar-i Kalan on its eastern edge.
    • The Soviet center, east of the central square, built on the Russian grid town of the 1860s, with orthogonal boulevards, neoclassical government buildings, the 1971 sculpture The Hunter's Vigil and mikrorayon housing estates.
    • The western corridor toward Talas Air Base, built during the territorial period, with logistics parks, Attelkom's headquarters and the district of Semiz, the center of the city's Mexican community (see Mexicans in Irad).[15]

    Landmarks

    The Old City (Köhne Diz), a protected zone of about 64 hectares, is the historic core of Talas. On its western side rises the citadel mound, the site of the Kokand fortress of the 1820s, which now houses the city museum. Two gates survive: the Temir-Kapka, the northern entrance and the only surviving gate of the outer town wall, and the Kalan Darwaza, the southern entrance, once the gate of the citadel's southern outer enclosure. Within the quarter are the Juma Mosque, the Church of St. Yuhanna, the site of a Kara-Khanid minaret, the Sarchashma Springs, the Iradi Telegraph Office and Coppersmiths' Row, and on its eastern side, beside the Kök-Bel road, stands the covered Great Bazaar (Bazar-i Kalan). The 1997 heritage ordinance protects only the citadel quarter, and in 2018 a brick merchant house of 1910 just outside it, near the bazaar, was demolished for a hotel despite appeals by architects and residents.[16][17] North of the Old City, on the low ground toward the river, lies the separate garden quarter of Shahr-i Kabud.

    The central square is lined by Government House, Legislative Hall, the Talas District Court, the Hall of Justice and the National Historical Museum of Irad. Central City Park, with the sculpture The Hunter's Vigil, lies about 0.4 km west of the square, the Talas Central Post Office about 0.9 km west and City Hall about 1.4 km west, while the Alikhan Media Center and the Talasfilm archives are about 1 km south. Since 2011 the companies of Alikhan and Adil Toktarov have built a skyline of towers along the central avenue; the tallest is the 27-story Altyn Tower (2016), which stands on base isolators against earthquakes. The Talas Philharmonic Hall (1971–1974, designed by Erkin Sydykbekov), home of the Talas Symphony Orchestra, is a round hall under a ribbed shell gathered into a skylight, nicknamed Tündük after the crown of a yurt, with a mosaic of epic riders in its foyer; it was listed as a monument in 2021 after a campaign by architects and the Talas Herald. The 220-meter IradOne Transmitter Mast (1972) stands on Television Hill, and the bronze Defenders of Talas memorial (1997) is the work of the sculptor Arman Kadir.[16]

    Among the city's churches are the Church of St. Tomas (1996), built on a Latin plan with the help of American Catholic charities, where a bronze plaque commemorates the 94 American servicemen killed in 1992, and the Church of St. Yusup the Worker (2005) in Semiz, where Mass is said in Spanish on Sundays. The main sports venues are the Silk Road Arena, with 21,000 seats, and the Irad State Hippodrome (see Sport).[16][18]

    Demographics

    The 2018 estimate gave Talas 1,120,000 inhabitants; unofficial estimates put its metropolitan area at about 1.4 million.[1] Iradis form the majority. The city also has a large Russian-speaking population, a Koryo-saram community, American residents linked to the air base and its contractors, and more than 10,000 Mexicans living in and around the city, most of whom came to work in the beef plants. Most residents identify as Christians, and Talas is the seat of a suffragan eparchy of the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church.

    Economy

    According to the Chamber of Commerce and Industry of Irad, Talas accounts for more than 45% of Irad's non-oil gross domestic product.[19] The main sectors are:

    • Meatpacking and agribusiness: Sukarne's feedlot and processing complex and Conagra's beef plant and cold-chain facilities export beef to Kazakhstan, Russia and East Asia, and the Talas Central Cattle Mart handles livestock from across the valley.
    • Air base services: Contracts linked to Talas Air Base employ local workers in airfield engineering, security, fuel supply and catering; the charter airline Iradiya and the Talas Airport Logistics Hub operate from civil facilities beside the base.
    • Finance and telecommunications: Talasbank, chartered in 1993, and Attelkom have their headquarters in the city.
    • Horse breeding: The State Stud Farm of Talas holds auctions of Akyel and Chongtor horses.
    • Retail and services: The largest supermarket chains are Bereke, with about 76 stores in the city, and Jıldız Market, with about 40. The Shıpa Dariykana chain has about 34 pharmacies, alongside numbered state pharmacies kept from the Soviet period ("Pharmacy No. 22", 22-Dariykana). Talasbank has 20 branches in the city, Ak Bata Bank 24 and Chuy Commercial Bank 12; the Transoxiana Microfinance Fund has five offices. About 60 percent of filling stations carry the NurGaz brand. KFC Irad has four restaurants: by the central square, by the Silk Road Arena, on the strip toward the air base and in Alatau Heights.

    Transport

    The Trans-Talas Highway runs east–west through the city, passing just south of the central square, and the Fergana Mountain Transit Highway leads east up the valley and across the Talas Alatau, by the Otmek Tunnel and the Fergana Gateway, to the Toktogul basin and the Lower Naryn District. The Talas branch of Irad Temir Jolu enters the city from the west and runs east–west through its southern districts, where rail yards and industrial land line the tracks; it connects Talas by way of the lowland junction with the Kazakh network, which carries most of the city's freight. The railway station, the terminus of the branch, lies about 1.4 km south of the central square. The Sary-Bulak spur leaves the branch on the western side of the city, just east of Semiz, runs south to near the mouth of the Kara-Suu Canyon and west along the foothills, climbs the gorge of the Urmaral and turns east up the upper valley to the antimony works at Sary-Bulak. Civil charter and cargo flights use aprons beside Talas Air Base.[20]

    Culture and education

    Talas is home to Talas State University, whose press is a leading publisher of Iradi scholarship, the National Conservatory of Folk Music and the Talas Symphony Orchestra, founded in 1965.[21] The Irad State Hippodrome, with 25,000 seats, hosts the national finals of ulak tartysh on Irad Day. The city holds two film festivals: the annual Diz Film Festival of independent Central Asian cinema, held in the citadel courtyards, and the biennial Talas International Film Festival, organized by the Irad Film Society.[22]

    Sport

    Football and the mounted game of ulak tartysh are the main spectator sports. Tulpar Talas, founded in the Soviet period as Dinamo Talas, plays in the Irad League at the Silk Road Arena, which opened in 1956 as the Dinamo Stadium and was rebuilt in 2014; it is also the home ground of the national team. Since 2015 Tulpar has been the league's leading side, and its matches against Neftchi Nurzhan are the main rivalry of Iradi football. Universitet Talas, the club of Talas State University, and Tulpar's women's team have dominated the Women's League since 2009.[23]

    The Irad State Hippodrome, about 3.7 kilometers northeast of the central square, seats about 25,000 and is the headquarters of the National Equestrian Sports Directorate. It holds flat racing twice a week, league Saturdays of ulak tartysh under floodlights, and each March the final of the President's Cup in the week of Irad Day.[24]

    Twin towns – sister cities

    Talas's sister-city ties date from the territorial period, when they were arranged through Sister Cities International; because Irad is not a recognized state, the city has no formal twinning with any city of a neighboring country.[25]

    • Cheyenne, Wyoming, United States (1994)
    • Lexington, Kentucky, United States (1996), a link through the trade in Akyel horses
    • Kottayam, Kerala, India (2013), a link between the Syro-Iradi and Syro-Malabar churches
