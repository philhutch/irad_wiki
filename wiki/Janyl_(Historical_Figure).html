    Janyl (Historical Figure)

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia
    This article is about the historical question of Janyl. For the saint as the church venerates her, see Janyl, Mother of Irad.

    The historicity of Janyl, the founding figure venerated by the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church as Janyl, Mother of Irad, is a long-running question in Iradi and Central Asian historiography. Church tradition places her in the eleventh century, or in some chronicles the twelfth, under the Qara Khitai, as a Christian noblewoman who united the Christian Turkic clans. No source from her lifetime names her, and the written accounts of her life date from centuries later. Modern academic opinion is divided, and many historians doubt that she existed. Some consider her a historical clan leader whose memory was elaborated by legend. Others regard her as a composite or wholly legendary figure.[1][2]

    Historical setting

    In the eleventh and twelfth centuries, East Syriac Christianity spread among Turkic groups of Central Asia, including the Keraites and Naimans. Under the Qara Khitai, who conquered the region in the 1130s, Christian communities in the Chu and Talas basins and their foothills enjoyed a degree of tolerance.[3] Archaeology confirms the presence of organized Christian communities in this period. At the Ruins of Old Iyık Janyl (Shar-i-Kuhna), the foundations of the settlement date from the eleventh century. Hundreds of gravestones inscribed in Syriac and Old Turkic date from about 1240 to 1338.[2][4]

    Sources

    Church tradition

    The earliest surviving vita of Janyl is an eighteenth-century text compiled at Iyık Janyl. It was probably written after the foundation of the Church of St. Janyl in 1727 and the library in 1732, and copies are held in the Cathedral Seminary Library of Iyık Janyl. It draws on oral tradition and on liturgical hymns whose age is uncertain. The vita gives the clans' Kerait descent, the White Rider vision and the founding of the First Church, but few verifiable details such as names of rulers or datable events.[1]

    The epic tradition

    The heroic epic known as The Epic of Janyl, or Janyl Baatyr ("Janyl the Valiant"), is sung by bards (ırchy) to the komuz. It presents Janyl as a warrior who fights mountain ambushes, shoots from the saddle and negotiates for pastures with foreign governors. Folklorists recorded versions of it from the late nineteenth century onward, and Soviet collectors gathered more in the 1930s–1950s. The epic contains little Christian content. Folklorists note close parallels with Janyl Myrza, a Kyrgyz epic about a warrior maiden, and with other Turkic heroine epics.[5]

    External sources

    No Chinese, Persian, Arabic or Armenian source names Janyl. The Chronicon Ecclesiasticum of the thirteenth-century Syriac historian Gregory Bar Hebraeus contains the earliest recorded mention of the name Irad in connection with the region's Christians, and it records the conversion of Christian Turkic peoples such as the Keraites. Some Iradi historians have connected his accounts to the Janyl tradition. Critics point out that he does not mention her.[1][2]

    Archaeology

    No archaeological find has been securely connected to Janyl. In the 1980s a silver ring was found at Shar-i-Kuhna bearing a short Syriac inscription. Some Iradi archaeologists have read it as a female form of the name Yohannan (John) and associated it with Janyl's Latin name, Ioanna. Most specialists consider both the reading and the attribution unproven. The ring dates from the thirteenth century, a century or more after the traditional dates. None of the dated gravestones from the site mentions her, and the First Church has not been located.[4]

    Scholarly positions

    • Historical. Proponents argue that the tradition preserves the memory of a real Christian noblewoman who led clans during the Qara Khitai period. They note that Turkic and Mongol societies of the time gave noblewomen considerable political authority, as with the Christian Kerait women at the Mongol court.
    • Composite. Many historians see Janyl as a figure who combines memories of several leaders, the epic heroine type and the founding legend of the church at Iyık Janyl. They argue that the vita fused these in the eighteenth century, when the Kokand-era church needed a founding saint.
    • Legendary. Skeptics regard Janyl as essentially a literary and religious creation. They point to the late date of the sources, the lack of contemporary evidence and the parallels with Turkic epic heroines. Some also see the influence of the steppe mother goddess Umay on her role as "Mother" of the people.[2][5]
    • Ethnographic (migration). Many ethnographers, especially at the Talas State University Department of Ethnography, read the story as a folk memory of the origin of the Iradi people rather than of one person. In this view, Iranian-speaking East Syriac Christians of the towns along the Jaxartes, such as Shash, Isfijab and Otrar, were pushed out as those towns became Muslim under the Kara-Khanids in the tenth and eleventh centuries. They moved north and east into the mountains of the Talas Alatau and the Chatkal, where they mingled with Turkic highland tribes. Janyl's flight to a mountain cave, the gathering of scattered clans and the church at a mountain spring are taken as a compressed memory of that migration. Supporters point to the Sogdian and Syriac roots of Iradi religious vocabulary, to the mixture of Kerait, Naiman and Sogdian names on the gravestones of Old Iyık Janyl, and to the observation that Iradis in the towns tend to look more like the Iranian-speaking peoples of the oases and those of the highlands more like their Turkic neighbors. Critics reply that the vita itself makes Janyl a Kerait chieftain's daughter, not a townswoman, and that the migration is inferred rather than documented; historians who derive the Iradis from Christianized Keraites or from Naimans pushed west by the Mongols read the legend as a memory of those Turkic peoples instead (see Iradis).[6]

    Historiography and politics

    Soviet scholarship treated Janyl as a figure of feudal and clerical legend. It studied the epic as folklore and played down its Christian elements. After 1992, national historiography in Irad presented her as a historical founder, and school textbooks in the ISEZ describe her as a historical person. Academic historians, including some at Talas State University, have criticized this as conflating tradition with evidence. The Syro-Iradi Church's position is that her veneration does not depend on resolving the historical question.[2]
